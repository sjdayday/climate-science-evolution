## 1. The question, made tractable

The project brief asks whether the IPCC *formalized* a consensus that had already formed in the literature, or *asserted* one prematurely — and in which areas. Those are two different relations between the assessment and the literature, and a third is possible: the assessment can *lag* the literature. For each problem and each assessment cycle the question is therefore: at the time the SPM sentence was written, had the specialist literature already adopted the result as a premise (criterion B), had it not yet, or had it moved past the assessment's position? The evidence for "adopted as a premise" is now in `04-acceptance-checks.md` and `05-citation-traces.md`; the assessment wording, cycle by cycle, is in `06-assessment-language.md`. This file lines the two up.

## 2. What the assembled evidence already shows

### 2.1 The assessment as the thing one cites

The clearest mechanism of formalization found in this pass is bibliographic. By 1995 the working literature was citing the IPCC for results three to five years old: Mitchell et al. 1995 cite IPCC 1990/1992 for "models predict more warming than observed" and IPCC 1994 for the magnitude of sulphate forcing, rather than Charlson et al. 1992 [P]; Santer et al. 1996 cite the SAR chapter and a *Scientific American* article for the same result [P] (`04` §5a). Later, Hausfather et al. 2020 and the CMIP6 model-description papers cite the assessment's ranges as the reference. Once a result is in an assessment, the assessment becomes its canonical citation and the origin paper's curve falls (`05` §3, shape ii). This is formalization in the literal sense: the IPCC turns a result into the reference form in which it is subsequently used. It is also why citation counts under-measure adoption of anything the IPCC has absorbed.

### 2.2 Where the assessments lagged the literature

`06-assessment-language.md` documents, with the SPM wording [P], several cases in which the assessment was behind the specialist literature's own premise:

- **Detection.** FAR 1990: "The unequivocal detection of the enhanced greenhouse effect from observations is not likely for a decade or more" [P] — written two years after Hansen et al. 1988 had claimed detection at the 99 per cent level [M: Hansen's testimony/paper wording to be checked], and five years before the SAR's "discernible human influence". The FAR was more conservative than GISS, and the SAR's 1995 statement came *after* Mitchell 1995 and the MPI/Hadley fingerprint work had been accepted for publication (P07). Verdict for this case: **formalized, with a lag**.
- **Ice-sheet dynamics.** AR4 2007 excluded "future rapid dynamical changes in ice flow" from its sea-level range "because a basis in published literature is lacking" [P], one year after Rahmstorf 2007 and Hansen 2007 and while Pfeffer 2008 was in preparation — the P12 case of the IPCC *understating* a risk for want of a model.
- **Sensitivity.** AR5 2013 gave no best estimate "because of a lack of agreement on values across assessed lines of evidence" [P] — deferring to the energy-budget estimates (Otto 2013) that the pattern-effect literature (Armour 2013; Andrews 2015; Gregory & Andrews 2016) was already explaining; AR6 restored the best estimate seven years later (P04). Verdict: **lagged, then formalized**.
- **Cumulative emissions.** The TCRE result of 2009 was operational in the RCP design by 2011 and endorsed by AR5 in 2013 (`05` §4): **formalized four years after premise adoption**.
- **Attribution language.** The progression "balance of evidence" (1995) → "likely" (2001) → "very likely" (2007) → "extremely likely" (2013) → "unequivocal" (2021) [P] tracks the optimal-fingerprint literature (Santer 1996 → Tett 1999 → Stott 2000 → Allen 2003 …) with a lag of roughly one cycle at each step (P07; `05` §4). Verdict: **formalized**.

### 2.3 Where the assessment may have asserted ahead of the literature

Candidates found so far, each needing the primary-source work listed in §4:

- **The TAR's use of the MBH99 reconstruction (2001).** The SPM stated: "New analyses of proxy data for the Northern Hemisphere indicate that the increase in temperature in the 20th century is likely to have been the largest of any century during the past 1,000 years. It is also likely that, in the Northern Hemisphere, the 1990s was the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year"; its Figure 1 caption reads "it is likely that the 1990s have been the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year of the millennium" [P, TAR SPM]. The NRC 2006 review later said that "even less confidence can be placed in the original conclusions by Mann et al. (1999)" on exactly that decade/year claim [P], while endorsing the century-scale conclusion; AR4 2007 retreated to the half-century and dropped the decade/year claim: "Average Northern Hemisphere temperatures during the second half of the 20th century were very likely higher than during any other 50-year period in the last 500 years and likely the highest in at least the past 1,300 years" [P, AR4 SPM]. This is the one case in the record where an assessment's headline claim was pulled back at the next cycle under external review — `06`'s "one correction under pressure". The P09 distinction between the *conclusion* (replicated) and the *emblem* (a choice by the IPCC) is the right frame. Verdict so far: **premature at the level of the decade/year claim; formalized at the level of the century claim**. §4 finds the sentence was the chapter's own; §4.1 finds the choice to make the single reconstruction the report's first figure was the summary's. §4 adds that the decade/year sentence is the chapter's own executive-summary sentence, corroborated in the chapter body by Jones et al. (1998) and Crowley & Lowery (2000): the SPM reproduced the chapter, so the over-reach was the scientists', not the negotiators'.
- **The SAR's "discernible human influence" (1995) and the Chapter 8 affair.** The claim was contested (Seitz 1996) as having been inserted after review — more precisely, Seitz alleged that "more than 15 sections in Chapter 8… were changed or deleted after the scientists charged with examining this question had accepted the supposedly final text" and named three deleted sentences [P: `08` §4]; the three are indeed absent from the printed chapter, whose Summary carries equivalent hedges about natural variability and global-mean attribution [P]. Whether the *science* was ahead of or behind the sentence is answerable from `05`: Santer 1996 was cited 12 times in 1996 and 27 in 1997, and the fingerprint chain was adopted immediately — the literature moved with the SAR, not after it. What the affair was about was procedure, not the result; it belongs to `08`.
- **FAR's sea-level "best estimate" of 66 cm by 2100 (1990)** [P] against the SAR's 49 cm [P] and the TAR's 9–88 cm [P]: a case of the first assessment stating a central value with more confidence than its successors. Verdict: **asserted, then corrected downward** — the direction the manufactured-consensus claim would predict, and worth a paragraph in `08`.

### 2.4 The assessment against the assessment-writers' own literature

A cleaner test than "the literature" is whether the lead authors' own papers had already adopted the premise. Santer was a lead author of SAR Chapter 8 and Santer 1996 appeared in the same year; Mitchell 1995 was co-authored by SAR authors; Solomon (AR4 co-chair) published the irreversibility paper in 2009 that AR5 later formalized; Sherwood, Hausfather and Forster (AR6 authors) wrote the sensitivity and hot-model papers AR6 used. The IPCC's calibrated statements are, in these cases, the same people's published results restated with a probability attached. The manufactured-consensus claim reads this as circularity; the alternative reading is that the people who did the work wrote the summary. Distinguishing the two requires the citation evidence in `05` §4 (was the result adopted by *others* before the assessment?) — for each of the cases above, it was.

**What the citation record adds to the judgment table.** Of the thirty-six cells with an anchoring result (the six engaged cells and the post-AR6 AMOC cell aside), the assessment stated it after the literature had adopted it in twenty-two, with lags from zero years (Zelinka 2020, Sherwood 2020 and Bellouin 2020 in AR6; Mears & Wentz 2005 in AR4; MBH 1998/99 in the TAR) to eight, ten and sixteen years (the FAR on carbon accumulation, internal variability and the radiative basis). In twelve it stated a result whose anchoring paper reached 25 citations only after the approval year — the summary adopted the paper within about a year of its appearance. Those twelve cluster in AR4–AR6 (nine of them), where `05` §2 shows adoption itself had become a matter of one to two years, so a "level" cell in 2007 means what a "+3" cell meant in 1990. No cell in the table has the summary stating a result before its anchoring paper was in print. The three cases the judgment table singles out are confirmed in kind: the FAR's detection statement is the one cell in which the summary *declined* a result the literature had already made a reference (Hansen 1988 reached 25 citations in 1989); AR4's ice-dynamics exclusion is the one in which a paper in print (Rahmstorf 2007, *Science*, December 2006) was set aside for want of a literature, and was adopted by the next cycle with a four-year lag; and the TAR's millennium cell is "formalized (+0)" for the century claim, the decade/year over-reach being a property of the sentence, not of the citation record. The closest the record comes to an assessment ahead of its literature is the SAR's attribution cell: "discernible human influence" was approved in November 1995 with Mitchell et al. 1995 three months in print and Santer et al. 1996 and Hegerl et al. 1996 in press — none of the three had 25 citations until 1996–98. That is the arithmetic behind the Chapter 8 affair (`08` §4): the sentence was level with the papers, and the papers were then adopted on the schedule of their era (Mitchell 1995: 25 by 1996, 100 by 1997).

For the manufactured-consensus claim the table gives a precise form to the question in §1. An assessment that asserted ahead of the literature would show cells in which the summary precedes the paper record; there are none. One in which the summary ran *behind* a literature it declined to use shows twice (FAR detection; AR4 ice dynamics), both in the direction of caution. The lag between a result's adoption and its appearance in a summary has shortened from a decade in 1990 to a year or less since 2007, which is as much a fact about the literature's own speed as about the IPCC.

## 4. The SPM against the underlying chapters

The sharpest test of "assertion" is internal to each report: did the Summary for Policymakers state more than the chapter it summarizes? The chapters are written by the scientists, reviewed in the open, and carry their own calibrated executive summaries; the SPM is negotiated line by line with governments. If consensus were being manufactured at the political stage, the SPM should systematically outrun its chapters. **Result.** In twenty-four comparisons across six cycles and five questions, the SPM's calibrated statement matched its chapter's own summary in twenty; in two (TAR projections, AR4 millennium) the SPM dropped a chapter caveat without changing the likelihood; in one (AR5 millennium) the SPM was *weaker* than the chapter; and in one (FAR sea level) the SPM carried a 2100 best estimate that the chapter's authors had confined to a figure and had not stated in their own summary. No case was found in which an SPM raised a chapter's likelihood or confidence, and the one "premature" statement identified in §2.3 — the TAR's decade/year millennium claim — turns out to have been the chapter's own executive-summary sentence, corroborated in its body by two further studies. On this test the institutional form of the manufactured-consensus claim (`08` §1, item 1) has no support in the physical-science reports: whatever assertion there was happened where the scientists wrote, not where the governments approved.

Three limits of the test. It covers the calibrated headline statements only; the choice of which findings to headline — the prominence of the MBH99 figure in the TAR SPM being the case in point — is a separate test, made in §4.1. It compares the SPM with the *published* chapter, so it cannot see changes made to a chapter between government review and publication — the substance of the 1996 Chapter 8 allegation, addressed in `08`; for the Synthesis summaries of 2007 and 2014 the drafts and government comments are now public, and §4.4 makes that comparison. And it covers Working Group I; the impacts and mitigation reports are tested in §4.2, for four questions each.

**What the prominence record shows.**

1. *Attribution climbed from last to first.* In 1990 it was the final block of the Executive Summary and the eighth of eleven sections, with no model-comparison figure; in 1995 and 2001 the fourth statement; in 2007 the fourth of five sections; in 2013 a sub-section, though by then the report's first sentence was that warming "is unequivocal"; in 2021 the first headline. The chapters' own executive summaries led with the attribution result from the SAR onward (§4), so the summaries' promotion of it trailed the chapters rather than running ahead — the prominence counterpart of the wording result in §2.2: formalized, with a lag.

2. *The sensitivity range was demoted before it was promoted.* The FAR SPM gave it a full paragraph, including the scientists' reluctance to name a best estimate; the SAR SPM did not state the range at all; the TAR SPM confined it to a footnote; AR4 restored it to a bullet; AR5 kept the range in the body but put the withdrawal of the best estimate — the one retreat in the sequence — in a footnote; AR6 gave the restored best estimate a numbered statement. The component of the manufactured-consensus claim that juxtaposes an unchanged range with rising attribution confidence (`08` §3, item 2) therefore juxtaposes two things the summaries themselves did not put side by side between 1995 and 2007: a reader of the TAR SPM's main text would not have found the range at all. The one asymmetry that favours the claim is AR5's footnote — a retreat given less prominence than the AR4 assertion it retreated from, or the AR6 restoration. The retreat was nonetheless the field's own doing (energy-budget estimates by mainstream authors; `08` §2.3), not a concession extracted by critics.

3. *The millennium is the case the claim is right about, and the sequence is not monotonic.* The TAR SPM took one of nearly forty figures from chapter 2 — the single Mann et al. (1999) reconstruction, rather than the chapter's own three-reconstruction comparison in the adjacent Figure 2.21 — and made it the first figure of the report, under the first headline, with a caption restating the decade/year claim. That is a choice of emphasis made at the summary stage: the chapter's executive summary carried the sentence (§4), but the chapter did not single out the one reconstruction. The next two assessments reversed the choice completely. AR4 and AR5 carried no millennium figure at all, although their chapters' multi-reconstruction figures (6.10; 5.7 and 5.8) were by then the stronger evidence, and AR5 reduced the topic to one bullet at medium confidence. AR6 returned the result to the first figure, now as a multi-method reconstruction over 2000 years with the uncertainty band drawn. A summary process that selected for the most alarming available emblem would not have dropped the figure in 2007 and 2013, after the result had been replicated (P09; `04` §1). The record is better described as one over-promotion in 2001, over-corrected in 2007–13, and restored with its uncertainty in 2021.

4. *Sea level has always had a figure*, and the FAR's two are the one case in which figures said more than the chapter's summary (§4). From AR4 onward the summaries' treatment of the ice-sheet contribution has been more hedged than the risk literature (`06` §4), which runs against the claim.

Taken with §4, the two tests give the same answer for Working Group I: the summaries did not raise their chapters' likelihoods, and their choices of emphasis ran in both directions — the TAR's millennium figure the one clear over-promotion, AR5's sensitivity footnote the one clear under-stated retreat. Neither amounts to a pattern; both are the kind of choice the claim's institutional form needs to be systematic, and is not.

For the question this file asks the finding is specific. Where the plenary changed a draft's *scientific* statements, it did not raise a likelihood or a confidence level; the one strengthening found ("a few" → "Several modelling studies") was not requested in writing by any government, and the changes governments obtained on the science were in the direction of narrower claims, explicit scenario conditions, and the working groups' approved wording. Where the plenary *added*, it added policy-relevant mitigation arithmetic already approved in a working-group summary. The manufactured-consensus claim's institutional form predicts a political stage that raises the science's confidence; in the two cases where the record is open it lowered or qualified it, subtracted a figure and a clause about risks at low warming, and added the mitigation numbers that governments, not authors, wanted in front of policymakers.

### 4.5 Draft against adopted text: the longer Synthesis Reports, and the stage before the plenary

§4.4 tested the two Synthesis summaries at the plenary. The same posted record allows two further tests, read 2026-10-01 [P]. The first is the stage *before* the plenary: the AR4 Synthesis SPM as sent for joint expert and government review on 15 May 2007 (21 pp), the 268-page compilation of every comment received on it ("EXPERT and GOVERNMENT COMMENTS – SPM, All Batches, July 27, 2007"), which prints beside each comment the writing team's response, and the 31 August draft the authors produced from it — the one stage at which the authors, not the governments, decided what to change. The second is the *longer* report: the AR4 Introduction and Topics 1–6 of 31 August 2007 (line-numbered) against the report adopted at Valencia, with the 728 comments governments and organizations filed on them ("Comments from Governments and Organizations on the Final Government Draft", batches to 7 November 2007); and the AR5 longer report (final draft, August 2014) against the report adopted at Copenhagen (IPCC-XL/Doc. 21, 2 November 2014), with the 838 government comments filed on its Introduction, Topics 1–4 and Box Article 2. The longer report is not approved line by line: the procedures have the Panel "adopt" it section by section, which in practice means the plenary accepts it as a whole after the authors have revised it for consistency with the approved SPM, and can ask for particular passages to be changed. Each draft was split into sentences and every sentence matched to its nearest sentence in the adopted text by word overlap; the passages whose match fell below a 0.75 Jaccard similarity were read, and the comments on each located by page and line.

**Result.** Three findings are new. First, the stage before the plenary is where most of the text changes: the AR4 authors rewrote two-thirds of the May summary in response to 2,146 comments, and the record of their responses shows the governments' approved working-group summaries operating as the fixed points of the Synthesis — 193 comments, from sceptics and from governments alike, were refused on the stated ground that the words were "approved WGI SPM wording". The one strengthening a government obtained at that stage was the deletion of "even though some benefits of climate change can be identified" from the reasons-for-concern sentence, at Germany's request. Second, the longer reports show the plenary moving text in both directions, and the written record identifies who moved it. Downward: a quantified carbon-cycle feedback (20–220 ppm), "Larger values for sea level rise cannot be excluded", the Arctic benefits, the Asian billion, the two warming limits in Topic 5's avoided-impacts passage (the United States: "a strong claim"), "and/or political" (the United States), the 2050 halving in the robust findings (China), and in 2014 the sentence on where mitigation is cheapest (Saudi Arabia). Upward: the comparison with the TAR's trend (Spain, the European Community, Germany), the extinction numbers (the European Community, which called the authors' August wording a distortion of the key findings), ocean acidification into the robust findings (Belgium, the United Kingdom), and two scientific statements made firmer with no written request at all — "Warming tends to reduce… uptake" became "Warming reduces", beyond WG I's own sentence, and the AR5's acidification impacts went from "consistent with expected" to "attributed to human influence". Refused: Germany's request to delete the cost–benefit "broadly comparable" sentence in 2007, and Saudi Arabia's and Iran's request to delete the 2 °C budget paragraph in 2014, which survived with the word "budget" taken out. Third, the one government whose comments in 2014 form a programme — Saudi Arabia, asking that "negotiated texts should be used from SPMs of WGs", that the budget paragraph go and the WG III SPM's sentence on differentiated contributions come in — got the insertions and one deletion and was refused the rest.

For the question this file asks, §4.4's conclusion stands and is sharpened. The plenaries did not raise a scientific likelihood; where the text changed on the science it was to the working groups' wording or to narrower claims, with the two unrequested firmings as the exceptions that the record cannot attribute to any government. What governments contested, and sometimes won, were statements about impacts, costs, responsibility and policy — the sentences the Synthesis adds to the working groups' findings. The manufactured-consensus claim locates the manufacture in the political stage; in the three cases where the full record is open, the political stage mostly cut.

### 4.6 The IAC's account of the political stage, against §4.4–4.5

The InterAcademy Council's 2010 review of the IPCC's procedures [P: report read 2026-10-01; `08` §5 has the findings and recommendations in full] is the one institutional description of the stage §4.4–4.5 examined, written from a questionnaire to participants rather than from the drafts. Its account (pp. 23–24): "The governments' line-by-line approval of the Summary for Policymakers drew more concerns and suggestions for improvement by respondents to the Committee's questionnaire than any other part of the IPCC assessment process. Although most respondents agreed that government buy-in is important, many were concerned that reinterpretations of the assessment's findings, suggested in the final Plenary, might be politically motivated. However, participating governments may have diverse political agendas that might cancel each other out. Moreover, the Working Group Co-chairs and Lead Authors exercise the authority to reject proposed revisions they believe are not consistent with their underlying Working Group report." It adds that "the individuals with the most endurance or the countries that have large delegations can end up having the most influence on the report", that "the choice of messages and description of topics may be influenced in subtle ways by political considerations", and that the Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency had found the AR4 WG II summary "more focused on the negative impacts of climate change than the underlying report, an approach agreed to by participating governments (PBL, 2010)". Two of its remedies bear directly on the record read above: "to have the Panel adopt the report one section at a time (as is done for the body of the Synthesis Report) rather than approve one line at a time… This could both speed the approval process and lessen the opportunity for political interference", and the formal recommendation that "governments provide written comments prior to the Plenary".

Read against §4.4–4.5, three of the IAC's propositions are borne out and one is qualified. The cancelling-out is visible in the AR4 reasons-for-concern sentence — China and the United States against the United Kingdom, Canada, the European Community and Belgium, the approved text between them — and in the AR5 budget paragraph, where Saudi Arabia's and Iran's deletion was refused while Norway asked for the same figure in the summary. The authors' authority to reject revisions "not consistent with their underlying Working Group report" is the mechanism the 2,146 responses of 2007 document from the other side: the working-group summaries were the fixed points, and 193 refusals say so. The written comments the IAC recommended already existed for the Synthesis Reports in 2007 and 2014 — they are the files §4.4–4.5 read — which is what makes those two reports the only ones for which the political stage can be audited. The qualification is to the IAC's remedy: the longer reports, adopted section by section as it proposed, were also changed at governments' request (the 2050 halving at China's, the where-mitigation-is-cheapest sentence at Saudi Arabia's), so section-by-section adoption reduced the opportunity for intervention without removing it. On the manufactured-consensus claim the IAC's text is double-edged and should be quoted whole: it records that participants themselves feared politically motivated reinterpretation, and it records the two structural reasons the fear is bounded — opposed governments and the authors' veto — both of which the drafts confirm.

The Panel's response is on record ("Decisions taken by the Panel at its 32nd Session with regards to the Recommendations resulting from the Review of the IPCC Processes and Procedures by the InterAcademy Council", Busan, 11–14 October 2010 [P: read 2026-10-01]; the conflict-of-interest decision of the 33rd Session, Abu Dhabi, May 2011 [P]). It "agreed" with the recommendations on review editors ("decided to strengthen its application of procedures, and amend them where necessary, to enable Review Editors to fully exercise their role"), on grey literature, on the full range of views ("handling the full range of scientific views is a core principle of the IPCC. Its procedures clearly require the representation of differing scientific viewpoints") and on the six uncertainty recommendations ("implementing the six recommendations in the IAC Review" through the new guidance note, `06` §cross-cutting 4); it agreed "in principle" to the targeted review-response process and sent it to a task group; it "agreed to work toward establishing a formal body" between sessions and sent the Executive Committee, the Executive Director and the term limits to a governance task group; it "decided to implement a rigorous conflict of interest policy" and adopted one in May 2011. On the one recommendation that touched the plenary itself it declined: "The Panel noted that in its report the IAC has recommended: 'The IPCC should revise its process for the approval of the Summary for Policymakers so that governments provide written comments prior to the Plenary.' The Panel acknowledges the importance of both written comments and inputs from the floor, which are current practice. No revision to the process is required." The written comments were, as §4.4–4.5 found, already there; what the IAC had asked for was that the floor be closed to new issues, and the Panel kept it open.
