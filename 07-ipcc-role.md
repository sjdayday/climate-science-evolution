# The IPCC's role: formalizing consensus, or asserting it

**Status:** scaffold (v0.2), 2026-09-30. Item 4 of the README list. This file sets out the questions, the evidence already assembled in `04`–`06` and the P-files, and the work still to do. Claims are tagged as in `06-assessment-language.md`: **[P]** read from the primary document this pass, **[S]** from a named secondary source, **[M]** from memory and not yet re-verified.

## 1. The question, made tractable

The project brief asks whether the IPCC *formalized* a consensus that had already formed in the literature, or *asserted* one prematurely — and in which areas. Those are two different relations between the assessment and the literature, and a third is possible: the assessment can *lag* the literature. For each problem and each assessment cycle the question is therefore: at the time the SPM sentence was written, had the specialist literature already adopted the result as a premise (criterion B), had it not yet, or had it moved past the assessment's position? The evidence for "adopted as a premise" is now in `04-acceptance-checks.md` and `05-citation-traces.md`; the assessment wording, cycle by cycle, is in `06-assessment-language.md`. This file lines the two up.

## 2. What the assembled evidence already shows

### 2.1 The assessment as the thing one cites

The clearest mechanism of formalization found in this pass is bibliographic. By 1995 the working literature was citing the IPCC for results three to five years old: Mitchell et al. 1995 cite IPCC 1990/1992 for "models predict more warming than observed" and IPCC 1994 for the magnitude of sulphate forcing, rather than Charlson et al. 1992 [P]; Santer et al. 1996 cite the SAR chapter and a *Scientific American* article for the same result [P] (`04` §5a). Later, Hausfather et al. 2020 and the CMIP6 model-description papers cite the assessment's ranges as the reference. Once a result is in an assessment, the assessment becomes its canonical citation and the origin paper's curve falls (`05` §3, shape ii). This is formalization in the literal sense: the IPCC turns a result into the reference form in which it is subsequently used. It is also why citation counts under-measure adoption of anything the IPCC has absorbed.

### 2.2 Where the assessments lagged the literature

`06-assessment-language.md` documents, with the SPM wording [P], several cases in which the assessment was behind the specialist literature's own premise:

- **Detection.** FAR 1990: "The unequivocal detection of the enhanced greenhouse effect from observations is not likely for a decade or more" [P] — written two years after Hansen et al. 1988 had claimed detection at the 99 per cent level [M: Hansen's testimony/paper wording to be checked], and five years before the SAR's "discernible human influence". The FAR was more conservative than GISS, and the SAR's 1995 statement came *after* Mitchell 1995 and the MPI/Hadley fingerprint work had been accepted for publication (P07). Verdict for this case: **formalized, with a lag**.
- **Ice-sheet dynamics.** AR4 2007 excluded "future rapid dynamical changes in ice flow" from its sea-level range "because a basis in published literature is lacking" [P], one year after Rahmstorf 2007 and Hansen 2007 and while Pfeffer 2008 was in preparation — the P12 case of the IPCC *understating* a risk for want of a model.
- **Sensitivity.** AR5 2013 gave no best estimate "because of a lack of agreement on values across assessed lines of evidence" [P] — deferring to the energy-budget estimates (Otto 2013) that the pattern-effect literature (Armour 2013; Andrews 2015; Gregory & Andrews 2016) was already explaining; AR6 restored the best estimate seven years later (P04). Verdict: **lagged, then formalized**.
- **Cumulative emissions.** The TCRE result of 2009 was operational in the RCP design by 2011 and endorsed by AR5 in 2013 (`05` §4): **formalized four years after premise adoption**.
- **Attribution language.** The progression "balance of evidence" (1995) → "likely" (2001) → "very likely" (2007) → "extremely likely" (2013) → "unequivocal" (2021) [P] tracks the optimal-fingerprint literature (Santer 1996 → Tett 1999 → Stott 2000 → Allen 2003 …) with a lag of roughly one cycle at each step (P07; `05` §4). Verdict: **formalized**.

### 2.3 Where the assessment may have asserted ahead of the literature

Candidates found so far, each needing the primary-source work listed in §4:

- **The TAR's use of the MBH99 reconstruction (2001).** The SPM stated: "New analyses of proxy data for the Northern Hemisphere indicate that the increase in temperature in the 20th century is likely to have been the largest of any century during the past 1,000 years. It is also likely that, in the Northern Hemisphere, the 1990s was the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year"; its Figure 1 caption reads "it is likely that the 1990s have been the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year of the millennium" [P, TAR SPM]. The NRC 2006 review later said that "even less confidence can be placed in the original conclusions by Mann et al. (1999)" on exactly that decade/year claim [P], while endorsing the century-scale conclusion; AR4 2007 retreated to the half-century and dropped the decade/year claim: "Average Northern Hemisphere temperatures during the second half of the 20th century were very likely higher than during any other 50-year period in the last 500 years and likely the highest in at least the past 1,300 years" [P, AR4 SPM]. This is the one case in the record where an assessment's headline claim was pulled back at the next cycle under external review — `06`'s "one correction under pressure". The P09 distinction between the *conclusion* (replicated) and the *emblem* (a choice by the IPCC) is the right frame. Verdict so far: **premature at the level of the decade/year claim; formalized at the level of the century claim**.
- **The SAR's "discernible human influence" (1995) and the Chapter 8 affair.** The claim was contested (Seitz 1996) as having been inserted after review [M]. Whether the *science* was ahead of or behind the sentence is answerable from `05`: Santer 1996 was cited 12 times in 1996 and 27 in 1997, and the fingerprint chain was adopted immediately — the literature moved with the SAR, not after it. What the affair was about was procedure, not the result; it belongs to `08`.
- **FAR's sea-level "best estimate" of 66 cm by 2100 (1990)** [P] against the SAR's 49 cm [P] and the TAR's 9–88 cm [P]: a case of the first assessment stating a central value with more confidence than its successors. Verdict: **asserted, then corrected downward** — the direction the manufactured-consensus claim would predict, and worth a paragraph in `08`.

### 2.4 The assessment against the assessment-writers' own literature

A cleaner test than "the literature" is whether the lead authors' own papers had already adopted the premise. Santer was a lead author of SAR Chapter 8 and Santer 1996 appeared in the same year; Mitchell 1995 was co-authored by SAR authors; Solomon (AR4 co-chair) published the irreversibility paper in 2009 that AR5 later formalized; Sherwood, Hausfather and Forster (AR6 authors) wrote the sensitivity and hot-model papers AR6 used. The IPCC's calibrated statements are, in these cases, the same people's published results restated with a probability attached. The manufactured-consensus claim reads this as circularity; the alternative reading is that the people who did the work wrote the summary. Distinguishing the two requires the citation evidence in `05` §4 (was the result adopted by *others* before the assessment?) — for each of the cases above, it was.
