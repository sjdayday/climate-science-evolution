# The manufactured-consensus claim: what it asserts, and what the record can test

**Status:** v0.5, 2026-10-01 (scaffold plus the primary statements and the inquiry findings, §§4–5, with Seitz 1996, the Wegman report and the Muir Russell report now read in the original; prominence results from `07` §4.1 folded into §§2.2 and 3; the claim's best case, internal variability, read in §6). Item 4 of the README list. This file states the claim in the forms its proponents give it, separates the parts that are testable against the literature from the parts that are not, and records what the evidence assembled in `04`–`06` and the P-files already says. Tags **[P]/[S]/[M]** as in `06`. Nothing here is a verdict on motives; the aim is to say which components of the claim the published record supports, contradicts, or cannot reach.

## 1. The claim, in its strongest forms

The assertion that the climate consensus was manufactured rather than earned is made in at least four distinguishable forms, which are often run together:

1. **Institutional.** The IPCC process — government-approved summaries, lead-author selection, the SAR Chapter 8 episode, the "hockey stick" as the TAR's emblem — was designed to produce, and did produce, a predetermined conclusion; the summaries assert more than the chapters support. (Seitz 1996; the Wegman report 2006; Koonin 2021 [M for all three: to be read and cited precisely].)
2. **Sociological.** Funding, peer review and journal editing screened out dissent; the CRU emails of 2009 are offered as the documentary evidence; the "97 per cent" studies measure the screening, not the science. (McIntyre; Curry; Montford 2010 [M].)
3. **Epistemic.** The science itself does not converge — the sensitivity range has not narrowed in forty years; models are tuned to the record they are then said to reproduce; every adjustment to the observational record raises the trend; natural variability is not independently characterized — and the appearance of convergence is produced by assessment language rather than by results. (Lindzen 1990, 2001; Koutsoyiannis; Christy; Lewis & Curry 2018 [M].)
4. **Rhetorical.** "Consensus" is not a scientific category; invoking it substitutes head-counting for evidence.

The fourth is not testable against the literature and is set aside except to note that the P-files do not use head-counts anywhere; acceptance is judged by criteria (A)–(E), which are about what papers *do*. The first three make claims about the record that the material in this folder can test in part.

## 2. Components the record can test, and what it shows so far

### 2.1 "Dissent was suppressed" (sociological)

The citation record is the direct test. `05` §6 traces the principal dissenting papers: Lindzen 1990 (441 citations), Lindzen et al. 2001 (406), Lindzen & Choi 2009 (106), Svensmark & Friis-Christensen 1997 (1,115), Friis-Christensen & Lassen 1991 (889), McIntyre & McKitrick 2005 (179). Each was published in a leading journal (*Bull. AMS*, *Science*, *J. Atmos. Sol.-Terr. Phys.*, *GRL*), each was cited by the mainstream within one to three years — Hansen & Lacis 1990, Held & Soden 2000 and Soden 2005 for Lindzen 1990; Hartmann & Michelsen 2002, Stephens 2005, Bony 2006 and AR4 for the iris; Carslaw 2002, Haigh 2002, Lockwood 1999 for the cosmic-ray papers; Huybers 2005, von Storch & Zorita 2005, NRC 2006 and Wahl & Ammann 2007 for MM05 — and each was answered in print. The curves then fall. This is the pattern of engagement followed by rejection on stated grounds, not of exclusion. The one case in the traced set of two literatures not citing each other is the tropical-troposphere exchange of 2017–20, where McKitrick & Christy 2018 does not cite Santer 2017 (`04` §5e) — and there the non-citation runs from the dissenting side. (The internal-variability exchange adds two non-citations running the other way: Mann 2020 and Laepple 2023 do not cite Kravtsov 2018 — §6.)

What the citation record cannot show is what was *not* published. The CRU-email allegations concern editorial gatekeeping at specific journals (the *Climate Research*/Soon & Baliunas episode of 2003, *Energy and Environment*, and Briffa's editorship of *The Holocene*, the three cases Muir Russell examined [P]); the inquiries that examined those allegations found no evidence of subversion of peer review, while conceding a failure of openness (§5).

### 2.2 "The summaries assert more than the chapters or the literature support" (institutional)

`07-ipcc-role.md` §2 lines the SPM sentences up against the literature's own premise-adoption dates. Of the cases examined, the assessment **lagged** the literature more often than it led it: FAR on detection (behind Hansen 1988), AR4 on ice-sheet dynamics, AR5 on the sensitivity best estimate, AR5 on TCRE (four years after operational adoption). The one clear case of an assessment claim running ahead of the evidence is the TAR's decade/year millennium statement, pulled back after NRC 2006 [P] — a correction the institutional version of the claim would not predict, since the process corrected itself under external review. The FAR's 66 cm sea-level best estimate, revised to 49 cm in the SAR, is a second candidate for "asserted then corrected", in the direction the claim predicts. The SPM-versus-chapter comparison, the sharpest form of this test, is now done (`07` §4): in twenty-four comparisons across the six Working Group I cycles, no SPM raised a chapter's likelihood or confidence; twenty matched their chapters, two dropped a caveat, one was weaker than its chapter, and one (the FAR's 2100 sea-level figure) carried a number the chapter's authors had left in a figure. The prominence test (`07` §4.1 — which results each SPM drew and put first) adds the one choice of emphasis the claim is right about, the TAR's use of the single Mann et al. (1999) reconstruction as the report's first figure in place of the chapter's three-reconstruction comparison; but the same test finds AR4 and AR5 carrying no millennium figure at all after the result had been replicated, the sensitivity range absent from the SAR SPM and confined to a footnote in the TAR's, and attribution climbing from the last section of the 1990 summary to the first headline of 2021 a cycle behind the chapters. The institutional claim, in the form Koonin gives it (§4), does not survive the Working Group I record. The impacts and mitigation summaries (`07` §4.2, fourteen comparisons) add one case of a headline dropping a chapter's condition — AR6 WG III on whether mitigation's benefits exceed its costs, with the condition restored in the sub-statement — and one case of governments removing a figure the chapter's authors had prepared (the AR4 embers), which runs the other way.

### 2.3 "The science does not converge" (epistemic)

Three sub-claims, each with a P-file treatment and now citation evidence:

- *The sensitivity range has not narrowed.* True of the headline range from 1979 to 2013 (P04, `06` §ECS table [P]); false since AR6 (2.5–4 °C likely, 2–5 very likely) [P]. The field's own explanation of the persistence (Roe & Baker 2007) was published in *Science*, and the same literature that explained it also defended the central value (Knutti & Hegerl 2008). The energy-budget episode is the test case the claim should be judged on: a *low* estimate by mainstream authors (Otto 2013; Lewis & Curry 2015, 2018) was taken up by an assessment (AR5 dropped its best estimate) and then explained by new physics (the pattern effect) — `04` §1 confirms that Sherwood 2020 cites Lewis & Curry 2018 and that Lewis 2023 cites Sherwood 2020: one conversation, not two. The skeptical reading ("a convenient rescue") and the mainstream reading ("the process working") are both readings of the same documented sequence; what the record excludes is the claim that the low estimates were ignored.
- *Models are tuned to the record.* The concern was raised inside the field (Kiehl 2007; Hourdin 2017; Mauritsen 2012 — `05` §4 shows Kiehl's early adopters are exactly the tuning literature) and the field's response — AR6's refusal to use the raw CMIP6 ensemble, the "hot model" papers — was triggered by models that ran *too warm* (P03 flashpoint). A consensus manufactured toward alarm would not have produced Hausfather 2022.
- *Adjustments always raise the trend.* The best-documented case, Karl 2015, was tested by Hausfather 2017 against instrumentally homogeneous series and then adopted by every major dataset (`05` §4). The pre-1941 bucket correction (Folland & Parker 1995) *lowers* early-century SSTs and thus raises the trend; the Cowtan & Way coverage correction raises it; the XBT corrections of 2007–09 *lowered* 1970s–80s ocean heat content; the diurnal-drift correction to the UAH satellite series (Mears & Wentz 2005) raised the tropospheric trend after Spencer & Christy had published a cooling. The record contains corrections in both directions; a tabulation by sign, with the paper that found each, is straightforward from P08 and is listed in §4.
- *Natural variability is not independently characterized.* This is the component the P-files themselves rate as the skeptical case's strongest (P13): the bound on internal variability rests on models whose multidecadal variance is not directly verified at regional and centennial scales (Laepple & Huybers 2014; Kravtsov 2018). `04` §5d adds that the three traditions raising the worry — Hasselmann's stochastic models, the hydrological long-memory school, the paleoclimate spectral work — arrived at it independently and do not cite one another. The mainstream answers (fingerprints, energy budget, millennium reconstructions) are collectively strong but indirect. This is the one place where the epistemic form of the claim identifies a genuine open problem rather than a closed one; §6 reads the papers and finds the problem real at regional scale and answered, by four lines of evidence independent of the models' control-run variance, at the global scale where the claim needs it.

### 2.4 What the citation record says about "consensus" as a citation phenomenon

The consensus-measurement papers (Oreskes 2004, 1,807 citations; Cook et al. 2013, 1,365; Cook et al. 2016, 1,470; Anderegg et al. 2010, 823; Doran & Zimmerman 2009, 532; Lynas et al. 2021, 421; Myers et al. 2021, 121; Verheggen et al. 2014, 91) form a citation cluster of their own. OpenAlex classifies 51–74 per cent of their citing works in the social-sciences domain and only 1–3 per cent in the field "Earth and Planetary Sciences"; their most frequent venues are SSRN, *Sustainability*, *Environmental Communication*, *Global Environmental Change* and *Climatic Change* (`data/citer-fields.json`, pulled 2026-09-30). The pivotal physical papers traced in `05` show the reverse: 83–99 per cent of their citers are in the physical-sciences domain (Charlson 1992: 99; Zelinka 2020: 94; Hasselmann 1976: 94; Santer 1996: 88; Sherwood 2020: 87; MBH98: 85), and the contested papers are cited almost entirely within the physical literature too (Lindzen 2001: 96; Svensmark 1997: 92; McIntyre & McKitrick 2005: 76). The two clusters touch only through papers that straddle science and policy — Matthews 2009 and Allen 2009 (36 and 34 per cent social-science citers, mostly economics), Roe & Baker 2007 (41) and Lenton 2008 (21). The P-files did not need the consensus papers: every acceptance claim in this folder rests on premise adoption, assessment language, and the disappearance of alternatives, none of which involve counting scientists. That separation is itself a finding for this strand: the "97 per cent" figure is a fact about abstracts and surveys, and the case that the science converged does not depend on it.

## 3. Where the claim is strongest, on the evidence so far

Set out as fairly as the record allows, the components with the most support are:

1. The TAR's promotion of the MBH99 figure and its decade/year claim (P09; `07` §2.3) — an assessment choice, criticized by the NRC, that made a single reconstruction the public emblem of a century-scale result that did not depend on it. `07` §4 and §4.1 now locate the two parts: the decade/year sentence was the chapter's own executive summary; the choice of the single reconstruction over the chapter's three-reconstruction comparison (Figure 2.21), and its placement as the report's first figure, was made at the summary stage. The choice was reversed in 2007 and 2013, when the summaries carried no millennium figure at all.
2. The persistence of the sensitivity range from 1979 to 2013 while assessments' attribution confidence rose — the two are not in tension scientifically (attribution does not depend on the value of ECS), but the claim's rhetorical force comes from their juxtaposition, and the field did not explain the difference to the public until Roe & Baker. The juxtaposition is the critics', not the summaries': the SAR SPM did not state the range and the TAR SPM confined it to a footnote (`07` §4.1). The one point of prominence that favours this component is AR5's placing of its withdrawn best estimate in a footnote.
3. The dependence of the internal-variability bound on models (P13) — read in §6: conceded by the field at regional scale (Laepple et al. 2023), answered at global scale (Cheung 2017; Parsons 2020; Sippel 2021; the energy budget), and cited by AR6 in the paragraph that sets its attribution confidence.
4. The AR6 decision to constrain projections rather than use the model ensemble — read by the claim's proponents as evidence that the models were never the independent check they were presented as, and by the field as the check working.

And the components the record contradicts most clearly: that dissent was excluded from the journals (it was published, cited and answered — `05` §6); that the assessments ran ahead of the literature (they more often ran behind — `07` §2.2); that convergence was produced by assessment language rather than by independent results (the parallel, non-citing lineages of `04` §5d converged without either the IPCC or each other); that adjustments run one way (P08).

## 4. The primary statements of the claim, read

What follows are the claim's own words, read this pass where the text was reachable ([P]) and otherwise quoted from named secondary reproductions ([S]). The point is to fix what is actually asserted before testing it.

**Seitz 1996 and the Chapter 8 affair.** Frederick Seitz, *Wall Street Journal*, 12 June 1996, "A Major Deception on Global Warming" [P: full text read on 2026-10-01 from the reproduction on sepp.org, Internet Archive capture of 30 April 2010]: "this report is not what it appears to be--it is not the version that was approved by the contributing scientists listed on the title page. In my more than 60 years as a member of the American scientific community, including service as president of both the National Academy of Sciences and the American Physical Society, I have never witnessed a more disturbing corruption of the peer-review process than the events that led to this IPCC report." The charge is procedural and specific: "Nothing in the IPCC Rules permits anyone to change a scientific report after it has been accepted by the panel of scientific contributors and the full IPCC… more than 15 sections in Chapter 8 of the report--the key chapter setting out the scientific evidence for and against a human influence over climate--were changed or deleted after the scientists charged with examining this question had accepted the supposedly final text. Few of these changes were merely cosmetic; nearly all worked to remove hints of the skepticism with which many scientists regard claims that human activities are having a major impact on climate." The op-ed gives three sentences as "included in the approved report but deleted from the supposedly peer-reviewed published version": "None of the studies cited above has shown clear evidence that we can attribute the observed [climate] changes to the specific cause of increases in greenhouse gases"; "No study to date has positively attributed all or part [of the climate change observed to date] to anthropogenic [man-made] causes"; "Any claims of positive detection of significant climate change are likely to remain controversial until uncertainties in the total natural variability of the climate system are reduced." It names Santer ("I am in no position to know who made the major changes in Chapter 8; but the report's lead author, Benjamin D. Santer, must presumably take the major responsibility"), states the effect ("to deceive policy makers and the public into believing that the scientific evidence shows human activities are causing global warming") and the remedy ("it would be best to abandon the entire IPCC process, or at least that part that is concerned with the scientific evidence on climate change"). Checked against the printed chapter [P: SAR full-report PDF, chapter 8 searched 2026-10-01]: none of the three sentences appears in the published text, so the deletions the op-ed alleges are real; but the published chapter's own Summary says that "large uncertainties still apply to current estimates of the magnitude and patterns of natural climate variability, particularly on the decadal- to century-time-scales that are crucial to the detection problem", that the global-mean studies "cannot be considered as compelling evidence of a clear cause-and-effect link between anthropogenic forcing and changes in the Earth's surface temperature", that "It is difficult to achieve attribution of all or part of a climate change to a specific cause or causes using global mean changes only", and that "Our ability to quantify the magnitude of this effect is presently limited by uncertainties in key factors, such as the magnitude of longer-term natural variability" — each of the three deleted sentences has a counterpart in the published Summary, stated of the global-mean studies rather than of all studies. Singer's follow-up letter (*WSJ*, 11 July 1996, reproduced on the same page) quotes the *Nature* editorial of 13 June 1996 conceding "some evidence that the revision process did result in a subtle shift… that… tended to favour arguments that aligned with the report's broad conclusions" and that "phrases that might have been (mis)interpreted as undermining these conclusions have disappeared" [P: as quoted by Singer; the editorial itself not read]. The Global Climate Coalition's pamphlet *The IPCC: Institutionalized Scientific Cleansing* (1996) alleged that Santer "had altered the text, after acceptance by the Working Group, and without approval of the authors, to strike content characterizing the uncertainty" [S: *Wikipedia*, "Global Climate Coalition"]. The same organization's internal 1995 primer had stated that "The scientific basis for the Greenhouse Effect and the potential impact of human emissions of greenhouse gases such as CO2 on climate is well established and cannot be denied" — a section its operating committee had redacted before distribution [S: same].

The replies: Santer and forty co-signatories (*WSJ*, 25 June 1996) stated that "all IPCC procedural rules were followed", that the pre- and post-Madrid versions were "equally cautious in their statements", that "roughly 20% of Chapter 8 is devoted to the discussion of uncertainties", and that both versions reached the same conclusion — "Taken together, these results point towards a human influence on climate" [S: *Wikipedia*, "Benjamin D. Santer"]; the presidents of the AMS and UCAR wrote of "a systematic effort by some individuals to undermine and discredit the scientific process" [S]; Bolin: "I was indeed amazed about Dr Seitz's way of proceeding" [S: *The Ecologist*]. Edwards & Schneider (1997), the contemporaneous study of the episode [P: PDF read]: "Commentators at the Madrid meeting had advised making changes to Chapter 8 for two reasons. First, they urged clarification of the meaning and scientific content of some passages in accordance with the recommendations of reviewers (including some criticisms introduced at the Madrid meeting itself)" (p. 4); "Sir John Houghton, in his capacity as co-chairman of WGI, specifically authorized the changes" (p. 4); "Ideas and concepts were somewhat altered, but basic conclusions by and large remained unchanged — because the vast bulk of those assembled were convinced that the carefully hedged statements the lead authors proposed were, in fact, an accurate reflection of the state of the science" (p. 9); the SPM sentence was "a lowest-common-denominator consensus view of the vast majority of scientists" (p. 12); and, on the IPCC's rules, "While it needs to revise its rules to better protect itself from accusations of political capture, the IPCC must also, at all costs, avoid becoming a science-stifling, inflexible bureaucracy" (p. 7). What this pass adds is the printed chapter itself (`07` §4 and the check above): its concluding section still says that "few would be willing to argue that completely unambiguous attribution of (all or part of) this change has already occurred", that "some scientists maintain that these uncertainties currently preclude any answer", and that the evidence "points towards" rather than establishes a human influence. The three sentences Seitz quoted were removed; the hedges they carried were not. What the record cannot settle is the procedural question — whether post-plenary revision was within the IPCC's rules as they then stood — which Edwards & Schneider answer one way and Seitz the other, and which the IPCC resolved by changing the rules.

**Wegman, Scott & Said 2006**, *Ad Hoc Committee Report on the 'Hockey Stick' Global Climate Reconstruction*, to the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, 14 July 2006 [P: PDF read in full on 2026-10-01; page numbers as printed]. Findings (pp. 48–50): "In general, we found MBH98 and MBH99 to be somewhat obscure and incomplete and the criticisms of MM03/05a/05b to be valid and compelling"; "The 1902-1995 data is not fully appropriate for calibration and leads to a misuse in principal component analysis. However, the reasons for setting 1902-1995 as the calibration point presented in the narrative of MBH98 sounds reasonable, and the error may be easily overlooked by someone not trained in statistical methodology"; "there is no evidence that Dr. Mann or any of the other authors in paleoclimatology studies have had significant interactions with mainstream statisticians"; "we found that at least 43 authors have direct ties to Dr. Mann by virtue of coauthored papers with him. Our findings from this analysis suggest that authors in the area of paleoclimate studies are closely connected and thus 'independent studies' may not be as independent as they might appear on the surface"; "In this case we judge that there was too much reliance on peer review, which was not necessarily independent"; "the paleoclimatology community… has rallied around the MBH98/99 position, and has issued an extensive series of alternative assessments most of which appear to support the conclusions of MBH98/99"; and Finding 7: "Our committee believes that the assessments that the decade of the 1990s was the hottest decade in a millennium and that 1998 was the hottest year in a millennium cannot be supported by the MBH98/99 analysis… The paucity of data in the more remote past makes the hottest-in-a-millennium claims essentially unverifiable." The report confines itself in Finding 10: "we are statisticians/mathematicians who were asked to comment on the correctness of the methodology found in MBH98/99. In this report we have focused on answering this question and not on whether or not the global climate is changing… The instrumented temperature record makes it clear that global temperatures have risen since 1850 CE", adding that rising ocean heat content "implies that the oceans are absorbing tremendous amounts of heat, which is much more alarming because of the coupling of ocean circulation to the atmosphere." Its answers to the House Science Committee's questions (pp. 65–66) are the sharpest statement of the sociological claim and of its limit: "there is no overarching consensus on MBH98/99. As analyzed in our social network, there is a tightly knit group of individuals who passionately believe in their thesis. However, our perception is that this group has a self-reinforcing feedback mechanism and, moreover, the work has been sufficiently politicized that they can hardly reassess their public positions without losing credibility"; "We hasten to repeat that the Earth is getting warmer"; "In a real sense the paleoclimate results of MBH98/99 are essentially irrelevant to the consensus on climate change. The instrumented temperature record since 1850 clearly indicates an increase in temperature. Whether this is unprecedented in the last millennium seems less clear"; and "MBH98/99 has been politicized by the IPCC and other public forums and has generated an unfortunate level of consensus in the public and political sectors and has been accepted to a large extent as truth." Recommendation 1 (p. 51): "authors of policy-related documents like the IPCC report, Climate Change 2001: The Scientific Basis, should not be the same people as those that constructed the academic papers." On the replies to MM05 the report says only, in a footnote (p. 48), that Wahl & Ammann's critiques "are not to the point" because McIntyre and McKitrick "were attempting to draw attention to the discrepancies in MBH98 and MBH99, and not to do paleoclimatic temperature reconstruction" (p. 48). The NRC (2006) committee's chair, Gerald North, said of the two reports that "we do roughly agree with the substance of their findings. There is a small disagreement over exactly how sure we are", while the NRC found the statistical criticisms "had a very minimal effect, not a material effect on the final reconstruction" [S]. The report's subsequent history: Mashey (2010) documented copied text; a derived paper (Said et al. 2008, *Computational Statistics & Data Analysis*) was retracted in 2011 for using "portions of other authors' writings without sufficient attribution"; George Mason University (2012) reprimanded Wegman for the paper and found no misconduct in the report's background sections [S]. For this project the report matters as the fullest statement of the *sociological* form of the claim (a small, interlinked community verifying itself) — the form `04` §5(d) and `05` §4 test with citation data — and, read in full, as a statement of its own scope: its authors concede the warming, call the millennium result "essentially irrelevant to the consensus on climate change", and locate the over-reach in the IPCC's use of the figure (`07` §4.1) rather than in the physics.

**Lindzen 2008**, "Climate Science: Is it currently designed to answer questions?" (arXiv:0809.3762) [P: abstract]: "For a variety of inter-related cultural, organizational, and political reasons, progress in climate science and the actual solution of scientific problems in this field have moved at a much slower rate than would normally be possible"; the essay describes "how political bodies act to control scientific institutions, how scientists adjust both data and even theory to accommodate politically correct positions, and how opposition to these positions is disposed of." This is the *institutional* and *sociological* forms in one statement, from the field's most cited dissenter (`05` §6).

**Curry 2017**, written testimony to the House Committee on Science, Space and Technology, 29 March 2017 [P]: "the climate community has prematurely elevated a scientific hypothesis on human-caused climate change to a ruling theory through claims of a consensus" (p. 1); "Premature theories enforced by an explicit consensus building process harm scientific progress because of the questions that don't get asked" (p. 1); "Motivated by the mandate from the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) to address dangerous human-caused climate change, the climate community has worked for more than 20 years to establish a scientific consensus" (p. 3); "If the IPCC found that climate change was not being affected by human alteration of the atmosphere or that it is not 'dangerous,' the UNFCCC would not need it to exist" (p. 11); "Overconfident assertions take away the motivation for scientists to challenge the consensus, particularly when they can expect to be called a 'denier'" (p. 8); "current global climate models are not fit for the purpose of attributing the causes of recent warming or for predicting global or regional climate change on timescales of decades to centuries, with any high level of confidence" (p. 5). This is the most explicit primary statement of "manufactured consensus" from inside the field, and it makes the claim *institutional* (the UNFCCC mandate), *sociological* (denigration of dissent) and *epistemic* (models unfit for attribution) at once.

**Koonin 2014, 2021** [S: *Wikipedia*, "Steven E. Koonin"]: the 2014 *WSJ* essay "Climate Science Is Not Settled" argued that "the limits of climate measurement data make it hard to untangle the planet's response to human influences", that model results "disagree with or contradict each other", and that "Press releases, summaries, headlines, and news stories often don't accurately reflect the consensus"; *Unsettled* (2021) extends the last point to the assessment reports themselves. That is the precise form of the institutional claim that `07` §4 tests — for Working Group I, the summaries match the chapters.

## 5. What the inquiries found

The CRU e-mails (November 2009) produced eight formal examinations in 2010–11; the InterAcademy Council's review of IPCC procedures, prompted by the AR4 errors, ran in parallel and is included here. Their findings on the specific allegations that bear on this file — data manipulation, subversion of peer review, misuse of the IPCC process, and openness — were:

**What the inquiries settle, and what they do not.** On the sociological claim of §2.1, the inquiries that examined the gatekeeping allegations directly (the Commons committee and Muir Russell §25) found no evidence of subversion of peer review, and none of the eight found data manipulation. Two components of the claim survive the inquiries intact and are conceded by them: the WMO 1999 figure was "misleading" in its presentation, and CRU's handling of data requests showed a "culture of non-disclosure". The IAC, separately, found that the IPCC's own procedures — on grey literature, review editors and uncertainty language — were not consistently applied in AR4, which is the process-level residue of the institutional claim; the IPCC adopted the IAC's governance and conflict-of-interest recommendations in 2011–12 [M]. The claim's proponents reject the inquiries as insufficiently independent or thorough (the Commons committee took evidence for a single session; Oxburgh's panel reported in five weeks on eleven papers; Muir Russell did not examine the science — and says so: it "examines the conduct of the scientists involved", the Oxburgh panel having examined "the science being undertaken at CRU" [P: §5]) [M: the critics' objections themselves are widely made and should be sourced precisely in a later pass]; that objection is to the inquiries' form, and the record of what they examined and found is as stated above. The corrections that raised the trend were, in each case, corrections of a cooling bias in an instrument change (buckets to engine intakes, ships to buoys, orbital decay); those that lowered it were corrections of warming biases (XBTs, urbanization, fleet composition). The pattern is that of instruments being fixed, not of a series being tuned.

## 6. The claim's best case, read: internal variability (P13)

P13 and `03` §10 rate the internal-variability problem as the strongest component of the skeptical position, and §2.3 above agreed on the strength of the earlier evidence. This section reads the principal papers on 2026-10-01 — Laepple & Huybers 2014 in full (PNAS, open), Kravtsov, Grimm & Gu 2018 and Scaife & Smith 2018 in full (*npj Climate and Atmospheric Science*, open), Mann, Steinman & Miller 2020 in full (*Nature Communications*, open), Cheung et al. 2017, Parsons et al. 2020, Laepple et al. 2023 and Sippel et al. 2021 by abstract [P: abstract], and the assessment chapters' own treatment [P] — and states the case at its strongest before saying what the record does with it.

### 6.1 The claim, in the words of its mainstream sources

The argument is that every attribution statement rests on a null distribution of unforced variability, that the null comes from model control runs, and that the models' variability is not verified at the scales that matter. Its best statements are not from the skeptical literature:

- **Laepple & Huybers 2014** [P], from a global network of 33 Holocene marine proxies corrected for proxy noise: "General circulation models, however, simulate SST variability that is systematically smaller than instrumental and proxy-based estimates. Discrepancies in variability are largest at low latitudes and increase with timescale, reaching two orders of magnitude for tropical variability at millennial timescales." The numbers: at low latitudes "the proxy estimates average 45 times greater variance than is associated with model SST variability [95% c.i. 22–92] at multicentennial timescales… more than a factor of 100 (c.i. 118–1,470) at millennial timescales"; at mid to high latitudes "10 (c.i. 6–18) times greater variance" at multicentennial and "49 (c.i. 34–151)" at millennial timescales, while "at decadal timescales… the models have slightly greater variance than the instrumental observations" there. The consequence is drawn explicitly: "Testing whether changes in temperature are attributable to anthropogenic causes requires a null distribution representing natural variability that is typically obtained from control runs of a model. Insomuch as models underestimate natural SST variability, tests for anthropogenic effects will tend to be biased positive. Optimal detection techniques that seek to rotate the fingerprint of climate change so as to maximize signal-to-noise ratios in model simulations are likely even more susceptible to bias." The authors leave the cause open — "major deficiencies in observational estimates or model simulations, or both" — and list candidate model deficiencies (coupling too weak, models too diffusive) alongside under-estimated forcing (volcanic, freshwater, solar).
- **Cheung et al. 2017** [P: abstract], using the CMIP5 ensemble mean as the forced signal and removing it from observations: "the models underestimate ICV amplitude on low-frequency time scales (>20 yr in the NA; >40 yr in the NP), while agreement is found in the NH variability."
- **Kravtsov, Grimm & Gu 2018** [P], the strongest form: a space–time filter applied to the twentieth-century reanalysis minus the scaled CMIP5 forced signal isolates "a pronounced Global Multidecadal Oscillation" originating in the North Atlantic and propagating to the Pacific, Southern Ocean, Antarctica and the Arctic over 25–35 years, "altogether absent from the model-simulated internal secular variability"; "none of the model simulations considered match the observed signal in terms of its magnitude, spatial patterns and their sequential time development." Its size: "The global-mean temperature trends associated with GSW are as large as 0.3 °C per 40 years, and so are capable of doubling, nullifying or even reversing the forced global warming trends on that timescale." The paper's own caveat: the oscillatory character "is impossible to establish due to shortness of the data record", and the scaling of the models' forced signal to observations "minimises, by construction, the differences between models and observations".
- **Laepple et al. 2023** [P: abstract], a review by the same group nine years on: "Locally, simulated temperature variability is consistently smaller on multidecadal and longer timescales than is indicated by proxy-based reconstructions… In contrast, at global scales, studies found agreement between simulated and proxy reconstructed temperature variations." Their conclusion is that the reconstruction deficiencies they identify "are probably insufficient to resolve such discrepancies" and that "regional climate variations persisted for longer timescales than climate models… are able to reproduce", which "would bias climate projections and attribution studies."

The claim, then, has three tiers. Regional variance at multidecadal-to-centennial scales is under-simulated by a large factor (Laepple & Huybers; Cheung; Laepple 2023 — the field's own position by 2023). Global or hemispheric variance may be adequately simulated (Cheung; Laepple 2023) or may contain a missing global mode (Kravtsov). And whichever is true, the assessments' bound on the internal contribution to the century's warming is model-derived.

### 6.2 What the assessments did with it

The problem was not discovered by the critics, and the assessments have recorded both the dependence and the checks against it in every cycle [P]:

- AR4 ch. 9: "Atmosphere-Ocean General Circulation Models may not simulate natural internal climate variability accurately, particularly at small spatial scales, and thus a residual consistency test (Allen and Tett, 1999) is typically used to assess the model-simulated variability at the scales that are retained in the analysis." And, on the TAR's results: "even substantially inflated (doubled or more) estimates of model-simulated internal variance were found unlikely to be large enough to nullify the detection of an anthropogenic influence on climate"; of AR4's own: "Most of the results noted above hold even if the estimate of internal climate variability from the control simulation is doubled."
- AR5 ch. 10, on the same test: "the F-test is relatively weak… so 'passing' this test is not a safeguard against unrealistic variability." The SPM bounded internal variability's contribution to 1951–2010 warming at "likely… −0.1°C to 0.1°C".
- AR6 ch. 3: "A signal can be spuriously detected due to too-small noise, and hence simulated internal variability needs to be evaluated with care." The attribution assessment cites the critics' own evidence: "In deriving our assessments, these considerations are balanced against new literature that raises questions about the ability of some models to simulate variability in surface temperatures over a range of time scales (Laepple and Huybers, 2014; Parsons et al., 2017; Friedman et al., 2020), and the finding that some CMIP6 models exhibit substantially higher multi-decadal internal variability than that seen in CMIP5, which remains to be fully understood (Parsons et al., 2020; Ribes et al., 2021)." It summarizes Laepple & Huybers as finding "underestimates of variance by models by at least a factor of ten at centennial time scales", notes that "the unforced variability of the five most variable models approaches half that observed over the historical period", and concludes "that the statistics of internal variability in models compare well in most cases to observational estimates and temperature proxy reconstructions, though some CMIP6 models appear to have higher multi-decadal variability than CMIP5 models or proxy reconstructions. When used in attribution studies, models with overestimated variability would increase estimated uncertainties and make results statistically conservative." The SPM's bound widened to "internal variability changed it by –0.2°C to +0.2°C" (A.1.3, for 1850–1900 to 2010–2019).

So the dependence the claim points to is stated in the assessments, the standard test for it is described as weak by AR5 itself, the critics' strongest paper is cited by AR6 in the paragraph that sets the attribution confidence, and the bound was widened between AR5 and AR6. Kravtsov et al. 2018 is not cited by AR6 chapter 3.

### 6.3 What the literature did with it

- **Engagement.** Laepple & Huybers 2014 has 192 citations in OpenAlex, its most-cited citers including Caesar & Rahmstorf 2018, Neukom et al. 2019 (*Nature Geoscience*, "Consistent multidecadal variability in global temperature reconstructions and simulations"), Thompson & Barnes 2015, McKinnon & Deser 2018 and Franzke et al. 2020 — the attribution and variability mainstream. Kravtsov et al. 2018 has 81, among them Olonscheck, Rugenstein & Marotzke 2020 (*GRL*: "observed and simulated trends in sea surface temperature (SST) patterns are globally consistent when accounting for internal variability", with the two interpretations stated — unusual realization, or "the models are systematically biased but large internal variability leads to some good matches") and Sippel et al. 2021 (*Science Advances*), which is a direct answer: "estimates of the magnitude of decadal-scale variability from models and observations are uncertain, limiting determination of the fraction of warming attributable to external forcing. Here, we use statistical learning to extract a fingerprint of climate change that is robust to different model representations and magnitudes of internal variability… It is extremely likely that at least 85% is attributable to external forcing based on the median variability across climate models. Detection remains robust even when evaluated against models with high variability and if decadal-scale variability were doubled. This work addresses a long-standing limitation in attributing warming to external forcing." Kravtsov is also cited from the other side (Connolly et al. 2020; Soon et al. 2023). The pattern of `05` §6 — published in a leading venue, cited by the mainstream, answered in print — holds here, with one exception noted below.
- **The global-mode question.** Mann, Steinman & Miller 2020 [P], from 43 CMIP5 control runs: "there is no compelling evidence from control simulations for any robust interdecadal or multidecadal climate oscillations, with the only signals that are distinct from coloured noise found within the interannual ENSO frequency band"; the 40–50-year peak in the observations "appears to reflect the response of the climate system to both anthropogenic and natural forcing rather than any intrinsic internal oscillation"; and the detrended-AMO features Kravtsov's mode shares — "positive peaks near 1940 and 2000 and negative peak near 1980" — are "largely an artifact of the substantial 1950s–1970s aerosol surface cooling trend masquerading as part of an AMO oscillation." The paper adds: "While this does not prove that physically-based interdecadal and multidecadal modes of variability do not exist, it does call into question whether they can be classified as an oscillation." Mann et al. 2021 (*Science*) [P: abstract] extend this to the pre-industrial era, where the apparent multidecadal oscillations in paleoclimate data are "an artifact of pulses of volcanic activity". Mann et al. 2020 cite Cheung et al. 2017 and Kravtsov & Callicutt 2017 but not Kravtsov et al. 2018 [P: reference list] — the one non-citation found in this strand, and it runs from the mainstream side.
- **The method dispute is symmetrical.** Kravtsov's "observed internal variability" is the observed record minus the models' scaled forced response; any error in the models' forced response — the aerosol-driven mid-century cooling above all — therefore appears in it as "internal". That is the same objection Mann et al. make to the detrended AMO, and it is why the two literatures read the same residual as a missing mode (Kravtsov) or as a forcing error (Mann; AR6 ch. 3's "very diverse spatial responses of different climate models to aerosol forcing"). Neither side can settle it from the twentieth-century record alone, which both acknowledge.
- **The paradox cuts the other way.** Scaife & Smith 2018 [P] is sometimes cited for the proposition that models' variability is structurally wrong. It is, but in the direction opposite to the claim: "the total variance in the models is often close in magnitude to the observed variance, and so it is not a simple matter of models containing too much variability. Instead, the proportion of Atlantic climate variance that is predictable in climate models appears to be too weak in amplitude by a factor of two, or perhaps more." Their fifth implication: large-ensemble estimates of internal variability's share of projection uncertainty rely "on the models correctly responding to external forcing… If the signal-to-noise paradox also applies to the response to these forcing factors, then the role of internal variability will be overestimated by this technique." A model whose forced signal is too weak overstates, not understates, the room for natural variability.
- **What CMIP6 added.** Parsons et al. 2020 [P: abstract]: "several CMIP6 simulations show more GMST interdecadal variability than the previous generations… Nonetheless, we find that 100-year trends in CMIP6 piControl simulations never exceed the maximum observed warming trend"; and the unforced variability has a different spatial pattern (high latitudes and the east Pacific) from the observed and forced warming (tropical convective regions) — the point AR6 ch. 3 repeats: "patterns of temperature variability simulated by even the most variable models differ from the pattern of forced temperature change."

### 6.4 Where that leaves the claim

The regional tier is conceded: the field's own 2023 review says regional multidecadal-to-centennial variability is under-simulated, "primarily due to climate model deficiencies", and that this biases regional projections and attribution. P13 and `03` §10 already rate it `CONTESTED-WITHIN`, and that is the right rating. The global tier is where the claim has to make its stand, and there the record is against it in four independent ways that do not run through the models' control-run variance: the global proxy–model agreement (Cheung 2017; Neukom 2019; Laepple 2023), which also means the reconstructions' own global variance contains no excursion like the present one (P09); the mismatch between the spatial pattern of unforced variability and the pattern of the observed warming (Parsons 2020; AR6 ch. 3); the robustness of detection to doubled variability (AR4 ch. 9; Sippel 2021); and the energy budget, since a redistribution cannot warm the whole system while the ocean gains heat (P13). The assessment widened its allowance from ±0.1 to ±0.2 °C between 2013 and 2021 in the presence of this literature rather than in spite of it.

So the best case, read in full, is a real open problem at regional scale that the field found, published, cited into its own assessment and is still working on, attached to a global claim that the same literature answers. What it is not is evidence that the convergence on attribution was produced by assessment language: the papers that raise the difficulty are the field's, the assessment cites them where it sets its confidence, and the one documentary failure of engagement found here (Mann 2020 not citing Kravtsov 2018) runs in the direction the claim does not predict. The claim's residual — that a global multidecadal mode exists in the real system, is absent from every model, and happens to have added to the warming since 1970 — cannot be excluded from the twentieth-century record alone; it is excluded, so far as it is, by the pattern, the paleo and the energy-budget evidence, each of which the claim must also reject.
