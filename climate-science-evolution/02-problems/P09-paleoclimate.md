# P09 — Paleoclimate as test and constraint: ice ages, ice cores, the last millennium

**Active:** 1955–present · **Status:** orbital pacing of ice ages `SETTLED` (~1980); CO₂ as amplifier of glacial cycles `ACCEPTED` (~1990s; mechanism of glacial CO₂ drawdown `CONTESTED-WITHIN`); the 100-kyr mechanism `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; LGM cooling and the resulting sensitivity `ACCEPTED` (~2020); the anomaly of recent warming over the last 2000 years `ACCEPTED` (2006 with caveats; 2013–2019 firmly); the Holocene temperature history `CONTESTED-WITHIN` · **Related:** P04 (sensitivity), P12 (abrupt change), P02 (carbon cycle)

## The problem

The past is the only experiment already run. Paleoclimate has served three functions in this history: (1) to explain the ice ages, which was the original motive for greenhouse theory; (2) to test whether the climate's sensitivity inferred from models is consistent with the size of past changes given their forcings; (3) to establish whether the present warming is unusual, which bears on detection. Each function generated its own controversies, because each requires converting a proxy (an isotope ratio, a tree-ring width, a fossil assemblage) into a temperature with a known uncertainty.

## Why the solution mattered

The ice-age problem, once solved, showed that the climate is *sensitive* — it responds to forcings of a few watts per square metre with changes of several degrees — and it identified CO₂ as part of the amplifier. The Last Glacial Maximum became the strongest independent check on model sensitivity. The last-millennium reconstructions became the most publicly contested result in the field, because a "hockey stick" makes the twentieth century look anomalous in a single picture.

## Key papers and competing answers

### Ice-age pacing (1955–1980)

**Emiliani (1955)**, *Pleistocene temperatures* (J. Geol.), produced oxygen-isotope records from deep-sea cores showing many glacial cycles rather than the four of the textbooks; **Shackleton (1967)** showed the isotope signal was mostly global ice volume rather than temperature, which made it a better ice-age index. **Broecker et al. (1968)** dated Barbados coral terraces and found the timing matched orbital insolation. **Hays, Imbrie & Shackleton (1976)**, *Variations in the Earth's orbit: pacemaker of the ice ages* (Science), performed spectral analysis on two Indian Ocean cores and found power at the orbital periods of ~100, ~41, and ~23/19 kyr. *Competing answers before 1976: orbital forcing (Milankovitch), oceanic mechanisms (Ewing & Donn 1956), volcanic dust, solar variation, and random walk; after 1976, orbital pacing was accepted within a few years* — Imbrie's group formalized it (Berger 1978 for the orbital calculations; Imbrie et al. 1984 for the "orbital theory"). `SETTLED`.

### The 100-kyr problem (1976–present)

The 100-kyr cycle dominates the last ~800 kyr but its orbital forcing (eccentricity) is tiny — the pacemaker paper itself said so. Proposals: nonlinear ice-sheet response with phase-locking (Imbrie et al. 1993; Paillard 1998's threshold model), orbital inclination (Muller & MacDonald 1997 — rejected), obliquity pacing with terminations every second or third cycle (Huybers & Wunsch 2005; Huybers 2011), and ice-sheet hysteresis with CO₂ (Abe-Ouchi et al. 2013, which reproduces the cycles in a coupled ice-sheet–climate model). The mid-Pleistocene transition from 41- to 100-kyr cycles is explained by regolith removal and declining CO₂ (Clark et al. 2006; Chalk et al. 2017; Willeit et al. 2019). `CONTESTED-WITHIN`: the field agrees the cycles are orbitally paced nonlinear responses of ice sheets and carbon cycle; it does not agree on which nonlinearity dominates.

### CO₂ in the glacial cycles (1987–2013)

**Barnola et al. (1987)** and **Jouzel et al. (1987)** published the Vostok CO₂ and temperature records over 160 kyr, showing them varying together (CO₂ from ~180 to ~280 ppm). **Lorius et al. (1990)**, *The ice-core record: climate sensitivity and future greenhouse warming* (Nature), inferred a sensitivity of 3–4 °C. **Petit et al. (1999)** extended Vostok to 420 kyr; EPICA (2004; Lüthi et al. 2008) to 800 kyr.

*The lag.* **Fischer et al. (1999)** found CO₂ lagging Antarctic temperature by 600 ± 400 years at terminations; **Caillon et al. (2003)** 800 ± 200 years at Termination III. This became a standard skeptical argument (temperature drives CO₂, not the reverse). The field's answer, present already in Lorius et al. (1990) and in the assessments, is that orbital forcing initiates deglaciation and CO₂ amplifies it — the lag is expected. **Shakun et al. (2012)**, *Global warming preceded by increasing carbon dioxide concentrations during the last deglaciation* (Nature), showed that *global* temperature lagged CO₂ during the last deglaciation, with Antarctica leading because of the bipolar seesaw; **Parrenin et al. (2013)** revised the ice-core chronology and found CO₂ and Antarctic temperature synchronous within ±200 years. `ACCEPTED`: CO₂ is an amplifier, responsible for roughly a third to a half of glacial–interglacial temperature change. *What drove the glacial CO₂ drawdown* — Southern Ocean stratification and sea ice, iron fertilization (Martin 1990), carbonate compensation — is reviewed in Sigman & Boyle (2000) and Sigman, Hain & Haug (2010) and is `CONTESTED-WITHIN`.

### The Last Glacial Maximum as a sensitivity test (1976–2023)

**CLIMAP (1976, 1981)** reconstructed LGM sea-surface temperatures from foraminiferal assemblages and found the tropics only 1–2 °C cooler. This was a standing problem for high-sensitivity models, which cooled the tropics more, and it was used to argue for low sensitivity. Coral (Guilderson et al. 1994), noble-gas groundwater (Stute et al. 1995), and alkenone evidence showed tropical cooling of 3–5 °C on land and in some ocean regions; **Crowley (2000)** argued CLIMAP's tropical SSTs were too warm; **MARGO (2009)** produced a multi-proxy synthesis with moderate tropical cooling. Sensitivity estimates from the LGM then ranged from ~2.3 °C (Schmittner et al. 2011, criticized for its data and its model) through ~2.5 °C (Hargreaves et al. 2012; Annan & Hargreaves 2013, with a global cooling of 4.0 ± 0.8 °C) to 3.4 °C (Tierney et al. 2020, with data assimilation and a global cooling of 6.1 °C; Osman et al. 2021 obtained ~7 °C by a related method) and 4.8 °C (Hansen et al. 2023, with ~7 °C cooling and a different forcing accounting). **PALAEOSENS (Rohling et al. 2012)** standardized the definitions (which feedbacks are counted as forcing on which timescale) and obtained 2.2–4.8 °C across intervals. *State dependence* — sensitivity higher in warm climates — is indicated by Caballero & Huber (2013), Friedrich et al. (2016), and Zhu, Poulsen & Tierney (2019), and complicates the use of cold intervals. `ACCEPTED`: LGM cooling of ~5–7 °C and a resulting sensitivity consistent with ~3 °C; the higher value depends on the larger cooling, which is `CONTESTED-WITHIN`.

### Deep time (1991–present)

The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (Kennett & Stott 1991; Zachos et al. 2001, 2008) is the analogue of a large carbon release; Zeebe, Ridgwell & Zachos (2016) showed the present release rate exceeds it by an order of magnitude. The early-Eocene "equable climate problem" (Huber & Caballero 2011) — models could not warm the poles enough — was resolved partly by cloud feedbacks that strengthen in warm climates (Zhu et al. 2019). Royer et al. (2004) and Royer (2006) showed CO₂ and temperature coupled through the Phanerozoic; Judd et al. (2024) extended the reconstruction to 485 million years and found an apparent Earth-system sensitivity of ~8 °C, a figure that depends on how slow feedbacks and continental configuration are treated and is debated. Snyder (2016) inferred an Earth-system sensitivity of ~9 °C over the last 2 million years and was rebutted (Schmidt et al. 2017) for conflating forcing and feedback. These are constraints on ESS rather than ECS.

### The last millennium (1965–2021)

**Lamb (1965)** described a "medieval warm epoch" from European documentary and Central England evidence; a schematic based on it appeared in the FAR (1990, Fig. 7.1c) and became the reference point for later argument. **Bradley & Jones (1993)** and **Hughes & Diaz (1994)** (*Was there a "Medieval Warm Period," and if so, where and when?*) found the medieval warmth regionally heterogeneous. **Jones et al. (1998)** and **Mann, Bradley & Hughes (1998)**, *Global-scale temperature patterns and climate forcing over the past six centuries* (Nature), and **MBH (1999)**, extending to AD 1000, produced the multiproxy reconstructions; the TAR (2001) reproduced the MBH99 figure and its statement that the 1990s were likely the warmest decade of the millennium. Crowley (2000) showed a forced model reproduced the reconstruction.

*The critiques.* **Esper, Cook & Schweingruber (2002)** used a tree-ring method (RCS) that preserves more low-frequency variability and found a larger medieval–modern contrast; **Soon & Baliunas (2003)** argued from a survey of regional studies that the medieval period was as warm — a paper whose review process caused editorial resignations at *Climate Research*; **McIntyre & McKitrick (2003, 2005)** identified data errors and, more substantively, that MBH's principal-component step (centering on the calibration period) preferentially selects hockey-stick-shaped series, so that red noise could produce a hockey stick; **von Storch et al. (2004)** showed in a model pseudo-proxy test that regression-based methods underestimate low-frequency variance. *Competing answers: (a) twentieth-century warming anomalous over the millennium, medieval warmth regional (MBH; Jones; Crowley); (b) a global medieval period as warm as or warmer than now (Soon & Baliunas; Loehle 2007; the skeptical literature); (c) the shape is roughly right but the pre-1600 amplitude is underestimated and MBH's method is unsound (Esper; von Storch; McIntyre in part).*

*The resolutions.* **Moberg et al. (2005)** used wavelets to combine low-resolution and tree-ring data and found more variability but a still-anomalous twentieth century. **Wahl & Ammann (2007)** showed that MBH's result survives the correction of the PC convention because the offending series enter regardless when enough PCs are retained; **Huybers (2005)** and von Storch & Zorita (2005) qualified McIntyre & McKitrick's noise argument. The **Wegman report (2006)** to Congress endorsed the statistical critique (and was later found to contain plagiarized passages); the **NRC (2006)** report, *Surface Temperature Reconstructions for the Last 2,000 Years*, concluded that it was "plausible" that the late twentieth century was the warmest in the last thousand years, with high confidence only for the last 400 years, and that MBH's method choices were unfortunate but not decisive. **Mann et al. (2008)** reproduced the result without tree rings and with a different method; **Mann et al. (2009)** mapped the Medieval Climate Anomaly as regionally heterogeneous; Ljungqvist (2010) and Christiansen & Ljungqvist (2012) produced independent reconstructions with larger amplitude and the same conclusion. The **PAGES 2k Consortium (2013)** assembled continental reconstructions; **Neukom et al. (2019)**, *No evidence for globally coherent warm and cold periods over the preindustrial Common Era* (Nature), showed that the medieval warmth and the Little Ice Age were not globally synchronous, whereas 98 per cent of the globe reached its warmest period of the Common Era in the late twentieth century. AR6: "Global surface temperature has increased faster since 1970 than in any other 50-year period over at least the last 2000 years (high confidence)," and 2011–2020 exceeds the most recent multi-century warm period ~6500 years ago (medium confidence).

*The divergence problem* — some high-latitude tree-ring density series stop tracking temperature after ~1960 (Briffa et al. 1998; D'Arrigo et al. 2006) — is what the 1999 "hide the decline" email concerned (the divergent segment was omitted from a WMO cover figure); it is a real limitation of one proxy type, discussed in the literature, and does not affect the instrumental record.

*The Holocene.* **Marcott et al. (2013)** reconstructed 11,300 years and found the present warmer than most of it; **Liu et al. (2014)** noted the "Holocene temperature conundrum" — reconstructions show cooling after ~6000 years ago while models, forced by rising CO₂ and receding ice, show warming; Bova et al. (2021) attributed the reconstructed cooling to seasonal bias in the proxies; Osman et al. (2021) and Kaufman & Broadman (2023) review. `CONTESTED-WITHIN`.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Orbital pacing:** 1976–80. Marker (B): every subsequent Pleistocene paper takes it as the frame; marker (C): Imbrie & Imbrie's *Ice Ages* (1979).
- **CO₂ as amplifier:** 1987–1990. Marker (A): FAR and SAR; marker (B): Lorius et al. 1990's sensitivity inference is cited as a constraint through AR6.
- **LGM cooling ~5–7 °C, sensitivity ~3 °C:** 2012–2020 (PALAEOSENS; Tierney). Marker (A): Sherwood et al. (2020) and AR6 use the LGM as one of three lines of evidence.
- **Recent warming anomalous over the last 2000 years:** 2006 (NRC, with caveats) → 2013–2019 (PAGES 2k) → AR6 (high confidence). Marker (D): the "global Medieval Warm Period warmer than present" position does not appear in the assessment-cited literature after ~2010; marker (B): Neukom et al. (2019) is now the reference.
- **MBH98's specific method:** superseded (marker D); the critique's main statistical point (the PC centering) was conceded in substance by 2007 without changing the conclusion.

## What remains open

The 100-kyr mechanism; the cause of glacial CO₂ drawdown; the size of LGM tropical and global cooling; state dependence of sensitivity; the Holocene conundrum; the amplitude of pre-1600 variability; the long-term (ESS) sensitivity from deep time.

## Flashpoint notes

The hockey stick is the central exhibit of the manufactured-consensus claim, and the record here is unusually rich: a flawed-but-not-decisive method, an adversarial statistical critique that was partly right, a congressional report of doubtful provenance, a National Academy review that endorsed the conclusion at reduced confidence, and a decade of independent replication with different data and methods. The "CO₂ lags temperature" argument is the second exhibit and is answered by the amplifier framework that predates the lag measurements. For the later strand, the distinction to preserve is between the *conclusion* (anomalous twentieth-century warming — replicated) and the *emblem* (the MBH99 figure's prominence in the TAR — a choice by the IPCC that the NRC report implicitly criticized).
