# P05 — Feedbacks: water vapour, lapse rate, surface albedo, clouds

**Active:** 1967–present · **Status:** water vapour `SETTLED` (~2005–09); lapse rate `ACCEPTED`; surface albedo `ACCEPTED`; clouds `CONTESTED-WITHIN` (sign accepted positive ~2013–21; magnitude open) · **Related:** P01 (origin of the water-vapour assumption), P03 (feedback decomposition of models), P04 (ECS is the sum), P08 (tropical amplification as the observable of the lapse-rate feedback), P10 (pattern effect)

## The problem

The direct warming from doubled CO₂ is about 1.2 °C. Everything above that comes from feedbacks: processes by which the initial warming changes the system in ways that alter the radiation balance further. The problem is to identify them, determine their signs and magnitudes, and — the hard part — establish which are known from physics and which depend on sub-grid processes the models cannot resolve. In the standard decomposition (Hansen et al. 1984; Soden & Held 2006; AR6 Chapter 7), the feedbacks are: Planck (the basic negative restoring response, ≈ −3.2 W m⁻² K⁻¹), water vapour (positive, ≈ +1.8), lapse rate (negative, ≈ −0.5; usually combined with water vapour, ≈ +1.3), surface albedo (positive, ≈ +0.35), and clouds (positive, ≈ +0.4, with a likely range that reaches from slightly negative to nearly +1).

## Why the solution mattered

The feedbacks are where the sensitivity uncertainty lives. If the net feedback were zero, ECS would be ~1.2 °C and the problem manageable; if the cloud feedback is at the top of its range, ECS exceeds 4 °C. Every low-sensitivity argument from within the field (Lindzen's) was an argument about a feedback — first water vapour, then clouds — and every step in narrowing the sensitivity has been a step in constraining a feedback. The problem also carries the field's clearest example of a hypothesis (negative water-vapour feedback) that was proposed by a leading scientist, tested by observation over fifteen years, and rejected.

## Key papers and competing answers

### Water vapour (1967–2009): from assumption to measurement

**Manabe & Wetherald (1967)** made fixed relative humidity an assumption, doubling the no-feedback response; they noted the assumption was supported by the observed climatology but not by theory. It remained an assumption in the GCMs of the 1970s–80s (which produced approximately constant relative humidity as an emergent property, but from the same convective schemes).

**Raval & Ramanathan (1989)**, *Observational determination of the greenhouse effect* (Nature), used ERBE satellite data to show that the clear-sky greenhouse effect increases with sea-surface temperature across the present climate, as a positive water-vapour feedback implies. **Rind et al. (1991)** confirmed the model behaviour against satellite humidity.

**Lindzen (1990)**, *Some coolness concerning global warming* (BAMS), argued that deep convection would *dry* the upper troposphere as the climate warmed (more efficient precipitation, more subsidence drying), making the water-vapour feedback negative and ECS below 1 °C. *Competing answer: negative water-vapour feedback.* Sun & Lindzen (1993) developed the argument. This was the most serious internal challenge to the sensitivity estimate and was taken seriously; it generated a decade of observational work.

**Held & Soden (2000)**, *Water vapor feedback and global warming* (Ann. Rev. Energy Environ.), reviewed the evidence and the theory: the feedback is controlled by the upper-tropospheric humidity, which is set by the temperature at which air last saturated; a warming climate raises that temperature. **Soden et al. (2002)** used the Pinatubo cooling as a natural experiment: the observed drying matched the model's water-vapour feedback and could not be reproduced without it. **Soden et al. (2005)**, *The radiative signature of upper tropospheric moistening* (Science), showed from satellite radiances that the upper troposphere had *moistened* over 1982–2004, as fixed relative humidity implies and Lindzen's mechanism forbids. **Dessler, Zhang & Yang (2008)** measured the feedback directly from interannual variability with AIRS data; **Dessler & Sherwood (2009)**, *A matter of humidity* (Science), declared the question settled; Sherwood et al. (2010) explained why relative humidity stays roughly constant.

*Accepted answer:* strongly positive, ≈ +1.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹, with the lapse-rate feedback offsetting about a third of it. Marker (A): AR4 (2007) — "the water vapour feedback is... consistent with observations"; AR5 and AR6 treat the combined water-vapour–lapse-rate feedback as among the best-constrained. Marker (D): the negative-feedback hypothesis disappears from the literature after ~2005; Lindzen's subsequent work concerned clouds and the energy budget.

### Lapse rate

The lapse-rate feedback — tropical warming is amplified aloft because moist convection follows the moist adiabat, which radiates more efficiently to space — was identified in the 1-D models (Cess 1975; Hansen et al. 1984) and is negative. It is tied physically to the water-vapour feedback (both depend on the moist-adiabatic structure), so their sum is more robust than either (Colman 2003; Soden & Held 2006). It is `ACCEPTED`; its observable, the tropical upper-tropospheric "hot spot," is a matter of dispute in the satellite and radiosonde records (P08), but that dispute is about the data and the transient response, not the feedback's existence. In the Arctic the lapse-rate feedback is *positive* (stable boundary layers trap warming near the surface), and **Pithan & Mauritsen (2014)** showed it, not the albedo feedback, is the largest contributor to Arctic amplification in models.

### Surface albedo

The ice–albedo feedback is the oldest (Croll in the nineteenth century; Budyko 1969; Sellers 1969 — P03). It is positive, well understood, and modest globally (≈ +0.35 W m⁻² K⁻¹). **Hall & Qu (2006)** used the seasonal cycle to constrain the snow-albedo feedback across models (the first "emergent constraint"); **Flanner et al. (2011)** and **Pistone et al. (2014)** measured the Northern Hemisphere cryosphere's radiative contribution from satellites, finding it at or above the model range. `ACCEPTED`.

### Clouds (1972–present): the residual problem

**Schneider (1972)** first analysed cloudiness as a climate feedback and showed its sign was indeterminate: low clouds cool (reflect sunlight), high clouds warm (trap infrared), and the net depends on which change. **Ramanathan et al. (1989)**, using ERBE, measured the present-day net cloud effect at about −17 to −20 W m⁻² — clouds cool the planet strongly — so that a small fractional change would be a large feedback.

**Cess et al. (1989, 1990)** ran fourteen atmospheric GCMs through the same SST perturbation and found that cloud feedback accounted for most of a threefold spread in sensitivity. This is the origin of the phrase "clouds are the largest source of uncertainty," which has appeared in every assessment since the FAR.

**Lindzen, Chou & Hou (2001)**, *Does the Earth have an adaptive infrared iris?* (BAMS), proposed that tropical cirrus anvils contract as the sea surface warms, opening a window for infrared to escape — a strong negative feedback. *Competing answer: strong negative high-cloud feedback.* **Hartmann & Michelsen (2002)**, *No evidence for iris*, Lin et al. (2002) and Fu, Baker & Hartmann (2002) found the correlation was an artefact of the analysis and that the radiative effect, if any, had the wrong sign. The hypothesis was `ABANDONED` in its original form; Mauritsen & Stevens (2015) revived a weak version to explain why models underestimate some hydrological changes, without a large sensitivity effect. **Hartmann & Larson (2002)** proposed instead that anvil *temperature* is fixed (the "FAT" hypothesis), which implies a modest positive high-cloud feedback; Bony et al. (2016) refined it. FAT is broadly accepted.

**Bony & Dufresne (2005)**, *Marine boundary layer clouds at the heart of tropical cloud feedback uncertainties in climate models* (GRL), showed that the inter-model spread came mostly from low clouds over the subtropical oceans in regimes of large-scale subsidence — a decisive narrowing of *where* the problem is. **Soden & Held (2006)** and **Webb et al. (2006)** confirmed it for the AR4 ensembles; **Bony et al. (2006)** reviewed the state of feedback knowledge; **Soden et al. (2008)** introduced radiative kernels, which made feedback decomposition routine (marker E).

**Clement, Burgman & Norris (2009)**, *Observational and model evidence for positive low-level cloud feedback* (Science), found from ship and satellite records that north-east Pacific low cloud decreases when the ocean warms. **Dessler (2010)** estimated the short-term cloud feedback from a decade of CERES data as probably positive but with a wide range. **Zelinka, Klein & Hartmann (2012)** built cloud-radiative kernels; the "cloud-controlling factor" approach (Klein et al. 2017 review) relates low-cloud amount to observable predictors (SST, inversion strength, humidity) and uses observed relationships to constrain models. **Sherwood, Bony & Dufresne (2014)** traced model spread to lower-tropospheric convective mixing and argued observations favoured ECS above 3 °C.

**Lindzen & Choi (2009, 2011)** inferred a strongly negative net feedback from the relationship between tropical SST and outgoing radiation in ERBE/CERES data. Trenberth et al. (2010), Murphy (2010), Dessler (2011) and others showed the result depended on the choice of intervals and on ignoring the direction of causality (clouds driving SST rather than the reverse); the method is not used in the assessments. `ABANDONED` in the mainstream literature.

**Tan, Storelvmo & Zelinka (2016)**, *Observational constraints on mixed-phase clouds imply higher climate sensitivity* (Science), showed that models with too much ice in mixed-phase clouds have a spurious negative feedback (ice-to-liquid transition brightens clouds); correcting it raises sensitivity. This is the mechanism behind several CMIP6 hot models (Gettelman et al. 2019 for CESM2; Bodas-Salcedo et al. 2019 for HadGEM3), which **Zelinka et al. (2020)** confirmed: the higher CMIP6 sensitivities came almost entirely from stronger positive cloud feedbacks, especially extratropical low clouds. Bjordal et al. (2020) argued that ECS above 5 °C is plausible on those grounds.

**Ceppi et al. (2017)** reviewed the mechanisms; **Myers et al. (2021)** and **Ceppi & Nowack (2021)** used observed cloud-controlling relationships to constrain the low-cloud feedback and found it positive but smaller than in the hot models, narrowing ECS toward ~3 °C. **Sherwood et al. (2020)** assessed the total cloud feedback at +0.45 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (with a 90 per cent range that just reaches negative); **AR6** at +0.42 (likely −0.10 to +0.94). Schneider et al. (2019) showed in large-eddy simulations that stratocumulus decks can break up abruptly at very high CO₂ (~1200 ppm), a reminder that the feedback need not be linear.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **The forcing–feedback decomposition itself:** 1984–88 (Hansen et al. 1984; Schlesinger 1985; Wetherald & Manabe 1988); universal by the FAR (marker E: kernels since 2008).
- **Positive water-vapour feedback, roughly constant relative humidity:** assumed 1967; observationally established 2002–2009; marker (A) AR4; marker (D) Lindzen's drying hypothesis abandoned.
- **Clouds as the dominant uncertainty:** 1987–1990 (Schlesinger & Mitchell; Cess); every assessment since.
- **Low clouds in subsidence regimes as the specific locus:** 2005–06 (Bony & Dufresne; Webb; Soden & Held); marker (B): the cloud-controlling-factor programme and the WCRP Grand Challenge on Clouds, Circulation and Climate Sensitivity (Bony et al. 2015) are built on it.
- **Net cloud feedback positive:** AR5 (2013) "likely positive"; Sherwood (2020) and AR6 (2021) positive with a small probability of slightly negative. This is acceptance of a *sign*, reached roughly 2009–2021, not of a magnitude.

## What remains open

The magnitude of the low-cloud feedback and its dependence on SST pattern; mixed-phase cloud microphysics and the aerosol–cloud interactions that entangle feedback with forcing (P06); high-cloud altitude and amount; whether cloud feedback is state-dependent (stronger in warmer climates); whether km-scale models change the answer. See `03-open-problems.md` §1.

## Flashpoint notes

Lindzen's sequence (water vapour 1990 → iris 2001 → energy-budget 2009) is the canonical case of a dissenting programme inside the field: each step was published in the mainstream literature, tested there, and rejected there; none was suppressed, and each produced observational work that strengthened the mainstream answer. The skeptical literature presents the same sequence as a lone dissenter ignored. Both descriptions are of the same record and could be checked against the citation trail — a good candidate for the project's later strand. The "97 per cent" consensus studies do not bear on this problem at all; the disagreement here was never about whether humans warm the climate but about how much.
