# P01 — Does added CO₂ change the radiation balance at all?

**Active:** 1896–1967 (with a coda of confirmations to 2015) · **Status:** `SETTLED` · **Related:** P03 (method), P05 (water vapour), P06 (forcing formula)

## The problem

Does adding CO₂ to an atmosphere that already contains water vapour and some CO₂ measurably reduce the outgoing infrared radiation, and if so by how much? Three sub-questions were entangled: (a) whether the CO₂ absorption bands were already *saturated*, so that more CO₂ absorbs no more; (b) whether *water vapour*, which absorbs across much of the same spectrum, made CO₂ redundant; (c) how to translate a change in absorption into a change in surface temperature — the question of *method* that turned out to be the hard one.

## Why the solution mattered

If the answer had been "no" — Ångström's position, held by most meteorologists from 1900 to the 1950s — there was no greenhouse problem, and every later question in this project would be moot. The answer "yes, by about 4 W m⁻² per doubling, giving 1–2 °C before feedbacks" is the foundation on which every subsequent estimate stands. It is also the one element of the science that skeptics inside the field have never contested since 1956; the disputes moved to feedbacks, data and noise.

## Key papers and competing answers

**Arrhenius (1896)**, *On the influence of carbonic acid in the air upon the temperature of the ground* (Phil. Mag.). Using Langley's lunar infrared measurements and a surface energy balance with a crude water-vapour feedback, he calculated that halving CO₂ would cool the Earth by 4–5 °C and doubling would warm it by 5–6 °C, more at high latitudes. His purpose was the ice ages (Chamberlin 1899 supplied a geochemical carbon-cycle mechanism), and he thought human emissions would take millennia to matter. **Arrhenius (1906)** revised the doubling figure downward — to about 1.6 °C without and 2.1 °C with water-vapour feedback in the journal paper, though his popular book of the same year (English edition *Worlds in the Making*, 1908) still said "about 4°". The discrepancy is noted in the secondary literature and is worth checking against the original if the number is ever load-bearing.

**Ångström (1900)**, *Über die Bedeutung des Wasserdampfes und der Kohlensäure bei der Absorption der Erdatmosphäre* (Ann. Phys.). Reporting an experiment by his assistant Koch — infrared transmission through a tube of CO₂ was almost unchanged when the amount was reduced by a third — he argued that the band was saturated and overlapped by water vapour. *Competing answer: added CO₂ is radiatively negligible.* Arrhenius replied (1901), but Ångström's position prevailed for fifty years; it appears in textbooks into the 1950s. The experiment was later understood to be flawed (low spectral resolution, sea-level pressure, and — the decisive point — irrelevant, because what matters is the altitude from which the atmosphere radiates to space, not the transmission of a tube at the surface).

**Hulburt (1931)**, *The temperature of the lower atmosphere of the Earth* (Phys. Rev.). A physicist at the Naval Research Laboratory redid the calculation with a radiative-equilibrium atmosphere and a convective adjustment and got about 4 °C per doubling — anticipating the Manabe–Wetherald method by thirty-six years. The paper had almost no influence; it is an example of a correct answer that the field did not take up because it came from outside the community and did not engage the reigning objection.

**Callendar (1938)**, *The artificial production of carbon dioxide and its influence on temperature* (QJRMS), and **Callendar (1941)**, *Infra-red absorption by carbon dioxide, with special reference to atmospheric radiation* (QJRMS). Callendar estimated ~2 °C per doubling, argued from spectroscopy that the saturation objection failed, and compiled temperature records showing warming since 1890. *Competing answer: the effect is real, ~2 °C, and already visible.* The discussants at the Royal Meteorological Society (Simpson, Brooks, others) doubted the physics, the attribution, and the data. Callendar kept publishing into the 1960s and was cited by Plass, Revelle and Keeling; the term "Callendar effect" was used for a time.

**Plass (1956)**, *The carbon dioxide theory of climatic change* (Tellus), with companion papers in QJRMS and *American Scientist*. Plass, working with new high-resolution spectroscopic data on the 15-μm band and with early digital computers (the work was funded for infrared missile-guidance research), computed absorption layer by layer and showed that the saturation argument fails: the band wings and the upper troposphere, where water vapour is scarce and the band is far from saturated, control emission to space. He estimated 3.6 °C per doubling with no feedbacks and a surface-based method. *Competing answer: the radiative effect is large and the saturation argument was simply wrong.* Plass's paper is generally taken as the moment the physics question was reopened within meteorology.

**Kaplan (1960)**, *The influence of carbon dioxide variations on the atmospheric heat balance* (Tellus). Argued that Plass had neglected clouds, which would cut the effect to perhaps 1.5 °C or less. Plass replied in the same journal. *Competing answer: real but smaller.* This exchange is the origin of the cloud question (P05), which has never closed.

**Möller (1963)**, *On the influence of changes in the CO₂ concentration in air on the radiation balance of the Earth's surface and on the climate* (JGR). Using a surface energy balance with fixed relative humidity, Möller found that the computed response was extremely sensitive to details and could range from negligible to ~10 °C; he concluded the CO₂ effect was "almost arbitrary" and that clouds could cancel it. *Competing answer: indeterminate by this method.* Möller's result was read, correctly, as a failure of method: the surface balance alone cannot determine the surface temperature, because the surface is coupled to the troposphere by convection. It was the direct stimulus for Manabe's approach.

**Manabe & Möller (1961)** and **Manabe & Strickler (1964)** built the radiative–convective equilibrium (RCE) model: compute radiative heating and cooling in every layer, and wherever the lapse rate exceeds a critical value, adjust it (a stand-in for convection). **Manabe & Wetherald (1967)**, *Thermal equilibrium of the atmosphere with a given distribution of relative humidity* (J. Atmos. Sci.), applied it to CO₂: doubling from 300 to 600 ppm warms the surface by **2.36 °C** with relative humidity fixed and **1.33 °C** with absolute humidity fixed; the stratosphere cools. *Answer: the radiative effect is real, the surface warming is 1–2.5 °C depending on water vapour, and the correct method treats the column as a whole.* This paper is now often called the most influential in the field (Forster's 2017 appreciation in *Carbon Brief*; the 2021 Nobel citation).

**Later confirmations (the coda).** Line-by-line radiative codes (e.g., Kiehl & Ramanathan 1983) and the simplified forcing expressions of **Myhre et al. (1998)** — ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C₀), giving 3.7 W m⁻² per doubling, revised slightly by Etminan et al. (2016) — fixed the number. Observational confirmation came from satellite spectra: **Harries et al. (2001)** compared outgoing spectra from 1970 and 1997 and saw the growth of the CO₂ and methane absorption features; **Feldman et al. (2015)** measured the increase in downwelling CO₂ forcing at the surface over 2000–2010 (0.2 W m⁻² per decade) directly.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **That CO₂ absorbs and the atmosphere warms the surface:** settled by 1861 (Tyndall). Never reopened.
- **That the saturation objection fails:** accepted within the specialist community by about 1960. Marker (B): Revelle & Suess (1957) and Keeling (1960) treat Plass's radiative result as the reason to measure CO₂; marker (A): the PSAC report of 1965 states the greenhouse mechanism as established. Marker (D): after 1963 the saturation argument does not appear in the meteorological literature; it survives in non-specialist venues (and in one 2009 paper in a physics journal, Gerlich & Tscheuschner, answered by Halpern et al. 2010).
- **That the surface warming from doubling is 1.3–2.4 °C in a column model, with the fixed-relative-humidity case regarded as more realistic:** accepted by about 1975. Marker (B): Manabe & Wetherald (1975) is explicitly the 1967 model extended to three dimensions and treats the column result as the benchmark; Ramanathan & Coakley (1978, Rev. Geophys.) review RCE models as the standard tool; Schneider (1975) uses MW67 to correct Rasool & Schneider (1971). Marker (A): Charney (1979).
- **The forcing per doubling (~3.7–4 W m⁻²):** operationally universal since Myhre et al. (1998) (marker E: used in every simple climate model and every IPCC report since the TAR).

## Inheritance

- *From:* nineteenth-century radiative physics; the ice-age problem.
- *To:* P03 — the RCE model is the root of the model hierarchy; P05 — the fixed-relative-humidity assumption is the first quantitative statement of the water-vapour feedback and the object of Lindzen's 1990 challenge; P06 — the forcing formula; P07 — the predicted stratospheric cooling became a fingerprint.

## What remains open

Nothing of substance. Residual work concerns spectroscopic line parameters (HITRAN updates), the treatment of the far-infrared water-vapour continuum, and the exact forcing under very high CO₂ (the logarithmic law weakens above ~2000 ppm), none of which bear on the doubling problem.

## Flashpoint notes

The saturation argument ("the effect of CO₂ is already saturated") and the "logarithmic, therefore negligible" argument recur in skeptical writing; both were answered by Plass (1956) and are treated as closed in the literature. The 2009 Gerlich–Tscheuschner paper claiming the greenhouse effect violates thermodynamics is the only instance of the basic mechanism being disputed in a peer-reviewed physics journal in the modern era; it did not generate follow-up research. For the project's later strand, the point of interest is that no part of the manufactured-consensus claim has ever concerned this problem, which suggests the claim is about detection, data and models, not radiation.
