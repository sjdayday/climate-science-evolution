# P11 — The hydrological cycle, circulation, regional change, and extremes

**Active:** 1975–present · **Status:** thermodynamic responses (moistening, intensified extremes, wet-get-wetter over oceans) `ACCEPTED`; dynamical responses (circulation shifts, regional precipitation) `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; extreme-event attribution methodology `ACCEPTED` (2016) with confidence varying by event type; Arctic–midlatitude linkage `OPEN`; tropical cyclone frequency `OPEN` · **Related:** P05 (clouds), P07 (attribution), P10 (pattern effect), P13 (internal variability dominates regional scales)

## The problem

Global-mean temperature is the summary variable, but no one lives in the global mean. The problems here are how warming changes the water cycle (how much more vapour, rain, and evaporation; where), the large-scale circulation (the Hadley cells, jet streams, storm tracks, monsoons, ENSO), and the frequency and intensity of extremes — and, since 2004, whether a specific event can be attributed to human influence. These are the problems on which impacts and adaptation depend, and they are harder than the global mean because the dynamics do not obey a simple energy balance.

## Why the solution mattered

The damages of climate change are mostly hydrological and dynamical: floods, droughts, storms, heatwaves, sea-level extremes. The credibility of climate science with the public rests increasingly on event attribution. And the largest remaining uncertainties in *projections* (as opposed to global sensitivity) are here: Hawkins & Sutton (2009, 2011) showed that for regional precipitation the spread among models and internal variability dominate, and Shepherd (2014) that the dynamical part of the response is where models disagree most.

## Key papers and competing answers

### The thermodynamic core (1975–2006)

**Manabe & Wetherald (1975)** found a ~7 per cent intensification of the hydrological cycle in the first doubled-CO₂ GCM and mid-continental summer drying in a later one (Manabe & Wetherald 1987). **Allen & Ingram (2002)**, *Constraints on future changes in climate and the hydrologic cycle* (Nature), separated two scalings: atmospheric water vapour rises with the Clausius–Clapeyron relation (~7 per cent per °C) because relative humidity is roughly conserved (P05), but global-mean precipitation is constrained by the atmospheric energy budget to rise only ~1–3 per cent per °C (Mitchell, Wilson & Cunnington 1987 had noted the energetic constraint); the *extremes* of precipitation, which depend on moisture convergence, should follow the larger Clausius–Clapeyron rate. **Trenberth et al. (2003)** described the consequent change in the *character* of precipitation (more intense, less frequent); **O'Gorman & Schneider (2009)** gave the physical basis for extreme-precipitation scaling in models; Westra et al. (2013) found observed annual-maximum daily precipitation rising at close to the Clausius–Clapeyron rate. **Held & Soden (2006)**, *Robust responses of the hydrological cycle to global warming* (J. Climate), derived the pattern: because vapour rises faster than precipitation, the tropical circulation must weaken (Vecchi et al. 2006 found the Walker circulation weakening in observations and models), the existing pattern of evaporation-minus-precipitation intensifies ("wet-get-wetter, dry-get-drier"), and the subtropical dry zones expand poleward. `ACCEPTED` for the oceans and the global mean; **Byrne & O'Gorman (2015)**, Greve et al. (2014) and Roderick et al. (2014) showed that "dry-get-drier" does not hold over land, where moisture supply and vegetation intervene.

### Circulation (2005–present)

**Yin (2005)** found a consistent poleward shift of the storm tracks across models; **Fu et al. (2006)**, **Lu, Vecchi & Reichler (2007)** and **Seidel et al. (2008)** found the Hadley cells and tropical belt widening in observations and models, at first faster in observations; Grise et al. (2019) reassessed and attributed much of the observed widening to internal variability and ozone depletion. `ACCEPTED` in sign (poleward expansion), `CONTESTED-WITHIN` in rate and cause.

*Arctic amplification and mid-latitude weather.* **Francis & Vavrus (2012)** proposed that Arctic warming weakens the jet and makes it wavier, prolonging extremes; **Barnes (2013)** and **Screen & Simmonds (2013)** found the evidence for increased waviness not robust; **Cohen et al. (2014)** reviewed the proposed mechanisms; **Cohen et al. (2020)**, *Divergent consensuses on Arctic amplification influence on midlatitude severe winter weather*, documented that observational studies find a link and model studies do not; **Blackport & Screen (2020)** found no significant effect on wave amplitude in either. `OPEN` — one of the clearest cases of a sustained disagreement within the mainstream, with the sides split by method.

*ENSO and the tropical Pacific.* **Collins et al. (2010)** found the models could not agree even on the sign of the mean-state change; **Cai et al. (2014)** found more frequent extreme El Niño events in most models; Cai et al. (2021) review. Observations show the eastern equatorial Pacific *cooling* relative to the west since about 1980, opposite to most models (Seager et al. 2019; Wills et al. 2022) — the same discrepancy that governs the pattern effect (P10). `OPEN`.

*Drought.* Dai (2013) found increasing drought in observations and models; **Sheffield, Wood & Roderick (2012)** found "little change" once the drought index was corrected for its temperature dependence; Trenberth et al. (2014) reconciled the two as a question of index choice and of whether precipitation deficits or evaporative demand are counted. Williams et al. (2020) attributed roughly half of the 2000–2018 south-western North American megadrought's severity to anthropogenic warming. `CONTESTED-WITHIN` on trends; `ACCEPTED` that warming intensifies drought where it occurs.

*Tropical cyclones.* **Emanuel (1987)** derived that potential intensity rises with sea-surface temperature; **Emanuel (2005)** and **Webster et al. (2005)** reported observed increases in power dissipation and in the fraction of category 4–5 storms; **Landsea et al. (2006)** argued the pre-satellite record could not support the trends. **Knutson et al. (2010)**, an expert-team review, concluded: intensity likely to increase, frequency likely to decrease or be unchanged, rainfall to increase; **Knutson et al. (2019, 2020)** updated with medium-to-high confidence in intensification; Kossin (2018, 2020) found slower translation and a global increase in major-storm probability. `ACCEPTED`: intensification and rainfall increase; `OPEN`: frequency.

*Sea ice.* **Stroeve et al. (2007)** found Arctic sea ice declining faster than any CMIP3 model; Stroeve et al. (2012) that CMIP5 was closer; **Notz & Stroeve (2016)** that September ice area follows cumulative CO₂ emissions linearly (about 3 m² per tonne); Notz & SIMIP (2020) that an ice-free September before 2050 is likely under all scenarios. `ACCEPTED`. Antarctic sea ice *increased* slightly until 2014 — unexplained by models (Turner et al. 2013) — then fell precipitously (Parkinson 2019) to record lows in 2023 (Purich & Doddridge 2023). `OPEN`.

### Event attribution (2003–present)

**Allen (2003)**, *Liability for climate change* (Nature), proposed that the probabilistic framework of epidemiology could be applied to weather events; **Stott, Stone & Allen (2004)**, *Human contribution to the European heatwave of 2003* (Nature), estimated that human influence had at least doubled the risk of such a summer (the "fraction of attributable risk"). **Pall et al. (2011)** applied it to the 2000 England floods with thousands of model runs on volunteers' computers; **Min et al. (2011)** detected a human contribution to precipitation extremes globally; **Fischer & Knutti (2015)** estimated that about 75 per cent of moderate hot extremes and 18 per cent of heavy-precipitation extremes were attributable at 0.85 °C of warming. *A methodological dispute:* the 2010 Russian heatwave was found "not anticipated" by Dole et al. (2011) and "mainly attributable" by Rahmstorf & Coumou (2011); **Otto et al. (2012)** showed the two were answering different questions (magnitude vs probability). **Trenberth, Fasullo & Shepherd (2015)** and **Shepherd (2016)** proposed the *storyline* (conditional) approach as an alternative to the probabilistic one, arguing the latter is biased toward finding nothing when models under-represent the dynamics; the two approaches are now used side by side. The **NAS (2016)** report *Attribution of Extreme Weather Events in the Context of Climate Change* endorsed the enterprise and ranked confidence by event type (highest for heat and cold extremes, lower for precipitation, lowest for severe convective storms and wildfire). **World Weather Attribution** (Philip et al. 2020 protocol; Otto 2017; van Oldenborgh et al. 2021) made rapid attribution routine. **Fischer et al. (2021)** showed that record-shattering extremes (the 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome; Philip et al. 2022) become likely under fast warming; **Vautard et al. (2023)** found western European heat extremes rising faster than models simulate because of circulation trends the models miss. `ACCEPTED` as a method; confidence graded by event type.

### Regional projections and the limits of prediction

**Hawkins & Sutton (2009, 2011)** partitioned projection uncertainty into scenario, model and internal-variability components and showed that for regional precipitation over the next few decades, internal variability dominates; **Deser et al. (2012)** made the same point with large ensembles (P13); **Shepherd (2014)** argued that the dynamical component of regional change is fundamentally less constrained than the thermodynamic one, and **Shepherd et al. (2018)** proposed storylines as the honest form of regional projection. Downscaling (CORDEX; Giorgi et al. 2009) adds resolution but inherits the driving model's circulation. `ACCEPTED` as a diagnosis; there is no accepted solution.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Moistening at ~7 per cent per °C, precipitation at 1–3 per cent per °C, extremes at the higher rate:** 2002–2009. Marker (A): AR4; marker (B): universal in the extremes literature; marker (C): Hartmann (2016 ed.).
- **Wet-get-wetter and circulation weakening over the oceans; poleward expansion:** 2006–2008 (Held & Soden; Vecchi; Lu). Marker (B): the "robust responses" paper is the standard starting point for hydrological-cycle papers.
- **Event attribution as a legitimate enterprise:** 2004 → NAS 2016 (marker A).
- **Arctic sea-ice decline is forced and near-linear in cumulative emissions:** 2007–2016.
- **Tropical-cyclone intensification:** 2010 (Knutson) → 2020.

## What remains open

Regional precipitation change; the tropical Pacific mean-state and ENSO response; Arctic–midlatitude linkages; tropical-cyclone frequency; Antarctic sea ice; European heat extremes running ahead of models; attribution of convective storms and wildfire. See `03-open-problems.md` §6–7.

## Flashpoint notes

Extremes are where public claims run furthest ahead of, and behind, the literature at once: attribution statements about heat are strongly supported, those about tornadoes or hurricane *counts* are not, and the skeptical literature exploits the latter to discredit the former. The Sheffield–Dai drought dispute and the Cohen "divergent consensuses" paper are unusually candid statements of mainstream disagreement and are worth citing in the later strand as evidence of what open disagreement in the field looks like when it is not about the anthropogenic signal.
