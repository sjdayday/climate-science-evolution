# P08 — The observational record: surface, satellite, ocean heat, and the "hiatus"

**Active:** 1938–present · **Status:** surface trend `SETTLED` (~2000–2012); satellite–surface consistency `ACCEPTED` (2005–06) with tropical tropospheric amplification `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; ocean heat content and Earth's energy imbalance `ACCEPTED` (2005–2021); the 1998–2012 slowdown `RESOLVED` (2015–17); the 2023–25 surge and possible acceleration `OPEN` · **Related:** P07 (uses the record), P13 (noise), P06 (aerosols), P10 (energy budget)

## The problem

Is the Earth warming, by how much, and where? The problem has been re-posed each time a new instrument or a new decade has produced a discrepancy: land stations vs sea-surface measurements, thermometers vs satellites, the upper ocean vs the surface, and each decade's trend against the previous one. Because the field's *claims* rest on these data, the data have been the most scrutinized in the earth sciences and the primary target of outside challenge.

## Why the solution mattered

Detection (P07) requires a record with known biases; the energy budget (P04, P10) requires ocean heat; the model tests (P03) require the right target. A record that was substantially wrong would undermine the attribution result, which is why the urban-heat-island question, the satellite discrepancy, and the 2015 sea-surface-temperature revision each became public controversies.

## Key papers and competing answers

### The surface record and its critics (1938–2013)

**Callendar (1938, 1961)** and **Mitchell (1961, 1963)** made the first compilations. **Jones et al. (1986)** (Climatic Research Unit, later HadCRUT with the Met Office) and **Hansen & Lebedeff (1987)** (GISS) built the two long-running global series from station and ship data, with different interpolation philosophies; NOAA's series (Smith & Reynolds; later Vose et al. 2012) and the Japan Meteorological Agency's followed. The series agree on the trend to within about 10 per cent.

*Urban heat islands.* The objection that station warming reflects urbanization was tested repeatedly: **Karl, Diaz & Kukla (1988)** quantified and removed the US urban effect; **Jones et al. (1990)** found it small globally (a subset of the Chinese stations was later shown to have poor metadata — Keenan's 2007 allegation, addressed in Jones et al. 2008 — without changing the result); **Peterson (2003)** found no residual urban signal in the US; **Parker (2004)**, *Large-scale warming is not urban* (Nature), showed the same trend on windy and calm nights; **Menne, Williams & Palecki (2010)** and **Fall et al. (2011)** — the latter with Watts, who had organized the surfacestations.org survey of poorly sited stations — found that siting affects maximum and minimum temperatures in opposite directions and the mean trend hardly at all; **McKitrick & Michaels (2004, 2007)** argued that trends correlate with economic activity, and Schmidt (2009) showed the correlation is expected from spatial autocorrelation. The **Berkeley Earth** project (Rohde et al. 2013; Wickham et al. 2013), funded partly by skeptical donors and led by a physicist (Muller) who had criticized the existing groups, rebuilt the record from raw data with new methods and reproduced it. *Competing answer (record substantially contaminated): `ABANDONED` after ~2012.*

*Homogenization.* Adjustments for station moves, instrument changes and time-of-observation changes are made by pairwise comparison (Menne & Williams 2009); the benchmarking of methods (Venema et al. 2012) and the comparison against the US Climate Reference Network (Hausfather et al. 2016) showed the adjustments reduce rather than create trend error. Globally, adjustments *lower* the trend (because sea-surface corrections dominate).

*Sea-surface temperature.* **Folland & Parker (1995)** corrected the pre-1941 bucket measurements; **Kennedy et al. (2011, 2019)** built the HadSST bias models; **Chan et al. (2019)** corrected national-fleet offsets. **Karl et al. (2015)**, *Possible artifacts of data biases in the recent global surface warming hiatus* (Science), introduced ERSSTv4 with a correction for the ship–buoy offset, which raised the post-1998 trend; the paper was subpoenaed by a congressional committee and attacked by a retired NOAA scientist (Bates 2017); **Hausfather et al. (2017)** tested it against instrumentally homogeneous buoy, Argo and satellite series and found the correction right; marker (E): within five years the ship–buoy correction was built into ERSSTv5 (Huang et al. 2017), HadCRUT5 (Morice et al. 2020), GISTEMP (Lenssen et al. 2019) and ERA5 (Hersbach et al. 2020), all of which cite Karl et al. (`05-citation-traces.md`). **Cowtan & Way (2014)** showed that HadCRUT's missing Arctic coverage biased its recent trend low.

*Independent checks.* Reanalyses (ERA5; Hersbach et al. 2020), whose atmospheric analysis is driven largely by satellite and upper-air data rather than by the station record, reproduce the warming; so do ocean heat content, sea level, glacier mass, sea ice, snow cover, phenology and borehole temperatures (Huang et al. 2000). AR4 (2007) called the warming "unequivocal" on these grounds.

### Satellites and the tropical troposphere (1990–present)

**Spencer & Christy (1990)**, *Precise monitoring of global temperature trends from satellites* (Science), derived tropospheric temperatures from the Microwave Sounding Units and found no warming over 1979–88; through the 1990s their record (UAH) showed a slight cooling while the surface warmed. *Competing answers: the surface record is wrong (Spencer, Christy) vs the satellite record is wrong vs both are right and the troposphere really is not amplifying.* The **NRC (2000)** report *Reconciling Observations of Global Temperature Change* judged the discrepancy real but the surface warming not in doubt. A series of corrections followed: orbital decay (Wentz & Schabel 1998), stratospheric contamination of the mid-troposphere channel (Fu et al. 2004), and — the decisive one — the sign error in the diurnal-drift correction found by **Mears & Wentz (2005)**, *The effect of diurnal correction on satellite-derived lower tropospheric temperature* (Science). Radiosonde records were found to have daytime solar-heating biases (Sherwood et al. 2005; Thorne et al. 2005). The **CCSP (2006)** synthesis declared that "the previously reported discrepancy... no longer exists." `ACCEPTED`: the troposphere is warming; the satellite datasets (RSS, UAH, NOAA STAR) now all show it, though their trends differ by up to 50 per cent because of intersatellite calibration choices (Mears & Wentz 2017; Spencer, Christy & Braswell 2017).

*Residual: tropical amplification.* Models predict that the tropical upper troposphere warms 1.5–2 times faster than the surface (the moist-adiabatic lapse-rate feedback, P05). **Santer et al. (2005)** showed models and observations agree on interannual timescales; Douglass et al. (2008) claimed the decadal trends were inconsistent and **Santer et al. (2008)** showed their statistical test was wrong; **Santer et al. (2017)** found the models overestimate the tropospheric warming rate over the satellite era, partly because of forcing errors (the early-2000s volcanoes and solar minimum) and partly for reasons unresolved; **McKitrick & Christy (2018, 2020)** found a pervasive warm bias in the tropical 200–300 hPa layer across CMIP5 and CMIP6 (the 2018 paper does not cite Santer et al. 2017, the mainstream analysis of the same discrepancy — `04-acceptance-checks.md` §5(e)). AR6 assessed with medium confidence that most models overestimate tropical upper-tropospheric warming over 1979–2014, in part because they overestimate tropical sea-surface warming (the eastern-Pacific pattern problem, P10). `CONTESTED-WITHIN`.

### Ocean heat and the energy imbalance (2000–2021)

**Levitus et al. (2000)**, *Warming of the world ocean* (Science), assembled the historical hydrographic data; **Barnett et al. (2001, 2005)** showed the pattern matched greenhouse forcing; **Hansen et al. (2005)**, *Earth's energy imbalance: confirmation and implications* (Science), argued that the measured ocean heat gain confirmed the model's ~0.85 W m⁻² imbalance — "the smoking gun." **Gouretski & Koltermann (2007)** discovered a warm bias in expendable bathythermograph (XBT) data, which had exaggerated 1970s–80s variability; **Domingues et al. (2008)**, Wijffels et al. (2008), Levitus et al. (2009, 2012) and Ishii & Kimoto (2009) produced corrected series; **Lyman et al. (2010)** showed the warming robust across them. The **Argo** array (global from ~2005) removed the sampling problem for the upper 2000 m; **Cheng et al. (2017)** and **Zanna et al. (2019)** extended the record back; **von Schuckmann et al. (2020, 2023)** compiled the Earth heat inventory (about 89 per cent of the excess heat is in the ocean). **Loeb et al. (2021)** showed that CERES satellite radiation and Argo heat uptake agree, and that the imbalance doubled over 2005–2019 (citing von Schuckmann et al. 2016, 2020 and Johnson et al. 2016 for the framing, not Hansen 2005 — the origin paper had been superseded as the anchor); Mauritsen et al. (2025) confirmed the doubling and the Indicators (Forster et al. 2026) report trends of 0.3–0.44 W m⁻² per decade. `ACCEPTED`: the imbalance (~1 W m⁻² now) is the most direct measurement of global warming, and its increase is a first-order open question (P10, `03-open-problems.md`).

### The 1998–2012 slowdown (2009–2017)

The surface trend after the 1998 El Niño was below the 1990s trend and below most model projections. **Easterling & Wehner (2009)** showed such decades are expected; Knight et al. (2009) tested the models' capacity for them; **Meehl et al. (2011)** found deep-ocean heat uptake during model hiatuses; **Kosaka & Xie (2013)**, *Recent global-warming hiatus tied to equatorial Pacific surface cooling* (Nature), reproduced it by prescribing the observed tropical Pacific; **England et al. (2014)** tied it to intensified trade winds; **Fyfe, Gillett & Zwiers (2013)** showed the models' 1998–2012 trends exceeded the observed one in most cases; Santer et al. (2014) and **Schmidt, Shindell & Tsigaridis (2014)** showed that updating the models' forcings (volcanic aerosol, solar minimum, Chinese sulphate) removed much of the discrepancy; Huber & Knutti (2014) partitioned it. **Cowtan & Way (2014)** and **Karl et al. (2015)** revised the observations upward. **Fyfe et al. (2016)** insisted the slowdown was real relative to expectation; **Rahmstorf, Foster & Cahill (2017)** and Risbey et al. (2018) found no statistically significant change in the underlying trend; **Medhaug et al. (2017)**, *Reconciling controversies about the "global warming hiatus"* (Nature), and Hedemann et al. (2017) synthesized: a combination of Pacific decadal variability, small negative forcings, and observational coverage bias, within the range of what the models produce. The record El Niño of 2015–16 and the years after ended the observational question. AR6 treats the episode as temporary and explained. `RESOLVED`.

### The 2023–25 surge (2023–present)

Global temperature jumped ~0.3 °C from 2022 to 2023 and stayed high through 2024, beyond what the developing El Niño explained. **Schmidt (2024)** called it unexplained and "humbling"; **Goessling, Rackow & Jung (2024)**, *Recent global temperature surge intensified by record-low planetary albedo* (Science), attributed a large part to a reduction in low cloud, cause unknown (aerosol decline, feedback, or variability); the ship-sulphur papers (P06) supply a few hundredths of a degree; Raghuraman et al. (2024) and Samset et al. (2024) attribute most of it to ENSO plus steady warming; Rantanen & Laaksonen (2024) find the September 2023 jump very unlikely from internal variability alone; Terhaar et al. (2025) find the sea-surface jump rare but within model spread; **Farago et al. (2026)** reconstruct 2023–24 from ENSO, the Indian Ocean Dipole, Atlantic variability, solar cycle, volcanic aerosol and IMO2020, plus the underlying trend. On the trend itself: **Forster et al. (2025, 2026)** report the human-induced rate at 0.27 °C per decade over 2016–2025 (up from ~0.18); **Hansen et al. (2025)** claim a large acceleration driven by aerosol unmasking; **Beaulieu et al. (2024)** find no statistically detectable acceleration in the raw record; **Foster & Rahmstorf (2026)**, *Global warming has accelerated significantly* (GRL), find it after removing ENSO, volcanic and solar effects — about 0.18 °C per decade over 2000–2012 rising to about 0.30 over 2013–2025 — with the caveat that the adjustment itself is imperfect. `OPEN` (see `03-open-problems.md` §4).

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **The surface warming trend:** by 2000 (NRC) and beyond dispute by 2012 (Berkeley Earth replication). Marker (A): AR4 "unequivocal"; marker (D): the contamination hypothesis leaves the literature.
- **Satellite–surface consistency:** 2005–06 (Mears & Wentz; CCSP). Marker (A).
- **Ocean heat content as the primary metric:** 2005–2012. Marker (B): energy-budget estimates of sensitivity (Otto et al. 2013) treat it as an input; marker (E): the annual Earth heat inventory.
- **The slowdown explained:** 2015–17. Marker (A): AR6; marker (D): the "pause" ceases to appear as a live problem after 2017.

## What remains open

The tropical upper-tropospheric warming rate; the cause of the recent low-cloud decline and whether it is forced; whether the warming rate has accelerated beyond what forcing growth explains; the doubling of the energy imbalance; pre-1950 sea-surface biases and the size of early-twentieth-century warming.

## Flashpoint notes

More public controversy has attached to this problem than to any other: urban heat islands and surfacestations.org (2007–2011), the satellite discrepancy (1990–2005), the CRU emails (2009), the "pause" (2009–2015), and "Karlization" (2015–17). In each case the challenge was published or publicized, the field tested it, and the record survived — sometimes with the challengers as co-authors (Fall et al. 2011) or as the replicators (Berkeley Earth). For the later strand, the pattern worth documenting is that the outside challenges concentrated on the *record* precisely when the *attribution* was hardening (1995–2015), and largely ceased after 2017, when the record's replication by an adversarial team and the end of the slowdown removed the two strongest arguments.
