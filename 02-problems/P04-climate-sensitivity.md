# P04 — What is the equilibrium climate sensitivity?

**Active:** 1896–present; as a defined problem, 1979–present · **Status:** `ACCEPTED` (framework; central value ≈ 3 °C; likely range 2.5–4 °C since 2020–21); `CONTESTED-WITHIN` (the tails; the size of the pattern-effect correction; the meaning of the CMIP6 hot models) · **Related:** P03, P05, P06, P09, P10

## The problem

By how much does global-mean surface temperature rise, once the system has re-equilibrated, for a sustained doubling of atmospheric CO₂? The Charney report (1979) fixed this quantity — *equilibrium climate sensitivity*, ECS — as the field's summary metric. Three definitional wrinkles matter to the history: (1) *equilibrium* vs *effective* sensitivity (the latter estimated from a model run or the observed record before equilibrium, assuming the feedback parameter is constant — an assumption now known to fail; P10); (2) ECS vs *Earth-system sensitivity* (ESS), which includes slow feedbacks from ice sheets and vegetation and is larger; (3) ECS vs the *transient climate response* (TCR), the warming at the moment of doubling under a 1 per cent per year increase, which is smaller and more policy-relevant.

## Why the solution mattered

ECS is the single number that scales every projection. A 2 °C world and a 4.5 °C world under the same emissions differ by roughly a factor of two in the remaining carbon budget for any target, and by more than that in projected damages. It also became, because of its persistence, the emblem of whether the science was progressing: the range 1.5–4.5 °C stood from 1979 to 2020, and the low end of that range is precisely the position occupied by the "lukewarm" scientists (Lindzen, Michaels, Lewis, Curry) whose disagreement with the mainstream is, in this respect, a disagreement *within* the framework.

### The lineages behind the table

**The model lineage (1967–2013).** The GCM estimates cluster at 2–4 °C from the beginning; the spread was traced to clouds by Schlesinger & Mitchell (1987) and Cess et al. (1989, 1990), localized to low clouds by Bony & Dufresne (2005), and has not closed (P05). The IPCC's ranges through AR5 were, in effect, the model range with expert judgment about the tails.

**The low-sensitivity lineage (1979–present).** Newell & Dopplick (1979) argued evaporation would cap surface warming at ~0.25 °C; Idso (1980) inferred tiny sensitivities from "natural experiments" (the seasonal cycle, dust storms). Both were answered by Ramanathan (1981), who showed the surface energy budget cannot be used that way (the same error Möller made — P01). Lindzen (1990) argued the water-vapour feedback was negative; Lindzen, Chou & Hou (2001) proposed that tropical cirrus contract with warming (the "iris"); Lindzen & Choi (2009, 2011) inferred low sensitivity from satellite radiation and sea-surface temperature. Each was tested and rejected (P05). From 2013 the low-sensitivity case moved to a stronger footing: the *observed energy budget* (Gregory et al. 2002; Otto et al. 2013; Lewis & Curry 2015, 2018), which divides observed warming by observed forcing minus ocean heat uptake and gets ECS ~1.5–2 °C. This was not a fringe method — Otto et al. included most of the AR5 lead authors — and it is why AR5 declined to give a best estimate.

**The reconciliation (2013–2020).** The energy-budget estimates were found to be biased low for three reasons, each accepted in turn: (1) the *pattern effect* — the historical pattern of sea-surface warming (eastern Pacific and Southern Ocean lagging) makes the historical feedback more negative than the equilibrium feedback (Armour, Bitz & Roe 2013; Andrews, Gregory & Webb 2015; Gregory & Andrews 2016; Zhou, Zelinka & Klein 2016; Andrews et al. 2018) (P10); (2) the *forcing revisions* — larger methane forcing (Etminan et al. 2016) and the shift to effective radiative forcing (P06); (3) *observational coverage and blending* — comparing like with like (Cowtan et al. 2015; Richardson et al. 2016). With these corrections the historical line of evidence is consistent with ~3 °C but, by itself, still cannot exclude ~2 °C.

**The paleo lineage (1990–2023).** Lorius et al. (1990) from Vostok; the PALAEOSENS project (Rohling et al. 2012) standardized the method; the Last Glacial Maximum became the key interval, with the answer depending on how much the tropics cooled (P09): CLIMAP-era data gave low sensitivity, Tierney et al. (2020) gave 3.4 °C, Hansen et al. (2023) gave 4.8 °C from a larger cooling and a different forcing accounting. Sensitivity appears to be *state-dependent* — higher in warmer climates (Caballero & Huber 2013; Friedrich et al. 2016; Zhu, Poulsen & Tierney 2019) — which complicates using cold periods to constrain a warm future.

**The structural argument (2007–2011).** Roe & Baker (2007), *Why is climate sensitivity so unpredictable?* (Science), showed that if the feedback factor f is uncertain by a modest amount, ECS = ECS₀/(1 − f) has a long upper tail that improved knowledge of f can barely shorten. Hannart et al. (2009) and Annan & Hargreaves (2011) replied that the tail depends on the prior and the parametrization; a uniform prior on ECS (common in early Bayesian studies) manufactures fat tails. The debate clarified that the upper tail is partly a statement about what is assumed, which is why Sherwood et al. (2020) devoted considerable effort to priors. (The citation trace in `05-citation-traces.md` shows where the fat tail lived on: Roe & Baker's largest early adopters were economists and adaptation scientists — Weitzman 2009, Hallegatte 2009, Pindyck 2013, Golosov 2014 — for whom it became a premise, while the physical reply came within two years.)

**The emergent-constraint lineage (2006–2020).** Hall & Qu (2006) showed that a model's seasonal snow-albedo feedback predicts its long-term one; Fasullo & Trenberth (2012), Sherwood et al. (2014), Cox et al. (2018) and others found observables across the ensemble that correlate with ECS and used observations to pick the value. Caldwell et al. (2018) showed most such constraints lack a physical basis and fail out of sample, and Schlund et al. (2020) found that constraints derived on CMIP5 largely fail on CMIP6. The method survives with a physical-mechanism requirement; on its own it did not narrow ECS.

**The narrowing (2020–21).** Sherwood et al. (2020), *An assessment of Earth's climate sensitivity using multiple lines of evidence* (Rev. Geophys.), combined (a) process-based feedback estimates, (b) the historical record with pattern-effect and forcing corrections, and (c) the LGM and mid-Pliocene, in a single Bayesian framework with explicit priors and sensitivity tests, and obtained a likely range of 2.6–3.9 °C. The AR6 (Forster et al. 2021, Chapter 7) adopted the approach and reported 2.5–4.0 °C likely, 3 °C best estimate — and, decisively, did *not* use the CMIP6 model range, because the CMIP6 range (1.8–5.6 °C; Zelinka et al. 2020) was wider than the assessed one. Hausfather et al. (2022) argued that projections should therefore weight models by their consistency with the assessed range and observed warming.

**Since 2021.** Two dissents in opposite directions. Lewis (2023) re-ran the Sherwood framework with different data choices and a different treatment of the pattern effect and obtained ~2.2 °C — the current statement of the lukewarm position, notable for accepting the framework. Hansen et al. (2023) argued for 4.8 °C from a larger LGM cooling and larger aerosol forcing — the current statement of the high position, notable for rejecting the historical-record line as compromised by aerosols. The mainstream assessment sits between them and has not moved.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **ECS as the summary metric:** 1979 (Charney), universal thereafter (marker E: every assessment; the CMIP protocol's abrupt-4×CO₂ experiment exists to compute it).
- **Central value ≈ 3 °C:** stated by Charney (1979); reaffirmed by AR4 (2007), Knutti & Hegerl (2008), Sherwood (2020), AR6 (2021). It is the most durable number in the field. Marker (B): integrated-assessment models (Nordhaus's DICE from 1992) and simple climate models adopted 3 °C as the default; marker (C): Hartmann (1994), Pierrehumbert (2010).
- **The range 1.5–4.5 °C as an honest statement of ignorance:** 1979–2013 (marker A, five assessments). Note that Sherwood et al. reinterpret the old range as having meant roughly a 66 per cent interval, which is a reinterpretation, not a finding.
- **That ECS below ~2 °C is very unlikely:** AR4 (very unlikely <1.5), Sherwood (very likely >2.3), AR6 (very likely >2). The low-sensitivity programme's feedback-based arguments are `ABANDONED` in the literature (P05); its energy-budget arguments are `CONTESTED-WITHIN` and currently judged biased low by the assessments.
- **The narrowed range (2.5–4 °C likely):** accepted rapidly (2020–22) as the operational range — marker (A) AR6; marker (B) the CMIP6 weighting literature and the emulator calibrations (FaIR, MAGICC) use it; marker (D) no major group has published an assessment defending 1.5–4.5 since. Whether the narrowing is a *finding* or a *methodological choice* is disputed at the edges (Lewis 2023 on one side; the hot-model groups and Hansen on the other).

## What remains open

The upper tail above ~4.5 °C (structural, and now tied to the hot models — P05); whether the pattern effect is as large as the models say (the models' failure to reproduce the observed eastern-Pacific cooling trend — Seager et al. 2019; Wills et al. 2022 — cuts both ways); state dependence; the true equilibrium vs the 150-year effective value (Rugenstein et al. 2020: the regression method underestimates the equilibrium by roughly 10–20 per cent); and, since 2023, whether the recent acceleration implies a higher sensitivity or a larger aerosol unmasking (P06, P08). See `03-open-problems.md` §1–3.

## Flashpoint notes

The forty-one-year persistence of the range is the most-cited evidence, in the skeptical literature, that the science does not converge; the field's own literature (Roe & Baker; Knutti & Hegerl) explained the persistence and, in the same breath, defended the central value. The energy-budget episode (2013–2020) is a case in which a low estimate was published by mainstream authors, taken seriously by an assessment (AR5 dropped its best estimate), and then explained by new physics (the pattern effect) rather than dismissed — which is either the process working or, on the skeptical reading, a convenient rescue. The AR6's decision not to use the model range is the clearest example in the record of the IPCC *not* deferring to models. All three episodes are candidates for the project's later strand.
