# P10 — Transient response, ocean heat uptake, committed warming, and cumulative emissions

**Active:** 1979–present · **Status:** ocean delay and TCR `ACCEPTED`; proportionality of warming to cumulative emissions (TCRE) `ACCEPTED` (2009–13); zero-emissions commitment ≈ 0 `ACCEPTED` (2008–2021); the pattern effect `ACCEPTED` in kind, `CONTESTED-WITHIN` in magnitude; remaining carbon budgets `ACCEPTED` as a framework with wide ranges · **Related:** P02 (carbon sinks), P04 (ECS), P05 (feedbacks), P08 (ocean heat), P12 (sea-level commitment)

## The problem

Equilibrium sensitivity describes a state the climate will not reach for centuries. The problems that grew out of it are about *time*: how fast does the climate approach equilibrium, given that the ocean absorbs heat; how much warming is already "committed" by past emissions; and — the reformulation that changed policy — how does peak warming depend on the total amount of carbon ever emitted, rather than on the concentration at any moment?

## Why the solution mattered

The ocean's delay determines how much of the eventual warming is visible at any time and therefore how the observed record maps onto sensitivity (P04). The committed-warming question decides whether stopping emissions stops warming, which is the central fact for mitigation policy. The cumulative-emissions result replaced "stabilize concentrations at X ppm" with "emit no more than Y gigatonnes," which is the form every target since Paris has taken. Together these results are the bridge between the physics of P01–P09 and the carbon budgets that governments negotiate.

## Key papers and competing answers

### The ocean delay (1979–1989)

The **Charney report (1979)** noted that the ocean's heat capacity would delay the warming by decades and that this "makes the problem more difficult... the warming will not be apparent until it is too late to prevent." **Hoffert, Callegari & Hsieh (1980)** built the upwelling–diffusion ocean model that became the standard simple representation; **Hansen et al. (1985)**, *Climate response times: dependence on climate sensitivity and ocean mixing* (Science), showed that the response time grows with sensitivity (roughly as the square), so that a high-sensitivity climate hides more of its warming in the ocean — a result with the uncomfortable corollary that a slow observed warming is consistent with a high sensitivity. **Wigley & Schlesinger (1985)** gave analytical solutions. **Stouffer, Manabe & Bryan (1989)** and Washington & Meehl (1989) ran the first coupled transients and found the Southern Ocean lagging by decades because of its deep mixing. (The GFDL line — Bryan et al. 1982, Thompson & Schneider 1982, Manabe & Stouffer 1988 — and the GISS line — Hansen 1981, 1984, 1985, 1988 — ran in parallel: Stouffer 1989 cites Hansen 1988 as the contrasting approach and does not cite Hansen 1985; `04-acceptance-checks.md`.) `ACCEPTED` immediately; the FAR (1990) built its projections on transient runs and simple models calibrated to them.

### Transient climate response and heat-uptake efficacy (1990–2013)

The **transient climate response** (warming at the time of doubling under a 1 per cent per year CO₂ increase) was defined in the SAR/TAR as the model-comparable measure of the delayed response; AR6 assesses it at 1.8 °C (likely 1.4–2.2). Gregory & Mitchell (1997), Raper, Gregory & Stouffer (2002) and Gregory (2000) analysed the transient in terms of an *ocean heat-uptake efficiency*; **Winton, Takahashi & Held (2010)** found that heat uptake is more effective at reducing surface warming than an equivalent reduction in forcing — the "efficacy" of heat uptake — because the uptake occurs in particular regions (Southern Ocean, North Atlantic) and the pattern of surface warming it produces has a more negative net feedback than a uniform warming would. This is the pattern effect in its first form. **Held et al. (2010)** separated fast and slow ("recalcitrant") components of warming; **Geoffroy et al. (2013)** showed a two-layer energy-balance model with an efficacy factor reproduces GCM transients, and this model underlies the emulators (MAGICC — Meinshausen et al. 2011; FaIR — Smith et al. 2018) used in AR6. `ACCEPTED`; a case of the zero-dimensional description (P03) being repaired rather than abandoned.

### The pattern effect (2013–present)

**Armour, Bitz & Roe (2013)**, *Time-varying climate sensitivity from regional feedbacks* (J. Climate), showed that if feedbacks differ by region and regions warm at different rates, the *global* feedback changes over time; **Andrews, Gregory & Webb (2015)** and **Gregory & Andrews (2016)** showed the same in atmosphere-only runs with prescribed observed SST patterns — the historical pattern (weak warming in the eastern tropical Pacific and Southern Ocean) produces more low cloud and a more negative feedback than the equilibrium pattern; **Zhou, Zelinka & Klein (2016)** found the effect in the CERES decade. **Proistosescu & Huybers (2017)**, **Armour (2017)**, **Andrews et al. (2018)** and **Marvel et al. (2018)** showed that this reconciles the low energy-budget estimates of ECS (Otto 2013; Lewis & Curry) with the model and paleo estimates; **Dong et al. (2019)** located the control in the western Pacific warm pool and Southern Ocean. Sherwood et al. (2020) and AR6 include the effect as an explicit correction. *Open question:* the models' historical SST pattern is not the observed one — the eastern Pacific has cooled relative to the west since 1980, which almost no model reproduces (Seager et al. 2019; Wills et al. 2022; Watanabe et al. 2021) — so the size of the correction, and whether the observed pattern will reverse (in which case warming accelerates) or persist (in which case sensitivity is effectively lower), is unresolved. `ACCEPTED` in kind; `CONTESTED-WITHIN` in magnitude. This is currently the most consequential open problem for the historical line of evidence on ECS.

### Committed warming (2005–2021)

Two different questions were long conflated. *Constant-concentration commitment:* if concentrations were frozen, how much more warming? **Wigley (2005)** and **Meehl et al. (2005)**, in paired *Science* papers, gave ~0.5 °C by 2100; **Hansen et al. (2005)** ~0.6 °C "in the pipeline." Mauritsen & Pincus (2017) inferred a larger constant-forcing commitment from observations, and Zhou et al. (2021) larger still after the pattern effect. *Zero-emissions commitment (ZEC):* if emissions stopped, how much more warming? **Matthews & Caldeira (2008)**, *Stabilizing climate requires near-zero emissions* (GRL), found that after emissions cease the ocean continues to take up both heat and carbon, and the two effects nearly cancel: temperature stays roughly constant for centuries. **Solomon et al. (2009)**, *Irreversible climate change due to carbon dioxide emissions* (PNAS), showed the warming does not *reverse* for a millennium, taking the near-zero commitment as its premise: "It is not generally appreciated that the atmospheric temperature increases caused by rising carbon dioxide concentrations are not expected to decrease significantly even if carbon emissions were to completely cease" (citing AR4, Matthews & Caldeira 2008 and Plattner et al. 2008). Gillett et al. (2011) and Frölicher, Winton & Sarmiento (2014) found modest continued warming in some models; **MacDougall et al. (2020)**, the ZECMIP intercomparison of eighteen models, found ZEC after 1000 PgC ranging from −0.36 to +0.29 °C with a mean near zero. AR6 assessed ZEC as close to zero (likely within ±0.3 °C). `ACCEPTED`: stopping CO₂ emissions stops CO₂-driven warming within a decade or two, to within a few tenths of a degree — an inversion of the pre-2008 belief, and a result still routinely misreported. (Non-CO₂ forcers complicate it: ending aerosol emissions adds warming, ending methane emissions removes some.)

### Cumulative emissions (2009–present)

Three papers in *Nature* in 2009 reframed the problem. **Allen et al. (2009)** showed that peak warming depends on cumulative CO₂ emissions and only weakly on their timing ("the trillionth tonne"). **Matthews et al. (2009)**, *The proportionality of global warming to cumulative carbon emissions*, showed that the ratio — the *carbon–climate response*, later *TCRE* — is nearly constant across models and emission paths, ~1.0–2.1 °C per 1000 PgC, because the declining airborne fraction and the declining radiative efficiency of CO₂ (logarithmic) roughly cancel the ocean's thermal lag (MacDougall & Friedlingstein 2015; Goodwin, Williams & Ridgwell 2015 for the mechanism). **Meinshausen et al. (2009)** converted this into a probability of exceeding 2 °C as a function of 2000–2050 emissions. Zickfeld et al. (2009) reached the same conclusion with an Earth-system model of intermediate complexity. **Gillett et al. (2013)** constrained TCRE from observations. AR5 (2013) made the cumulative-emissions figure (SPM Figure 10) its headline and gave TCRE as likely 0.8–2.5 °C per 1000 PgC; AR6 gives 1.0–2.3 °C per 1000 PgC (best 1.65). *No competing framework was proposed; the result was adopted within four years (marker B: the SR15 (2018) and every remaining-budget paper — Rogelj et al. 2019; Matthews et al. 2021; Lamboll et al. 2023 — take it as the premise).*

**Remaining budgets.** AR6 gave ~500 GtCO₂ from 2020 for a 50 per cent chance of 1.5 °C; Lamboll et al. (2023) revised to ~250 GtCO₂ from 2023 after accounting for updated aerosol forcing and the ZEC; the Indicators (Forster et al. 2025, 2026) put it at ~130 GtCO₂ from the start of 2025 — about three years of current emissions. The wide uncertainty (the 33rd–67th percentile budgets differ by hundreds of gigatonnes) comes from TCRE, non-CO₂ forcing, ZEC, and the definition of the baseline; it is a `CONTESTED-WITHIN` quantity built on an `ACCEPTED` framework.

### The temperature target

The 2 °C target originated with Nordhaus (1975, 1977) as the upper bound of Holocene experience; it was adopted by the German WBGU (1995), the EU (1996), the Copenhagen Accord (2009) and the Paris Agreement (2015), which added 1.5 °C (Randalls 2010 for the history). The SR15 (2018) assessed the difference between the two. The target is a policy choice; the science's contribution is the budget that goes with it and the sea-level commitment that follows it (Levermann et al. 2013: ~2.3 m per °C over millennia; Clark et al. 2016).

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **The ocean delays the response by decades and the delay scales with sensitivity:** 1979–85. Marker (B): FAR transient projections; every simple climate model since.
- **TCR as the transient metric:** 1995–2001 (marker A).
- **Heat-uptake efficacy / two-layer model:** 2010–13 (marker E: emulators).
- **Pattern effect as the reconciler of low historical estimates:** 2016–2020 (marker A: Sherwood; AR6).
- **ZEC ≈ 0:** proposed 2008, consolidated 2020–21 (marker A: AR6; marker D: "warming in the pipeline" now refers only to constant-forcing scenarios in the specialist literature, though not in public discussion).
- **TCRE and carbon budgets:** 2009–2013 (marker A: AR5 headline; marker B: universal in the mitigation literature; marker E: the Global Carbon Budget reports a remaining budget annually).

## What remains open

The magnitude of the pattern effect and the future of the eastern-Pacific pattern; the sign of ZEC for particular models and the role of non-CO₂ forcers; whether TCRE stays linear at high cumulative emissions and under strong carbon-cycle feedbacks; the remaining budget's dependence on aerosol assumptions. See `03-open-problems.md` §3.

## Flashpoint notes

Two public arguments belong here. "Warming in the pipeline" is used by some to argue that mitigation is futile and by others (Hansen) to argue for higher sensitivity; the ZEC literature is the field's answer to the first and the pattern-effect literature bears on the second. The "carbon budget" is attacked from the low-sensitivity side as depending on inflated TCRE and from the high-sensitivity side as ignoring feedbacks and aerosol unmasking; both critiques are within the framework. The quick, near-universal adoption of the cumulative-emissions result (2009–2013) is a good case for the later strand of a consensus that formed by premise adoption with no assessment needed to declare it.
