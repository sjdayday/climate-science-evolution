# P12 — Abrupt change, tipping points, ice sheets, and sea level

**Active:** 1961–present · **Status:** abrupt change in the past `SETTLED` (1993); AMOC bistability in models `ACCEPTED`, AMOC weakening this century `ACCEPTED`, AMOC collapse this century `OPEN`; marine ice-sheet instability `ACCEPTED`, marine ice-cliff instability `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; sea-level rise of 0.3–1 m by 2100 `ACCEPTED`, the multi-metre tail `OPEN`; the tipping-elements catalogue `ACCEPTED` as a framework, individual thresholds `CONTESTED-WITHIN` · **Related:** P09 (paleo evidence), P03 (models biased stable), P10 (commitments)

## The problem

Most of this project concerns responses that are smooth in the forcing. This problem concerns responses that are not: the Atlantic overturning circulation switching off, an ice sheet passing a point of no return, permafrost or forests converting from sink to source. Each raises the same questions — does the threshold exist, where is it, how close are we, and is the change reversible — and each is difficult for the same reason: the models that project the smooth response tend to be biased toward stability, and the observational record is too short to have seen a transition.

## Why the solution mattered

These are the "fat tail" outcomes that dominate risk assessments (Weitzman 2009) and the multi-century sea-level commitment that will outlast every other consequence. They are also the outcomes most often exaggerated in public discussion and most easily dismissed for that reason. The field's handling of them — AR4's refusal to project ice-sheet dynamics, AR6's "low-likelihood, high-impact storylines" — is a distinct chapter in how the IPCC expresses uncertainty.

## Key papers and competing answers

### The thermohaline circulation and abrupt change (1961–2002)

**Stommel (1961)**, *Thermohaline convection with two stable regimes of flow* (Tellus), showed in a two-box model that a circulation driven by both temperature and salinity can have two stable states. (Its citation record — eight citing works in 1961–84, then a rise that begins with the coupled-model discoveries of 1986–88 — shows the two-box picture being *rediscovered* as the origin of bistability in the late 1980s; as a framework it dates from ~1988–95, `05-citation-traces.md`.) **Broecker, Peteet & Rind (1985)** asked whether the real ocean–atmosphere system has more than one mode, pointing to the Younger Dryas; **Bryan (1986)** found the "polar halocline catastrophe" in an ocean GCM; **Manabe & Stouffer (1988)** found two stable equilibria of a coupled model differing in North Atlantic overturning. **Broecker (1987)**, *Unpleasant surprises in the greenhouse?* (Nature), and **Broecker (1991)** ("the great ocean conveyor") made the case that greenhouse warming could trigger a switch; **Broecker (1997)** called the circulation "the Achilles heel of our climate system." (The modelling line cites Stommel 1961 directly — Rahmstorf 1995, Lenton 2008 and van Westen 2024 all do — but cites Broecker through the 1985 Younger Dryas paper rather than the 1987 and 1997 essays; `04-acceptance-checks.md`.)

The Greenland ice cores made abrupt change a fact: **Dansgaard et al. (1993)**, *Evidence for general instability of past climate from a 250-kyr ice-core record* (Nature), documented the Dansgaard–Oeschger events — warmings of ~10 °C in Greenland within decades — and **Alley et al. (1993)** showed the Younger Dryas ended in a few years; **Bond et al. (1993)** tied them to North Atlantic sediment records. `SETTLED`: the climate has changed abruptly in the past, and the North Atlantic is where it shows.

**Manabe & Stouffer (1993, 1995)** simulated overturning weakening under CO₂ and collapse under freshwater input; **Rahmstorf (1995)** mapped the hysteresis in an ocean model; **Stocker & Schmittner (1997)**, *Influence of CO₂ emission rates on the stability of the thermohaline circulation* (Nature), showed that whether the circulation collapses depends on the *rate* as well as the amount of CO₂ increase — a result the field would return to in 2026 (van Westen et al. 2024 do not cite it; van Westen, Börner & Dijkstra 2026 do, as do Lenton et al. 2008 — `04-acceptance-checks.md`). **Rahmstorf (2002)** reviewed; the **NRC (2002)** report *Abrupt Climate Change: Inevitable Surprises* made it an assessment topic; Rahmstorf et al. (2005) intercompared hysteresis in models of intermediate complexity. *Competing answers by 2002: bistability real and the threshold possibly within reach (Broecker, Rahmstorf) vs weakening likely but collapse unlikely this century (most coupled models, and the TAR).*

### AMOC: observations, stability bias, and early warning (2005–2026)

**Bryden et al. (2005)** reported a 30 per cent slowdown from five hydrographic sections; the RAPID array (Cunningham et al. 2007) then showed variability so large that five sections could not measure a trend; **Smeed et al. (2018)** found a step down after 2008 in the array; **Lozier et al. (2019)** (OSNAP) found the subpolar overturning concentrated east of Greenland, not in the Labrador Sea as models assume. **Rahmstorf et al. (2015)** and **Caesar et al. (2018)**, *Observed fingerprint of a weakening Atlantic Ocean overturning circulation* (Nature), inferred a twentieth-century slowdown from the North Atlantic "cold blob" and coastal warming patterns; **Caesar et al. (2021)** from proxies argued it is the weakest in a millennium (disputed: Kilbourne et al. 2022).

*The stability bias.* Coupled models mostly show the AMOC in a monostable regime, but **Hawkins et al. (2011)**, **Liu et al. (2017)** and the review by **Weijer et al. (2019)** argued that the sign of the models' freshwater transport at the Atlantic's southern boundary is biased, making them too stable; Liu et al. showed that correcting the bias produces a collapse under warming. **Boers (2021)** found statistical early-warning signals (rising autocorrelation and variance) in sea-surface temperature and salinity fingerprints; **Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen (2023)**, *Warning of a forthcoming collapse of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation* (Nature Communications), extrapolated them to a collapse "around mid-century" (2025–2095); **van Westen, Kliphuis & Dijkstra (2024)**, *Physics-based early warning signal shows that AMOC is on tipping course* (Science Advances), found a physically based indicator (the freshwater transport minimum) in a model collapse and in observations. **Baker et al. (2025)**, *Continued Atlantic overturning circulation even under climate extremes* (Nature), found that across 34 models under quadrupled CO₂ or extreme freshwater the AMOC weakens but does not fully collapse, stabilized by Southern Ocean winds; the authors and their critics agreed it does not contradict severe weakening. **van Westen, Börner & Dijkstra (2026)**, *Failure to track a stable AMOC state under rapid climate change* (Nature Climate Change), found that at slow warming (0.5 ppm per year) the AMOC survives past 5 °C, while at current rates (2.5 ppm per year) it collapses near 2 °C — *rate-induced tipping*, with a threshold rate of roughly 0.3 °C per decade, which is about the present rate (P08). This reconciles earlier fixed-threshold estimates with each other and returns to Stocker & Schmittner's 1997 result.

*Assessment.* AR6 (2021): AMOC very likely to weaken over the century; medium confidence that it will not collapse abruptly before 2100 — a deliberate softening from AR5's "very unlikely", which chapter 9 justifies by the models' neglect of Greenland meltwater and by Lohmann & Ditlevsen (2021): "As a result, we change the assessment of an abrupt collapse before 2100 to medium confidence that it will not occur" (§9.2.3.1; `06` §4). Lenton et al. (2019) argued the risk is "too risky to bet against"; an open letter from 44 scientists (October 2024) urged policymakers to treat the risk as serious. `OPEN` — the field agrees on weakening, disagrees on collapse, and has moved since 2021 toward taking collapse more seriously.

### Ice sheets and sea level (1974–2021)

**Weertman (1974)** showed that a marine ice sheet grounded below sea level on a bed that deepens inland is unstable — the *marine ice-sheet instability* (MISI); **Mercer (1978)**, *West Antarctic ice sheet and CO₂ greenhouse effect: a threat of disaster* (Nature), and Hughes (1981) identified West Antarctica as the case. **Schoof (2007)** gave MISI a rigorous mathematical basis. Observations then found it under way: **Rignot et al. (2014)** measured grounding-line retreat of the Amundsen Sea glaciers; **Joughin, Smith & Medley (2014)**, *Marine ice sheet collapse potentially under way for the Thwaites Glacier Basin* (Science), and Favier et al. (2014) modelled the retreat as self-sustaining on century timescales. `ACCEPTED`.

**AR4 (2007)** projected 0.18–0.59 m of sea-level rise by 2100 but explicitly excluded "future rapid dynamical changes in ice flow" because they could not be modelled — a decision widely criticized as a lower bound presented as a range. **Rahmstorf (2007)**, *A semi-empirical approach to projecting future sea-level rise* (Science), scaled rate of rise to temperature and got 0.5–1.4 m; Vermeer & Rahmstorf (2009) and Grinsted et al. (2010) refined it; Holgate et al. (2007) and Schmith et al. (2007) disputed the statistics. **Pfeffer, Harper & O'Neel (2008)**, *Kinematic constraints on glacier contributions to 21st-century sea-level rise* (Science), bounded the physically possible contribution at ~2 m, with 0.8 m more plausible (its target was Hansen's 2007 multi-metre scenarios, which it cites, rather than Rahmstorf 2007, which it does not). *Competing answers: process models (~0.6 m) vs semi-empirical (~1 m) vs kinematic bound (~2 m).* AR5 (2013) gave 0.26–0.82 m; the SROCC (2019) up to 1.1 m; **AR6 (2021)** 0.28–1.01 m (likely, across scenarios), plus a "low-likelihood, high-impact" storyline of ~2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150 under very high emissions. Observations: Church & White (2006, 2011) found twentieth-century acceleration; **Nerem et al. (2018)** measured it in the altimeter era; **Frederikse et al. (2020)** closed the sea-level budget since 1900; IMBIE (Shepherd et al. 2018, 2020) measured ice-sheet mass loss accelerating in both Greenland and Antarctica.

*Marine ice-cliff instability (MICI).* **Bassis & Walker (2012)** showed that ice cliffs above ~100 m are mechanically unstable; **Pollard, DeConto & Alley (2015)** and **DeConto & Pollard (2016)**, *Contribution of Antarctica to past and future sea-level rise* (Nature), added hydrofracture and cliff collapse to an ice-sheet model, reproduced the high Pliocene and last-interglacial sea levels, and projected more than a metre from Antarctica alone by 2100 under high emissions. **Edwards et al. (2019)**, *Revisiting Antarctic ice loss due to marine ice-cliff instability* (Nature), found that the past sea levels could be reproduced without MICI and that with it the 2100 contribution was lower than DeConto & Pollard's; DeConto et al. (2021) revised their projection down for low emissions but kept the high-emissions tail; **Bamber et al. (2019)** used structured expert judgment to put a 5 per cent probability on more than 2 m by 2100; Pattyn & Morlighem (2020) review. `CONTESTED-WITHIN`: MICI is physically plausible, its rate is unconstrained, and whether it operates this century is the question that sets the tail.

*Greenland.* **Robinson, Calov & Ganopolski (2012)** found a threshold for irreversible loss at 0.8–3.2 °C (best 1.6 °C); Gregory & Huybrechts (2006) similar; **Box et al. (2022)** found the ice sheet already committed to ~27 cm of sea-level rise by its current disequilibrium. **Levermann et al. (2013)** put the multi-millennial commitment at ~2.3 m per °C; Clark et al. (2016) laid out the ten-thousand-year consequences.

### The tipping-elements framework (2008–present)

**Lenton et al. (2008)**, *Tipping elements in the Earth's climate system* (PNAS), defined the concept and catalogued nine candidates (its earliest and largest adopters were the planetary-boundaries and early-warning literatures — Rockström et al. 2009, Scheffer et al. 2009, Steffen et al. 2015 — rather than the modelling community, `05-citation-traces.md`); Kriegler et al. (2009) elicited expert probabilities; Drijfhout et al. (2015) catalogued abrupt shifts in CMIP5 models (many, mostly regional); **Steffen et al. (2018)** proposed a "Hothouse Earth" cascade (a perspective piece, criticized as speculative); Wunderling et al. (2021) modelled cascades; **Armstrong McKay et al. (2022)**, *Exceeding 1.5 °C global warming could trigger multiple climate tipping points* (Science), reassessed sixteen elements and put several (Greenland, West Antarctica, tropical coral reefs, abrupt permafrost thaw) within reach at 1.5–2 °C. Kemp et al. (2022) argued the field under-studies catastrophic outcomes. `ACCEPTED` as a framework; the thresholds are `CONTESTED-WITHIN`, and the assessments have been more conservative than the tipping literature.

*Individual elements.* Permafrost carbon (Schuur et al. 2015; Turetsky et al. 2020): a gradual, sizeable feedback, not an abrupt one at the global scale. Methane clathrates (Kennett et al. 2003's "clathrate gun"; Shakhova et al. 2010's Siberian shelf): Archer (2007), Ruppel & Kessler (2017) and Thornton et al. (2020) found the near-term release small — `ABANDONED` as a catastrophe scenario for this century. Amazon dieback: Cox et al. (2000) first modelled it; Boulton, Lenton & Boers (2022) found resilience declining; Flores et al. (2024) mapped the stressors. Coral reefs: repeated mass bleaching (Hughes et al. 2017, 2018) makes this the element most clearly already crossing its threshold.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Past abrupt change:** 1993 (marker A: SAR; marker C: any paleoclimate text after 1995).
- **AMOC bistability in models and hysteresis:** 1995–2005 (marker B: the NRC 2002 report; the AR4 discussion).
- **AMOC weakening this century:** AR4 onward (marker A).
- **MISI and Thwaites retreat under way:** 2007–2014 (marker B: the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration, 2018–).
- **Sea-level rise 0.3–1 m by 2100, with a multi-metre tail:** AR5 → AR6 (marker A). The semi-empirical–process-model dispute was resolved by the process models catching up.
- **Tipping elements as a framework:** 2008–2022 (marker B: Armstrong McKay et al. is the standard reference; marker A: AR6 treats tipping points explicitly for the first time).

## What remains open

Whether the AMOC collapses this century and at what rate of warming; the true stability of the AMOC in models; whether MICI operates; the Antarctic contribution to 2100 sea level; the Greenland threshold; the timing and magnitude of Amazon and permafrost transitions; the interaction of tipping elements. See `03-open-problems.md` §5.

## Flashpoint notes

AR4's exclusion of ice-sheet dynamics is the clearest documented case of the IPCC *understating* a risk for want of a model — the opposite of the manufactured-alarm claim — and is often cited by scientists critical of the IPCC's conservatism. The AMOC debate of 2023–2026 is the current example of the field's tension between statistical early-warning results (Ditlevsen; van Westen) and the ensemble's reassurance (Baker), and of scientists going outside the assessment process (the 2024 open letter) to press a risk. The clathrate-gun episode is the counter-example: a catastrophe hypothesis that was examined and set aside. All three belong in the later strand's account of how the field handles low-probability, high-consequence claims.
