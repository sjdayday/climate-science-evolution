# P07 — Detection and attribution: is warming detectable, and is it anthropogenic?

**Active:** 1938–present; as a formal problem, 1980–2021 · **Status:** `ACCEPTED` → AR6 "unequivocal" (2021); the fingerprint methodology `SETTLED`; regional and variable-specific attribution `ACCEPTED` with varying confidence · **Related:** P06 (forcing set), P08 (the data), P11 (extreme-event attribution), P13 (the noise model)

## The problem

*Detection* asks whether an observed change is outside the range of internal variability; *attribution* asks whether its pattern in space, time and height matches that expected from a particular cause and not from others. The problem was posed by Callendar's critics in 1938 and formalized by Madden & Ramanathan in 1980; it required (a) a global observational record (P08), (b) a characterization of the noise (Hasselmann 1976; P13), (c) predicted patterns for each candidate cause (P03, P06), and (d) a statistical method for comparing them. It was answered in stages, with the IPCC's calibrated language recording each stage.

## Why the solution mattered

This is the question on which policy turned, and therefore the one on which the manufactured-consensus claim is centred. The scientific answer moved from "not yet detectable" (1990) to "discernible" (1995) to "likely most" (2001) to "very likely" (2007) to "extremely likely" (2013) to "unequivocal" (2021). Whether that sequence records accumulating evidence or hardening assertion is exactly the question the project's later strand will take up; this file lays out the evidence at each stage.

## Key papers and competing answers

### Posing the problem (1938–1990)

**Callendar (1938)** claimed detection and attribution together; the Royal Meteorological Society discussants replied that the warming might be natural. **Mitchell (1961, 1963)** compiled global records showing warming to 1940 and cooling after, which was taken through the 1970s as evidence against detection.

**Hasselmann (1979)**, *On the signal-to-noise problem in atmospheric response studies*, and **Madden & Ramanathan (1980)**, *Detecting climate change due to increasing carbon dioxide* (Science), defined detection as a signal-to-noise problem and estimated that the CO₂ signal should become detectable around 2000. **Wigley & Jones (1981)** and **Hansen et al. (1981)** reached similar conclusions; Hansen's paper is the first to publish a global (rather than Northern Hemisphere) record and to project the emergence date. **Barnett & Schlesinger (1987)** attempted a multivariate detection and found a suggestive but not conclusive signal.

**Hansen's 1988 testimony** asserted "99 percent confidence" that the warming was real and a "high degree of confidence" of a cause-and-effect relationship with the greenhouse effect. Colleagues (Schneider, Wigley, Michaels from the other side) objected that the detection standard had not been met. The **FAR (1990)**, in Wigley & Barnett's chapter, agreed with the critics: "the unequivocal detection of the enhanced greenhouse effect from observations is not likely for a decade or more." This is the field's own statement that, in 1990, the problem was open.

### Fingerprints (1993–2001)

**Hasselmann (1993)**, *Optimal fingerprints for the detection of time-dependent climate change* (J. Climate), gave the method (its own antecedents are Hasselmann 1979, Bell 1982, Barnett & Schlesinger 1987 and Madden & Ramanathan 1980, not the 1976 stochastic-model paper): project the observations onto the model-predicted pattern, weighted inversely by the noise, and test the projection against the distribution obtained from control runs. **Hegerl et al. (1996)** applied it to surface temperature and detected a greenhouse signal at the 5 per cent level. **Santer et al. (1996)**, *A search for human influences on the thermal structure of the atmosphere* (Nature), used the vertical and latitudinal pattern — tropospheric warming with stratospheric cooling, predicted by Manabe & Wetherald in 1967, and hemispheric asymmetry from aerosols — and found that the combined greenhouse-plus-sulphate pattern matched the observations increasingly well over time, while solar and volcanic patterns did not. (Its inputs are cited in the forms then available: Hegerl et al. "in press", Hasselmann et al. 1995 as an MPI report, the sulphate forcing through Charlson & Wigley 1994 and Mitchell et al. 1995 rather than Charlson et al. 1992 — `04-acceptance-checks.md`.) The **SAR (1995)**, with Santer as lead author of Chapter 8, concluded that "the balance of evidence suggests a discernible human influence on global climate."

**Allen & Tett (1999)**, **North & Stevens (1998)** and **Allen & Stott (2003)** developed the statistical machinery (residual consistency tests; total least squares to handle noise in the model patterns). **Tett et al. (1999)**, *Causes of twentieth-century temperature change near the Earth's surface* (Nature), and **Stott et al. (2000)**, *External control of 20th century temperature by natural and anthropogenic forcings* (Science), regressed the observed record on model responses to each forcing separately and found that early-century warming was partly solar and volcanic, while late-century warming required greenhouse gases; no combination of natural forcings reproduced it. The **TAR (2001)**: "most of the observed warming over the last 50 years is likely to have been due to the increase in greenhouse gas concentrations."

### Multiple fingerprints (2001–2013)

The method was extended to other variables, each providing an independent test: ocean heat content (Barnett et al. 2001, 2005 — "Penetration of human-induced warming into the world's oceans," Science), tropopause height (Santer et al. 2003), atmospheric moisture (Santer et al. 2007), precipitation (Zhang et al. 2007), Arctic sea ice (Notz & Marotzke 2012), the full thermal structure including the stratosphere (Santer et al. 2013), and sea level (Slangen et al. 2016). **AR4 (2007)**: "very likely." **AR5 (2013)**: "extremely likely that more than half" of the 1951–2010 warming was anthropogenic, with a best estimate that the anthropogenic contribution was about *all* of it (greenhouse gases 0.5–1.3 °C, aerosols −0.6 to +0.1, natural −0.1 to +0.1, internal variability −0.1 to +0.1, against 0.65 °C observed).

### Completion (2013–2021)

**Haustein et al. (2017)** produced a continuously updated "global warming index"; **Gillett et al. (2021)** used the CMIP6 detection-attribution experiments to constrain the anthropogenic contribution to 2010–2019 warming to 0.9–1.3 °C against 1.1 °C observed. **AR6 (2021)**: "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land"; observed warming 1.09 °C (2011–2020 relative to 1850–1900), human-caused warming 1.07 °C (2010–2019; likely 0.8–1.3), of which well-mixed greenhouse gases 1.0–2.0, other human drivers (mainly aerosols) −0.8 to 0.0, natural drivers −0.1 to +0.1, internal variability −0.2 to +0.2. The **Indicators** updates (Forster et al. 2025, 2026) carry the accounting forward annually: human-induced warming 1.37 °C in 2025, rising at 0.27 °C per decade.

### The alternatives, and how each was tested

- **Solar.** Friis-Christensen & Lassen (1991) correlated solar-cycle length with Northern Hemisphere temperature; Laut (2003) showed the correlation depended on data handling errors. **Lockwood & Fröhlich (2007)**, *Recent oppositely directed trends in solar climate forcings and the global mean surface air temperature* (Proc. R. Soc. A), showed that every solar index had declined since about 1985 while temperature rose; Benestad & Schmidt (2009) put the solar contribution at roughly 7 per cent of twentieth-century warming and essentially none since 1980. AR6: solar contribution negligible. The minority literature (Soon, Scafetta, Connolly et al. 2021) persists (P06). `ABANDONED` in the assessments; `CONTESTED` only in that minority.
- **Cosmic rays and clouds.** Svensmark & Friis-Christensen (1997) proposed that cosmic-ray flux modulates cloud cover; the correlation broke down after 1994 (Sloan & Wolfendale 2008), aerosol microphysics found the nucleation effect too small (Pierce & Adams 2009), and the CERN CLOUD experiment (Kirkby et al. 2011, 2016; Dunne et al. 2016; Gordon et al. 2017) measured nucleation rates and found the cosmic-ray contribution to cloud-condensation nuclei negligible. `ABANDONED` in the mainstream; Svensmark continues to publish.
- **Internal multidecadal variability.** Tsonis et al. (2007) and Swanson & Tsonis (2009) proposed synchronization-driven regime shifts; DelSole et al. (2011) found a substantial unforced component in the 1980–2000 acceleration; Wyatt & Curry (2014) the "stadium wave." Booth et al. (2012) argued the Atlantic Multidecadal Variability was itself largely aerosol-forced; Zhang et al. (2013) disputed it; Mann, Steinman & Miller (2014) and Haustein et al. (2019) found "a limited role for unforced internal variability in twentieth-century warming." The assessments bound internal variability's contribution at ±0.2 °C. `CONTESTED-WITHIN` as to the AMV's nature (P13); `ABANDONED` as an explanation of the trend.
- **Urban heat islands and data artefacts.** P08.
- **"Recovery from the Little Ice Age."** Not a mechanism; addressed by the forcing budget (a recovery requires a forcing, and the forcings are accounted for).

### The meta-literature: consensus studies

Separate from the attribution literature is a literature *about* agreement: **Oreskes (2004)** (928 abstracts, none rejecting the consensus position), **Doran & Zimmerman (2009)** and **Anderegg et al. (2010)** (~97 per cent of active researchers), **Cook et al. (2013)** (97 per cent of abstracts stating a position; disputed by Tol 2014, 2016, defended by Cook et al. 2016), **Verheggen et al. (2014)**, **Myers et al. (2021)** and **Lynas et al. (2021)** (>99 per cent). These measure agreement with the attribution conclusion, not with any estimate of sensitivity or impacts, and are the object of much of the manufactured-consensus argument. They belong to the project's later strand rather than to this file, but they are listed here because they are frequently confused with the attribution evidence itself.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **That warming is detectable:** 1995 (SAR) at "discernible"; universally by 2001. Marker (A); marker (B): after 2000 the D&A literature stops asking *whether* and asks *how much of which variable*.
- **The fingerprint method:** 1993–1999; marker (E): every assessment's attribution chapter and the CMIP DAMIP protocol.
- **That most post-1950 warming is anthropogenic:** 2001 (likely) → 2007 (very likely) → 2013 (extremely likely; best estimate all). Marker (A); marker (C): Hartmann (2016 ed.); marker (D): the solar and cosmic-ray alternatives leave the mainstream literature after ~2007–2011.
- **"Unequivocal":** 2021. The word had been used by AR4 (2007) for the warming itself; AR6 applied it to the cause.

## What remains open

The partition between greenhouse and aerosol contributions (the ranges 1.0–2.0 and −0.8 to 0.0 are wide because of P06); the size of internal variability's contribution on multidecadal scales (P13); attribution of regional and precipitation changes; whether the post-2010 acceleration is forced (P08).

## Flashpoint notes

Four episodes belong to the later strand: (1) Hansen 1988 vs the FAR's "not for a decade or more" — a case of a scientist ahead of the field's own detection standard; (2) the SAR Chapter 8 affair (Seitz's 1996 *Wall Street Journal* op-ed alleging that Santer had altered the chapter after government review; the IPCC's and Santer's replies that the changes were required by the review process; Edwards & Schneider 2001 for a study of the episode); (3) the "hide the decline" email of 1999, which concerned the tree-ring divergence problem in a WMO cover graphic (P09), not the instrumental record, and was reviewed by several inquiries in 2010; (4) the consensus studies. On the substance, the point for that strand is that the alternatives (solar, cosmic rays, internal variability) were each published, tested by specific predictions, and found to fail specific tests — the trail is unusually well documented and could be reconstructed paper by paper.
