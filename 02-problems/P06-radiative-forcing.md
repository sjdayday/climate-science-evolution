# P06 — Radiative forcing: trace gases, aerosols, and the forcing–feedback framework

**Active:** 1975–present · **Status:** greenhouse-gas forcing `SETTLED`; the forcing–feedback framework `ACCEPTED` (effective radiative forcing since 2013); aerosol forcing sign `ACCEPTED`, magnitude `OPEN`; solar forcing small `ACCEPTED` (with a persistent minority) · **Related:** P01, P04, P05, P07 (aerosols made attribution work), P08 (aerosol unmasking and recent acceleration)

## The problem

The forcing–feedback framework separates *what pushes* the climate (forcing, in W m⁻²) from *how it responds* (feedback, in W m⁻² K⁻¹). The forcing problem is to enumerate everything that has changed the radiation balance since pre-industrial times — CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide, halocarbons, ozone, aerosols of several kinds, land-use albedo, solar output, volcanoes — and to quantify each with its uncertainty. The framework itself had to be constructed: what exactly counts as a "forcing" when the stratosphere, clouds and land surface adjust within days?

## Why the solution mattered

Attribution divides observed warming by forcing; sensitivity is warming per unit forcing. An error in the total forcing is an error in both. The aerosol term is the crux: its uncertainty is larger than that of all greenhouse gases combined, it is negative, and it is *declining* as air pollution is controlled. That combination makes it the largest single reason the historical record cannot pin down sensitivity (P04), the most plausible explanation offered for the 2023–25 acceleration (P08), and the object of the "tuning" complaint (P03).

## Key papers and competing answers

### Trace gases beyond CO₂ (1975–1990)

**Ramanathan (1975)**, *Greenhouse effect due to chlorofluorocarbons: climatic implications* (Science), showed that CFCs absorb in the atmospheric window and are, molecule for molecule, thousands of times more effective than CO₂. **Wang et al. (1976)**, *Greenhouse effects due to man-made perturbations of trace gases* (Science), extended the calculation to methane, nitrous oxide, ozone and others. **Ramanathan et al. (1985)**, *Trace gas trends and their potential role in climate change* (JGR), assessed the observed trends and concluded that the non-CO₂ gases could contribute as much warming as CO₂ over the coming decades. This is the transition from the "CO₂ problem" to the "greenhouse-gas problem," reflected in the FAR's title chapters and in the fact that the Montreal Protocol (1987) is, in forcing terms, the most effective climate treaty (Velders et al. 2007). **Myhre et al. (1998)** fixed the simplified forcing expressions (CO₂: 5.35 ln(C/C₀) W m⁻²); **Etminan et al. (2016)** revised methane upward by ~25 per cent by including shortwave absorption; **Shindell et al. (2009)** attributed forcing to *emissions* rather than concentrations, raising methane's share. *No competing answers of substance; the trace-gas forcings are the best-known quantities in the field.*

### Constructing "forcing" (1981–2015)

The FAR (1990; Shine et al.'s chapter) formalized *radiative forcing* as the instantaneous change at the tropopause. **Hansen et al. (1997)**, *Radiative forcing and climate response* (JGR), showed that stratospheric adjustment must be included (the "adjusted" forcing) and that different forcings of the same magnitude produce different warmings — their *efficacy* — which **Hansen et al. (2005)** quantified across forcings. **Shine et al. (2003)** and **Gregory et al. (2004)** proposed defining forcing after all fast adjustments (clouds, land, tropospheric temperature) have occurred, either by fixing sea-surface temperature or by regressing radiation on temperature in a model run. **Sherwood et al. (2015)**, *Adjustments in the forcing–feedback framework for understanding climate change* (BAMS), systematized this as *effective radiative forcing* (ERF). AR5 (2013) adopted ERF; AR6 (2021) built its whole assessment on it. *Competing answers: instantaneous vs adjusted vs effective forcing — resolved in favour of ERF, which makes forcing and feedback more nearly separable and, as a side effect, raised the aerosol–cloud term.*

### Aerosols: from the cooling scare to the largest uncertainty (1971–present)

**Mitchell (1971)** and **Rasool & Schneider (1971)** first modelled aerosol cooling; the SMIC report (1971) flagged it; Bryson argued it would dominate. The literature nevertheless favoured net warming (P07, Period III). The question went quiet in the 1980s because the aerosol burden could not be quantified.

**Twomey (1974, 1977)** showed that more aerosol particles make brighter clouds with more, smaller droplets — the *first indirect effect* — and **Albrecht (1989)** that smaller droplets suppress drizzle and prolong cloud lifetime — the *second*. Ship tracks (Coakley et al. 1987) made the first effect visible from space.

**Charlson et al. (1992)**, *Climate forcing by anthropogenic aerosols* (Science), estimated the direct and indirect sulphate forcings and concluded that their sum was of order −1 W m⁻² — comparable in magnitude, opposite in sign, to the greenhouse forcing then accumulated, and concentrated in the industrial Northern Hemisphere. **Kiehl & Briegleb (1993)** obtained a smaller direct forcing (≈ −0.3 W m⁻²). **Taylor & Penner (1994)** and **Mitchell et al. (1995)** put aerosols into GCMs and found that the models then reproduced the twentieth-century record, including the mid-century pause, which greenhouse-only runs had overshot. This is the result that made attribution (P07) work.

**Kiehl (2007)**, *Twentieth century climate model response and climate sensitivity* (GRL), showed that across nine models the total forcing used was anti-correlated with sensitivity: high-sensitivity models used stronger aerosol cooling. Forest et al. (2002, 2006) had shown the same degeneracy in a joint estimation. The result did not imply deliberate tuning (Hourdin et al. 2017 later described the practice), but it established that the twentieth-century record could not by itself discriminate between (high ECS, strong aerosol) and (low ECS, weak aerosol).

**Stevens & Feingold (2009)** argued that cloud systems are "buffered" — adjustments offset the Twomey effect — implying weaker indirect forcing; **Stevens (2015)** argued from the early-twentieth-century warming that aerosol forcing could not be more negative than about −1.0 W m⁻²; Kretzschmar et al. (2017) disputed the inference. **Bellouin et al. (2020)**, *Bounding global aerosol radiative forcing of climate change* (Rev. Geophys.), assembled all lines of evidence and gave a 16–84 per cent range of about −1.6 to −0.65 W m⁻². **AR6** assessed total aerosol ERF over 1750–2019 as −1.1 W m⁻² (very likely −1.7 to −0.4), with the aerosol–cloud term carrying most of the uncertainty. The **Indicators of Global Climate Change** update (Forster et al. 2026) gives −0.96 (−1.58 to −0.40) for 1750–2025 and a total anthropogenic ERF of 3.10 (2.35–3.83) W m⁻² in 2025. Black carbon (Jacobson 2001; Ramanathan & Carmichael 2008; Bond et al. 2013) is a warming aerosol whose net effect, once co-emitted species and cloud adjustments are included, AR6 assessed as small and poorly constrained.

*Competing answers within the mainstream: strong (≈ −1.5 or more; Hansen and co-authors) vs moderate (≈ −1; assessments) vs weak (≈ −0.5; Stevens) aerosol forcing.* This is the widest disagreement about a forcing term in the field and it has not narrowed appreciably since 1992.

### Aerosol decline and unmasking (2010–present)

Sulphur emissions fell in North America and Europe from the 1980s and in China after about 2006–2013; **Quaas et al. (2022)** found robust evidence that the aerosol forcing trend had reversed. **Samset et al. (2018)** estimated that removing all anthropogenic aerosols would add 0.5–1.1 °C. The 2020 International Maritime Organization limit on ship-fuel sulphur cut a large fraction of marine SO₂ emissions at once; **Yuan et al. (2024)** called it an "inadvertent geoengineering termination shock" and estimated a forcing of order +0.2 W m⁻²; Quaglia & Visioni (2024), Gettelman et al. (2024), and Jordan & Henry (2024) found smaller surface-temperature effects (a few hundredths of a degree, brought forward by a few years); Skeie et al. (2025) found the effect within internal variability; Farago et al. (2026) estimate 0.03–0.04 °C by the end of 2024. **Hansen et al. (2023, 2025)** argue the opposite: that aerosol forcing has been underestimated throughout, that its decline explains the post-2010 acceleration, and that sensitivity is correspondingly higher (~4.8 °C). This is the live form of the 1992 problem.

### Solar and volcanic forcing

**Lean, Beer & Bradley (1995)** reconstructed total solar irradiance since 1610 with a substantial long-term increase; **Wang, Lean & Sheeley (2005)** revised the increase downward by a factor of several; **Kopp & Lean (2011)** corrected the absolute value of TSI. AR6 assessed solar ERF over 1750–2019 as +0.01 (−0.06 to +0.08) W m⁻². The high-solar position persists in a minority literature — the ACRIM vs PMOD satellite-composite dispute (Willson & Mordvinov 2003; Fröhlich 2006), Scafetta's work, and **Connolly et al. (2021)**, which argues that the choice of TSI reconstruction and of rural-only temperature series can make the Sun a major contributor; Richardson & Benestad (2022) replied that the statistical method (sequential fitting) inflates the solar share. The assessments treat the solar contribution to post-1950 warming as negligible (P07). Volcanic forcing (Robock 2000) is episodic and well quantified since Pinatubo.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Non-CO₂ gases matter comparably to CO₂:** 1985–1990 (Ramanathan et al. 1985 → FAR). Marker (E): every scenario since Hansen et al. (1988) includes them.
- **Greenhouse-gas forcing values:** settled by 1998 (Myhre) with minor revisions (Etminan 2016). Marker (E).
- **Aerosols cool, substantially, and mainly in the Northern Hemisphere:** 1992–1995 (Charlson; Mitchell). Marker (A): SAR; marker (B): every attribution study since Santer et al. (1996) includes an aerosol fingerprint.
- **The forcing–sensitivity degeneracy of the historical record:** 2002–2007 (Forest; Kiehl). Marker (B): Sherwood et al. (2020) treat it as the reason the historical line of evidence is weak.
- **Effective radiative forcing as the standard:** 2013–2021. Marker (A).
- **Solar forcing small since 1950:** 2007 (Lockwood & Fröhlich; P07), reaffirmed in every assessment. The minority literature is `CONTESTED-WITHIN` only in the loose sense that it appears in peer-reviewed venues; it has not changed the assessments.

## What remains open

The magnitude of aerosol–cloud forcing (a factor of four); the rate at which aerosol forcing is declining and thus the "unmasking" now under way; black carbon's net effect; whether the historical record's aerosol term can ever be constrained well enough to make the energy-budget method decisive for ECS. See `03-open-problems.md` §2.

## Flashpoint notes

Aerosols are the term skeptics point to when arguing that models are tuned (Kiehl 2007 is the citation); the field's response is that the degeneracy is real, openly documented, and the reason it no longer relies on the historical record alone. The solar question is the other standing flashpoint: the assessments' negligible solar ERF rests on satellite-era TSI and on the physics of the solar cycle's small amplitude, and the minority position (Soon, Scafetta, Connolly and co-authors) rests on contested pre-satellite reconstructions and on regressions whose validity is disputed. For the project's later strand, the interesting feature is that the aerosol disagreement (Hansen vs the assessments) is currently *larger in stakes* than the solar one but generates no "manufactured consensus" claim, presumably because it points toward higher rather than lower sensitivity.
