# P13 — Predictability, internal variability, and climate as a statistical object

**Active:** 1963–present · **Status:** the chaos/stochastic framework `SETTLED` (1976–80); climate predictability "of the second kind" `ACCEPTED`; the magnitude of simulated multidecadal variability `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; the nature of the Atlantic multidecadal variability `CONTESTED-WITHIN` (moving toward "largely forced"); decadal prediction skill `ACCEPTED` as modest and regional · **Related:** P07 (detection), P08 (hiatus and acceleration), P11 (regional projections), P12 (early-warning statistics)

## The problem

Weather is unpredictable beyond about two weeks; climate is the statistics of weather. In what sense, then, can climate be predicted, and how much of any observed change is "noise" — variability the system generates on its own — rather than response to forcing? Every detection argument, every claim that a decade's trend means something, and every regional projection depends on an answer. The problem was posed by Lorenz and Hasselmann, and it recurs whenever the record does something the forcing did not obviously call for.

## Why the solution mattered

Without a noise model there is no detection (P07) and no way to say whether a slowdown (P08) or a surge (P08) is meaningful. The size of internal variability on multidecadal scales bounds how much twentieth-century warming could be natural (AR6: ±0.2 °C) — a bound that comes almost entirely from models, and that skeptical arguments attack directly. And the recognition that regional climate over the next decades is dominated by internal variability (Hawkins & Sutton 2009; Deser et al. 2012) changed what the field claims to be able to project.

## Key papers and competing answers

### Chaos and the two kinds of predictability (1963–1975)

**Lorenz (1963)**, *Deterministic nonperiodic flow* (J. Atmos. Sci.), showed that a simple deterministic system can be unpredictable because of sensitivity to initial conditions. **Lorenz (1968)** asked whether climate is "transitive" (has a unique statistical state) or "intransitive" (can occupy different statistical states with the same forcing) — the question that P12's multiple equilibria later gave content to. **Lorenz (1975)**, *Climatic predictability*, distinguished prediction of the *first kind* (from initial conditions, limited by chaos) from the *second kind* (of the statistical response to changed boundary conditions, which chaos does not preclude). Climate projection is a problem of the second kind; that framing is what allows a chaotic system to be projected at all, and it is `SETTLED` — it appears in every textbook and every assessment's treatment of "projection" vs "prediction."

### Stochastic climate models (1975–1977)

**Hasselmann (1976)**, *Stochastic climate models, Part I* (Tellus), showed that a slow component (the ocean) integrating fast random forcing (weather) produces a red-noise spectrum — variance increasing toward long periods — with no external cause (its cited antecedents are Lorenz 1968's transitivity question, J. M. Mitchell Jr.'s 1966 stochastic air–sea model, and the Budyko/Sellers energy-balance models — not Lorenz 1963); **Frankignoul & Hasselmann (1977)** applied it to sea-surface temperature; **Leith (1975)** connected variability to sensitivity through the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. This is the null hypothesis against which every detection statistic is tested and the reason that a decade of temperature can move without any forcing. `SETTLED`; marker (E): the control runs against which fingerprints are tested are its operational form; marker (C): by 2012 the red-noise null is used without attribution (Deser et al. 2012 do not cite Hasselmann). The paper's first decade of adopters — Hays et al. 1976, Frankignoul & Hasselmann 1977, Imbrie & Imbrie 1980, Benzi et al. 1982, Pisias & Moore 1981, North 1981 — is paleoclimate and dynamics; its role as the detection null (P07) is a 1990s development (`05-citation-traces.md`). Wunsch (1999) is the standard warning about the ease of seeing oscillations in short red-noise records.

### Modes of variability (1969–2000)

**Bjerknes (1969)** explained ENSO as a coupled ocean–atmosphere instability; **Cane et al. (1986)** and **Zebiak & Cane (1987)** predicted an El Niño with a coupled model — the first successful climate prediction of the first kind at seasonal range. The Pacific Decadal Oscillation (Mantua et al. 1997) and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation/Variability (Schlesinger & Ramankutty 1994; Kerr 2000; Delworth & Mann 2000) were identified as the principal slow modes, and each was then invoked to explain parts of the twentieth-century record. `ACCEPTED` as descriptions; `CONTESTED-WITHIN` as to whether the AMV is an internal oscillation at all.

### Is the AMV internal or forced? (2012–2021)

**Booth et al. (2012)** argued from a model that aerosol forcing drives most of the North Atlantic multidecadal variability; **Zhang et al. (2013)** replied that the model's ocean response was wrong; **Clement et al. (2015)** argued the AMO could arise without ocean circulation at all (rebutted by Zhang et al. 2016); **Mann, Steinman & Miller (2014, 2020)** and **Mann et al. (2021)** argued that model control runs show no multidecadal oscillation and that the observed one is driven by volcanic and aerosol forcing. The opposing view — that the AMV is a genuine internal mode with a substantial contribution to twentieth-century temperature — is represented by DelSole et al. (2011), Wyatt & Curry (2014), and Kravtsov et al. (2018). `CONTESTED-WITHIN`; the assessments treat internal variability's contribution to the century's warming as small either way.

### Is the models' variability the right size? (2006–present)

**Laepple & Huybers (2014)**, *Ocean surface temperature variability: large model–data differences at decadal and longer periods* (PNAS), compared 33 noise-corrected Holocene marine proxies and instrumental records with long simulations and found "SST variability that is systematically smaller than instrumental and proxy-based estimates. Discrepancies in variability are largest at low latitudes and increase with timescale, reaching two orders of magnitude for tropical variability at millennial timescales" [P]: at low latitudes proxy variance is 45 times the models' at multicentennial and more than 100 times at millennial timescales, at mid to high latitudes 10 and 49 times, while at decadal timescales outside the tropics "the models have slightly greater variance than the instrumental observations". They drew the consequence themselves: "Insomuch as models underestimate natural SST variability, tests for anthropogenic effects will tend to be biased positive" [P]. **Cheung et al. (2017)** found models under-represent internal variability in the North Atlantic (>20 yr) and North Pacific (>40 yr) "while agreement is found in the NH variability" [P: abstract]; **Kravtsov et al. (2018)** argued that the observed record minus the models' scaled forced signal contains "a pronounced Global Multidecadal Oscillation" absent from every simulation, with global-mean trends "as large as 0.3 °C per 40 years… capable of doubling, nullifying or even reversing the forced global warming trends on that timescale", while conceding that its oscillatory character "is impossible to establish due to shortness of the data record" [P]. **Laepple et al. (2023)**, *Regional but not global temperature variability underestimated by climate models at supradecadal timescales* (Nature Geoscience), is the field's own summary: the regional discrepancy is "primarily due to climate model deficiencies"; at global scales "studies found agreement between simulated and proxy reconstructed temperature variations" [P: abstract]. This is the crux for detection: if models under-represent multidecadal variability, the ±0.2 °C bound is too tight. The mainstream response is that the *global-mean* and hemispheric variance is adequately simulated (Cheung 2017; Neukom et al. 2019; Laepple 2023), that the spatial pattern of unforced variability (high latitudes, east Pacific) differs from the pattern of the observed warming (Parsons et al. 2020 [P: abstract]; AR6 ch. 3 [P]), that detection survives doubled variability (AR4 ch. 9 [P]; **Sippel et al. 2021**, *Science Advances*, "extremely likely that at least 85% is attributable to external forcing… robust even when evaluated against models with high variability and if decadal-scale variability were doubled" [P: abstract]), that paleo reconstructions of the last millennium (P09) show no internal excursion resembling the present warming, and that the energy budget excludes internal redistribution as the cause of a warming the ocean is gaining heat through. Mann, Steinman & Miller (2020) find no multidecadal oscillation distinct from red noise in 43 control runs and read Kravtsov's residual as the forced aerosol signature "masquerading as part of an AMO oscillation" [P] — the same objection in reverse, since Kravtsov's residual method attributes any error in the models' forced response to internal variability. The assessments have carried the dependence openly: AR4 ch. 9 noted that models "may not simulate natural internal climate variability accurately, particularly at small spatial scales" and that the detection results "hold even if the estimate of internal climate variability from the control simulation is doubled"; AR5 ch. 10 called the residual-consistency test "relatively weak… not a safeguard against unrealistic variability"; AR6 ch. 3 set its attribution confidence "balanced against new literature that raises questions about the ability of some models to simulate variability… (Laepple and Huybers, 2014; Parsons et al., 2017; Friedman et al., 2020)" and the SPM's allowance for internal variability widened from ±0.1 °C (AR5, 1951–2010) to ±0.2 °C (AR6, 1850–1900 to 2010–2019) [P]. `CONTESTED-WITHIN` at regional and centennial scales, which the field concedes; at the global scale the claim's residual — a real mode absent from every model that happened to add to the post-1970 warming — is excluded by the pattern, paleo and energy-budget evidence rather than by the models' own variance (`08-manufactured-consensus.md` §6).

*The long-memory argument.* Koutsoyiannis (2003), **Cohn & Lins (2005)**, *Nature's style: naturally trendy* (GRL), and Rybski et al. (2006) argued that if climate noise has long memory (Hurst behaviour), trends that look significant under short-memory assumptions may not be; Zorita, von Storch & Stocker (2008) and Lovejoy (2014) tested the recent run of warm years against long-memory nulls and found it still highly unlikely. The argument is technically correct about trend tests on a single series and is why attribution uses fingerprints and multiple variables rather than trend significance alone. (This is a separate tradition — Hurst 1951, Mandelbrot & Wallis 1968–69, Klemeš 1974, Koutsoyiannis — that does not cite Hasselmann; and Laepple & Huybers 2014, below, cite neither: three lineages arriving at the same worry, `04-acceptance-checks.md` §5(d).) `ACCEPTED` as a caution; `ABANDONED` as an argument against attribution.

### Large ensembles and time of emergence (2009–2020)

**Hawkins & Sutton (2009)** partitioned projection uncertainty into scenario, model and internal-variability components; **Deser et al. (2012)**, *Communication of the role of natural variability in future North American climate* (Nature Climate Change), used a forty-member initial-condition ensemble to show that regional trends over fifty years can differ in sign between members; **Kay et al. (2015)** made the CESM Large Ensemble a community resource; Thompson et al. (2015) quantified the consequences; Deser et al. (2020) reviewed. **Hawkins & Sutton (2012)** defined the *time of emergence* of the forced signal; Santer et al. (2011) showed that about seventeen years are needed for a tropospheric trend to be distinguishable from noise. `ACCEPTED`; marker (E): initial-condition ensembles are now standard in CMIP and in attribution; marker (B): the "hiatus" literature (P08) and the recent-acceleration literature (Beaulieu et al. 2024; Foster & Rahmstorf 2026) argue in these terms.

### Decadal prediction (2007–2020)

**Smith et al. (2007)** and **Keenlyside et al. (2008)** reported skill in initialized decadal forecasts (and disagreed about the coming decade); **Meehl et al. (2009)** reviewed the enterprise; the CMIP5/6 Decadal Climate Prediction Project institutionalized it. **Scaife & Smith (2018)** identified a "signal-to-noise paradox": models predict the real world better than they predict themselves, implying their forced signal is too weak relative to their noise — "the total variance in the models is often close in magnitude to the observed variance, and so it is not a simple matter of models containing too much variability", and if the paradox extends to the forced response "the role of internal variability will be overestimated" by large-ensemble partitions [P]; **Smith et al. (2020)** used it to argue that North Atlantic climate is far more predictable than models imply. `ACCEPTED`: modest, regional, mostly North Atlantic skill; the paradox is `OPEN` and bears on whether models' variability is structurally wrong.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Climate as predictability of the second kind:** 1975; marker (C): every text since.
- **Red-noise internal variability as the null:** 1976–1980; marker (E): control runs.
- **Internal variability's contribution to the century's warming is small (±0.1–0.2 °C):** 2001–2021 (marker A); rests on models and on the last-millennium reconstructions.
- **Regional projections over decades are dominated by internal variability:** 2009–2015 (marker A: AR5 Chapter 11; marker B: the large-ensemble literature).
- **Seventeen-plus years for a global trend to be meaningful:** 2011 (Santer et al.); marker (B): the hiatus and acceleration debates.

## What remains open

The magnitude of simulated multidecadal and regional variability; the nature of the AMV; the signal-to-noise paradox; whether the eastern-Pacific trend is internal or forced (P10); whether early-warning statistics can be trusted for the AMOC (P12).

## Flashpoint notes

The skeptical case is at its strongest on this problem: "it could be natural variability" is the residual argument once the radiative physics is granted, and the field's bound on it depends on models whose variability is not independently verified at the relevant scales. The field's answers — the fingerprints, the millennium reconstructions, the energy budget (a warming from internal redistribution would not show the ocean gaining heat), the stratospheric cooling — are collectively strong but are not a direct measurement of the noise. The Cohn–Lins and Laepple–Huybers papers are mainstream statements of the difficulty and were the starting point for the later strand's treatment of what the skeptical position, at its most defensible, actually claims (`08-manufactured-consensus.md` §6, 2026-10-01): the regional underestimate is conceded by the field; the global claim is answered by evidence that does not run through the models' control-run variance.
