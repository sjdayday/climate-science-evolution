# P02 — Does emitted CO₂ accumulate? Ocean uptake, the airborne fraction, and the carbon cycle

**Active:** 1938–present · **Status:** `ACCEPTED` (accumulation, partition, long tail); `CONTESTED-WITHIN` (feedback magnitudes, sink trends) · **Related:** P06 (forcing), P10 (cumulative emissions, zero-emissions commitment), P12 (permafrost, methane)

## The problem

Fossil-fuel combustion emits CO₂; the ocean contains about fifty times as much carbon as the atmosphere and exchanges with it rapidly. Does the emitted CO₂ stay in the air long enough to matter? If part of it stays, what fraction (the *airborne fraction*), where does the rest go, and will those sinks keep working as the climate warms?

## Why the solution mattered

Before 1957 the standard answer was that the ocean would absorb nearly all of it; the greenhouse hypothesis was moot on that ground alone. The answers that emerged — that the ocean's carbonate chemistry limits its uptake, that roughly 40–45 per cent of emissions have stayed airborne, that the land biosphere is a net sink rather than the source it was once thought to be, and that a substantial fraction of any pulse remains in the atmosphere for millennia — determine the relationship between what humans emit and what the atmosphere contains. Every projection, and the entire carbon-budget framing of policy (P10), depends on them.

## Key papers and competing answers

### Phase 1 — Does the ocean take it up? (1938–1965)

**Callendar (1938)** assumed most emissions remained airborne; his critics assumed the reverse. The question could not be settled without knowing the rate of air–sea exchange and the ocean's capacity.

**Suess (1955)** identified the dilution of atmospheric radiocarbon by ¹⁴C-free fossil carbon (the "Suess effect"), giving a tracer for fossil CO₂. **Craig (1957)** and **Arnold & Anderson (1957)** used bomb and natural ¹⁴C to estimate the air–sea exchange time at about a decade — fast enough, on its face, to support the "ocean absorbs it" view.

**Revelle & Suess (1957)**, *Carbon dioxide exchange between atmosphere and ocean and the question of an increase of atmospheric CO₂ during the past decades* (Tellus). The paper is famous for two things that sit uneasily together. Most of it argues that the ocean absorbs emissions within a decade and that the atmospheric increase should be small; near the end it introduces the carbonate *buffer factor* — because dissolved CO₂ reacts with carbonate ions, the surface ocean's capacity to take up additional CO₂ is only about a tenth of what the total carbon content suggests — and observes that human beings are "carrying out a large-scale geophysical experiment." Revelle's later reputation as the discoverer of the problem rests on the second part; contemporaries read the first.

**Bolin & Eriksson (1959)**, in the Rossby memorial volume, drew the buffer factor's consequence explicitly: with slow mixing into the deep ocean and a buffer factor near 10, a large fraction of emissions must remain in the atmosphere, and they projected a ~25 per cent increase by 2000 under exponential emissions growth (the observed figure was about 20 per cent over the base they used). *Competing answers: ocean absorbs nearly all (pre-1957 consensus) vs. ocean absorbs a fraction, atmosphere accumulates (Revelle–Suess as read by Bolin–Eriksson).*

**Keeling (1960)**, *The concentration and isotopic abundances of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere* (Tellus), and the subsequent Mauna Loa record (Keeling et al. 1976; Keeling 1998 retrospective), settled the empirical question: CO₂ was rising at a rate consistent with roughly half of fossil emissions remaining airborne. Earlier measurements (compiled by Callendar and by Slocum 1955) were too scattered to show it; Keeling's contribution was instrumental precision and site selection, not theory.

### Phase 2 — Where does the rest go? The "missing sink" (1970–2000)

With the airborne fraction known, the budget had to close: emissions = atmospheric increase + ocean uptake + land uptake. **Oeschger et al. (1975)** built the box-diffusion ocean model that became the standard for ocean uptake; **Bacastow & Keeling (1973)** and **Siegenthaler & Oeschger (1978)** developed similar models. Ocean models could absorb about a third of emissions.

**Woodwell et al. (1978)**, *The biota and the world carbon budget* (Science), argued that deforestation was releasing carbon at a rate comparable to fossil fuels. If true, the ocean would have to absorb twice what the models allowed. **Broecker et al. (1979)**, *Fate of fossil fuel carbon dioxide and the global carbon budget* (Science), replied that the ocean could not, and that the biosphere must be roughly neutral or a net sink. *Competing answers: biota a large source (Woodwell) vs. biota neutral or a sink (Broecker).* The ¹³C record (Stuiver 1978) and, later, atmospheric O₂ measurements supported Broecker. The "missing sink" — the carbon that neither atmosphere nor modelled ocean accounted for — was the field's central puzzle for two decades.

**Tans, Fung & Takahashi (1990)**, *Observational constraints on the global atmospheric CO₂ budget* (Science), used the north–south gradient of atmospheric CO₂ to argue that a large terrestrial sink must exist in the Northern Hemisphere mid-latitudes, and that ocean uptake was smaller than modelled. Ocean modellers resisted the second claim (Sarmiento et al. 1992; Sarmiento & Sundquist 1992 on the riverine correction). Resolution came from independent tracers: **Keeling, Piper & Heimann (1996)** and **Battle et al. (2000)** used the decline in atmospheric O₂/N₂ to partition land and ocean sinks; **Sabine et al. (2004)** inventoried anthropogenic carbon in the ocean directly (about 48 per cent of 1800–1994 fossil emissions). By the early 2000s the partition was: roughly 45 per cent airborne, 25–30 per cent ocean, 25–30 per cent land (with land-use emissions on the source side).

**The Global Carbon Budget** (Le Quéré et al. from 2009; Friedlingstein et al. annually since 2019) institutionalized the accounting; the residual "budget imbalance" is now of order 10 per cent of emissions.

### Phase 3 — How long does it last, and do the sinks hold? (2000–present)

**Archer (2005)** and **Archer et al. (2009)**, *Atmospheric lifetime of fossil fuel carbon dioxide* (Ann. Rev. Earth Planet. Sci.), established that "lifetime" is a spread of timescales: a pulse is taken up by the ocean over centuries, but 20–35 per cent remains airborne after a thousand years, and the final removal (silicate weathering) takes 10⁵ years. This distinguishes the *residence time* of a molecule (~5 years, set by gross exchange) from the *adjustment time* of a perturbation (centuries to millennia) — a distinction the FAR (1990) already drew, and one that is chronically confused in public argument.

**Cox et al. (2000)**, *Acceleration of global warming due to carbon-cycle feedbacks in a coupled climate model* (Nature), ran the first coupled climate–carbon model and found a large positive feedback (with Amazon dieback). **Friedlingstein et al. (2006)**, the C4MIP intercomparison, found all eleven models had a positive climate–carbon feedback, ranging from 20 to 200 ppm of extra CO₂ by 2100. Arora et al. (2020) updated for CMIP6; the spread persists. *Competing answers within the mainstream: modest vs. large feedback, mainly from tropical land and Southern Ocean uptake.*

**Airborne fraction trends.** Canadell et al. (2007) reported a rising airborne fraction; Knorr (2009) found no statistically significant trend; the question remains open and small in magnitude. Permafrost carbon (Schuur et al. 2015) and methane feedbacks are handled in P12.

## What was accepted, when, and on what evidence

- **Accumulation is real and the ocean does not absorb most of it:** accepted by ~1965. Marker (A): PSAC (1965), whose CO₂ appendix (Revelle, Broecker, Craig, Keeling, Smagorinsky) states it; marker (B): every 1970s modelling paper (Manabe & Wetherald 1975; Broecker 1975) takes the rising concentration as given; marker (D): the "ocean absorbs it all" position disappears from the literature after Keeling's first decade of data.
- **The land biosphere is a net sink, mostly in the Northern Hemisphere:** accepted ~1995–2004. Marker (B): the O₂/N₂ and inventory papers are taken as the partition in the TAR (2001) and AR4 (2007); marker (E): the Global Carbon Budget's annual accounting.
- **The multi-century tail:** accepted ~2005–2009. Marker (A): AR4 states that "about 50% of a CO₂ increase will be removed within 30 years... a further 30% within a few centuries, and the remaining 20% may stay in the atmosphere for many thousands of years"; marker (B): the irreversibility literature (Solomon et al. 2009; P10) builds on Archer.
- **Positive climate–carbon feedback:** sign accepted since C4MIP (2006); magnitude `CONTESTED-WITHIN`. AR6 does not state it as a separate warming increment; it enters the assessment through the spread of the transient climate response to cumulative emissions (P10) and through the permafrost adjustment to remaining carbon budgets.

## Inheritance

- *From:* P01 (Plass's radiative result made measuring CO₂ worthwhile); radiocarbon geochemistry (Libby, Suess).
- *To:* P06 (concentration pathways determine forcing); P10 (the near-proportionality of warming to cumulative emissions depends on the ocean carbon and heat sinks having similar timescales — Matthews et al. 2009 — and the zero-emissions commitment depends on the long tail); P12 (permafrost and methane as tipping elements).

## What remains open

The magnitude of carbon-cycle feedbacks (tropical forest, Southern Ocean, permafrost); whether the land and ocean sinks are already weakening (2023 saw an unusually weak land sink, attributed to El Niño and fires — Ke et al. 2024); the trend in airborne fraction; and the representation of nutrient limitation and fire in Earth-system models. None of these threatens the accumulation result; they affect the size of the remaining carbon budget (P10).

## Flashpoint notes

Three recurrent claims are addressed by this problem's history: (1) "CO₂'s lifetime is only five years" (confuses residence with adjustment time — FAR 1990; Archer 2009); (2) "human emissions are only ~4 per cent of natural fluxes" (gross vs. net — the natural fluxes are balanced; the budget accounting since Bolin has always been net); (3) "the CO₂ rise is out-gassing from a warming ocean" (contradicted by the Suess effect, the ¹³C decline, the O₂ decline, and the direct measurement that the ocean is taking carbon up — Sabine 2004). The history also shows a genuine, decades-long open problem (the missing sink) that was solved by new tracers rather than by assertion, which is a useful contrast case for the project's later strand.
