# Assessment language across cycles

**Status:** v0.2 (2026-09-30). Item 3 of the README's list of next passes.

This file tabulates, question by question, what the major assessments actually said — the calibrated statement, verbatim — so that the project's later strand (the IPCC's role in formalizing or asserting consensus) can work from the record rather than from paraphrase. It is organized by question rather than by report, because the point is to watch each statement move.

**Provenance.** Every quotation is tagged: **[P]** = extracted this session from the primary document (the IPCC PDF or HTML text, read directly); **[S]** = reproduced from a named secondary source; **[M]** = from memory of the document, not re-verified here. The IPCC PDFs were read by extracting their text in the browser; the 1990 report is an OCR scan and its text contains OCR errors, silently corrected here only where the reading is unambiguous (e.g., "0 3°C" → "0.3°C").

**Reports.** FAR = First Assessment Report, WG I Policymakers Summary (1990). SAR = Second Assessment, WG I Summary for Policymakers (1995; published 1996). TAR = Third Assessment (2001). AR4 (2007), AR5 (2013), AR6 (2021). SR15 = Special Report on 1.5 °C (2018). Where the Charney report (1979) or a National Academies report bears on the question it is included.

## 1. Detection and attribution of the warming

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) | Calibrated term |
|---|---|---|
| **Charney 1979** | "We estimate the most probable global warming for a doubling of CO₂ to be near 3 °C with a probable error of ± 1.5 °C" (on the prediction, not on detection). **[S: widely reproduced; the NAP reader renders page images only]** | none |
| **FAR 1990** [P] | "The size of this warming is broadly consistent with predictions of climate models, but it is also of the same magnitude as natural climate variability. Thus the observed increase could be largely due to this natural variability; alternatively this variability and other human factors could have offset a still larger human-induced greenhouse warming. The unequivocal detection of the enhanced greenhouse effect from observations is not likely for a decade or more." | none (pre-calibration) |
| **SAR 1995** [P] | "Our ability to quantify the human influence on global climate is currently limited because the expected signal is still emerging from the noise of natural variability, and because there are uncertainties in key factors… Nevertheless, the balance of evidence suggests that there is a discernible human influence on global climate." | none (pre-calibration) |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "In the light of new evidence and taking into account the remaining uncertainties, most of the observed warming over the last 50 years is likely to have been due to the increase in greenhouse gas concentrations." (Section heading: "There is new and stronger evidence that most of the warming observed over the last 50 years is attributable to human activities." [S: Trenberth's summary page; heading not captured by text extraction]) | *likely* (66–90 %) |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "Most of the observed increase in global average temperatures since the mid-20th century is very likely due to the observed increase in anthropogenic greenhouse gas concentrations." | *very likely* (>90 %) |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "It is extremely likely that human influence has been the dominant cause of the observed warming since the mid-20th century." And: "It is extremely likely that more than half of the observed increase in global average surface temperature from 1951 to 2010 was caused by the anthropogenic increase in greenhouse gas concentrations and other anthropogenic forcings together. The best estimate of the human-induced contribution to warming is similar to the observed warming over this period." Contributions: "Greenhouse gases contributed a global mean surface warming likely to be in the range of 0.5°C to 1.3°C over the period 1951 to 2010, with the contributions from other anthropogenic forcings, including the cooling effect of aerosols, likely to be in the range of −0.6°C to 0.1°C. The contribution from natural forcings is likely to be in the range of −0.1°C to 0.1°C, and from natural internal variability is likely to be in the range of −0.1°C to 0.1°C." | *extremely likely* (95–100 %) |
| **SR15 2018** [P] | "Human activities are estimated to have caused approximately 1.0°C of global warming above pre-industrial levels, with a likely range of 0.8°C to 1.2°C. Global warming is likely to reach 1.5°C between 2030 and 2052 if it continues to increase at the current rate. (high confidence)" | *likely* range |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land." (A.1) "The likely range of total human-caused global surface temperature increase from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 is 0.8°C to 1.3°C, with a best estimate of 1.07°C. It is likely that well-mixed GHGs contributed a warming of 1.0°C to 2.0°C, other human drivers… contributed a cooling of 0.0°C to 0.8°C, natural drivers changed global surface temperature by –0.1°C to +0.1°C." (A.1.3) | *unequivocal*; *likely* ranges |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P07).** The FAR statement is the one Wigley & Barnett's chapter supports and is, if anything, more cautious than Hansen (1988). The SAR statement rests on fingerprint work that was contemporaneous with the assessment (Santer et al. 1996 appeared in *Nature* in July 1996, after the SAR text was approved in late 1995; Hegerl et al. 1996 and Santer's 1995 technical report preceded it) — the one point in the sequence where the assessment's confidence ran level with, rather than behind, the peer-reviewed literature, which is the structural fact behind the Chapter 8 controversy. TAR's *likely* rests on Tett et al. (1999) and Stott et al. (2000); AR4's *very likely* on attribution across several variables; AR5's *extremely likely* on the multi-variable fingerprints and the best-estimate-equals-observed finding; AR6's *unequivocal* on the same plus a decade more of data. Each step is a step *behind* a published result rather than ahead of one, with the SAR the arguable exception.

## 2. Equilibrium climate sensitivity (and transient response)

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) | Range / best |
|---|---|---|
| **Charney 1979** | "near 3 °C with a probable error of ± 1.5 °C" [S] | 1.5–4.5; 3 |
| **FAR 1990** [P] | "…model studies is 1.9 to 5.2°C. Most results are close to 4.0°C but recent studies using a more detailed but not necessarily more accurate representation of cloud processes give results in the lower half of this range. Hence the models results do not justify altering the previously accepted range of 1.5 to 4.5°C. Although scientists are reluctant to give a single best estimate in this range, it is necessary for the presentation of climate predictions for a choice of best estimate to be made…" (2.5 °C follows) | 1.5–4.5; 2.5 |
| **SAR 1995** [P] | "Temperature projections assuming the 'best estimate' value of climate sensitivity, 2.5°C… Taking account of the range in the estimate of climate sensitivity (1.5 to 4.5°C)…" | 1.5–4.5; 2.5 |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "The climate sensitivity used in the simple model ranges from 1.7 to 4.2°C, which is comparable to the commonly accepted range of 1.5 to 4.5°C." | 1.5–4.5; none |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "It is likely to be in the range 2°C to 4.5°C with a best estimate of about 3°C, and is very unlikely to be less than 1.5°C. Values substantially higher than 4.5°C cannot be excluded, but agreement of models with observations is not as good for those values." Also: "Water vapour changes represent the largest feedback affecting climate sensitivity and are now better understood than in the TAR. Cloud feedbacks remain the largest source of uncertainty." | likely 2–4.5; 3 |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "Equilibrium climate sensitivity is likely in the range 1.5°C to 4.5°C (high confidence), extremely unlikely less than 1°C (high confidence), and very unlikely greater than 6°C (medium confidence). The lower temperature limit of the assessed likely range is thus less than the 2°C in the AR4, but the upper limit is the same. This assessment reflects improved understanding, the extended temperature record in the atmosphere and ocean, and new estimates of radiative forcing." Footnote 16: "No best estimate for equilibrium climate sensitivity can now be given because of a lack of agreement on values across assessed lines of evidence and studies." TCR: "likely in the range of 1.0°C to 2.5°C (high confidence) and extremely unlikely greater than 3°C." | likely 1.5–4.5; none |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | "The very likely range of equilibrium climate sensitivity is between 2°C (high confidence) and 5°C (medium confidence). The AR6 assessed best estimate is 3°C with a likely range of 2.5°C to 4°C (high confidence)." "Improved knowledge of climate processes, paleoclimate evidence and the response of the climate system to increasing radiative forcing gives a best estimate of equilibrium climate sensitivity of 3°C, with a narrower range compared to AR5." TCR is not stated in the AR6 SPM; the chapter-7 assessment is best estimate 1.8 °C, likely 1.4–2.2 [M]. The SPM does give the TCRE: "likely cause a 0.27°C to 0.63°C increase in global surface temperature with a best estimate of 0.45°C" per 1000 GtCO₂ [P]. | likely 2.5–4; 3 |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P04).** The FAR sentence is the origin of the Charney range's forty-year persistence: the models of 1990 gave 1.9–5.2 °C, "most… close to 4.0", and the panel chose *not* to move the range — an explicit decision to defer to the earlier assessment over the newer models. AR4's narrowing of the lower bound to 2 °C reflected the paleoclimate and model constraints of 2005–07 (P04); AR5's retreat to 1.5 and withdrawal of a best estimate reflected the energy-budget estimates of 2011–13 (Otto et al. 2013, whose authors included many AR5 lead authors); AR6's narrowing reflects Sherwood et al. (2020). AR5's footnote is the clearest instance in the whole record of the IPCC declining to assert a consensus that the literature did not contain.

## 3. Projected warming and sea-level rise

| Cycle | Warming (verbatim) | Sea level (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| **FAR 1990** [P] | "…a rate of increase of global mean temperature during the next century of about 0.3°C per decade (with an uncertainty range of 0.2°C to 0.5°C per decade); this is greater than that seen over the past 10,000 years. This will result in a likely increase in global mean temperature of about 1°C above the present value by 2025 and 3°C before the end of the next century." | "an average rate of global mean sea level rise of about 6cm per decade over the next century (with an uncertainty range of 3–10cm per decade)… The predicted rise is about 20cm in global mean sea level by 2030, and 65cm by the end of the next century." On West Antarctica: "Within the next century it is not likely that there will be a major outflow of ice from West Antarctica due directly to global warming." |
| **SAR 1995** [P] | "global mean surface air temperature relative to 1990 of about 2°C by 2100. This estimate is approximately one third lower than the 'best estimate' in 1990. This is due primarily to lower emission scenarios…, the inclusion of the cooling effect of sulphate aerosols, and improvements in the treatment of the carbon cycle." Range: "between 0.9 and 3.5°C". | "For IS92a, the sea level rise by 2100 is 49 cm. For comparison, the 'best estimate' of global sea level rise by 2100 given in IPCC (1990) was 66 cm." (SPM range 15–95 cm [M].) "…the likelihood of a major sea level rise by the year 2100 due to the collapse of the West Antarctic ice sheet is considered low." |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "The globally averaged surface temperature is projected to increase by 1.4 to 5.8°C over the period 1990 to 2100… Temperature increases are projected to be greater than those in the SAR, which were about 1.0 to 3.5°C based on the six IS92 scenarios." | "Global mean sea level is projected to rise by 0.09 to 0.88 metres between 1990 and 2100, for the full range of SRES scenarios… The range of sea level rise presented in the SAR was 0.13 to 0.94 metres." On West Antarctica: "loss of grounded ice leading to substantial sea level rise from this source is now widely agreed to be very unlikely during the 21st century, although its dynamics are still inadequately understood". |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "Best estimates and likely ranges for global average surface air warming for six SRES emissions marker scenarios are given in this assessment and are shown in Table SPM.3. For example, the best estimate for the low scenario (B1) is 1.8°C (likely range is 1.1°C to 2.9°C), and the best estimate for the high scenario (A1FI) is 4.0°C (likely range is 2.4°C to 6.4°C)." (2090–2099 relative to 1980–1999; the constant-year-2000-composition case: best estimate 0.6, likely 0.3–0.9.) | Table SPM.3: "0.18 – 0.38" m (B1) to "0.26 – 0.59" m (A1FI) for 2090–2099. "Models used to date do not include uncertainties in climate-carbon cycle feedback nor do they include the full effects of changes in ice sheet flow, because a basis in published literature is lacking… Larger values cannot be excluded, but understanding of these effects is too limited to assess their likelihood or provide a best estimate or an upper bound for sea level rise." |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | Table SPM.2 (2081–2100 vs 1986–2005): RCP2.6 mean 1.0 (0.3–1.7); RCP8.5 mean 3.7 (2.6–4.8). | Table SPM.2: RCP2.6 0.40 m (0.26–0.55); RCP8.5 0.63 m (0.45–0.82). |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | B.1.1: "Compared to 1850–1900, global surface temperature averaged over 2081–2100 is very likely to be higher by 1.0°C to 1.8°C under the very low GHG emissions scenario considered (SSP1-1.9), by 2.1°C to 3.5°C in the intermediate GHG emissions scenario (SSP2-4.5) and by 3.3°C to 5.7°C under the very high GHG emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5)." (Best estimates 1.4 and 4.4 °C are in Table SPM.1 [M].) | "Relative to 1995–2014, the likely global mean sea level rise by 2100 is… 0.63–1.01 m under the very high GHG emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5)… Global mean sea level rise above the likely range – approaching 2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150 under a very high GHG emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5) (low confidence) – cannot be ruled out." |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P12).** The sea-level story is the reverse of the attribution story: the assessments were repeatedly *behind* the risk literature. FAR and SAR judged West Antarctic collapse unlikely within a century on thin evidence; TAR said "very unlikely… although its dynamics are still inadequately understood"; AR4 excluded ice-flow dynamics altogether "because a basis in published literature is lacking" — the sentence that Rahmstorf (2007) and Pfeffer et al. (2008) were written to answer; AR5 included them; AR6 added the low-likelihood storyline. The FAR's 0.3 °C per decade and the TAR's 1.4–5.8 °C, by contrast, look in retrospect like honest ranges that bracketed what happened.

## 4. The thermohaline / Atlantic overturning circulation

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| **SAR 1995** [P] | "most simulations show a reduction in the strength of the north Atlantic thermohaline circulation" (Technical Summary); "In the oceans the meridional overturning might weaken in a future climate." |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "Most models show weakening of the ocean thermohaline circulation which leads to a reduction of the heat transport into high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere. However, even in models where the thermohaline circulation weakens, there is still a warming over Europe due to increased greenhouse gases. The current projections using climate models do not exhibit a complete shut-down of the thermohaline circulation by 2100. Beyond 2100, the thermohaline circulation could completely, and possibly irreversibly, shut-down in either hemisphere if the change in radiative forcing is large enough and applied long enough." |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "Based on current model simulations, it is very likely that the meridional overturning circulation (MOC) of the Atlantic Ocean will slow down during the 21st century. The multi-model average reduction by 2100 is 25% (range from zero to about 50%) for SRES emission scenario A1B… It is very unlikely that the MOC will undergo a large abrupt transition during the 21st century." |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "There is no observational evidence of a trend in the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), based on the decade-long record of the complete AMOC and longer records of individual AMOC components." "It is very likely that the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) will weaken over the 21st century. Best estimates and ranges for the reduction are 11% (1 to 24%) in RCP2.6 and 34% (12 to 54%) in RCP8.5… It is very unlikely that the AMOC will undergo an abrupt transition or collapse in the 21st century for the scenarios considered." |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | "The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios… There is medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100." (C.3.4) |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P12).** The language on weakening has been stable since 1995 and the language on collapse has *softened* from "very unlikely" (AR4, AR5: 0–10 %) to "medium confidence that there will not be" (AR6). That is the assessment following the stability-bias literature (Liu et al. 2017; Weijer et al. 2019) and the early-warning literature (Boers 2021) — and, since 2021, being overtaken by it (Ditlevsen 2023; van Westen 2024, 2026), which is the current example of the assessment lagging the risk literature.

## 5. Paleoclimate context of the present warming

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| **FAR 1990** | Figure 7.1(c), a schematic of global temperature over the last millennium with a "Medieval warm period" warmer than the mid-20th century, derived largely from Lamb's Central England work; no calibrated statement. [M] |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "New analyses of proxy data for the Northern Hemisphere indicate that the increase in temperature in the 20th century is likely to have been the largest of any century during the past 1,000 years. It is also likely that, in the Northern Hemisphere, the 1990s was the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year (Figure 1b). Because less data are available, less is known about annual averages prior to 1,000 years before present and for conditions prevailing in most of the Southern Hemisphere prior to 1861." |
| **NRC 2006** [P] | "It can be said with a high level of confidence that global mean surface temperature was higher during the last few decades of the 20th century than during any comparable period during the preceding four centuries." "Less confidence can be placed in large-scale surface temperature reconstructions for the period from A.D. 900 to 1600." "Even less confidence can be placed in the original conclusions by Mann et al. (1999) that 'the 1990s are likely the warmest decade, and 1998 the warmest year, in at least a millennium'…" while it is "plausible that the Northern Hemisphere was warmer during the last few decades of the 20th century than during any comparable period over the preceding millennium." |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "Average Northern Hemisphere temperatures during the second half of the 20th century were very likely higher than during any other 50-year period in the last 500 years and likely the highest in at least the past 1,300 years." |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "In the Northern Hemisphere, 1983–2012 was likely the warmest 30-year period of the last 1400 years (medium confidence)." |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | "Global surface temperature has increased faster since 1970 than in any other 50-year period over at least the last 2000 years (high confidence). Temperatures during the most recent decade (2011–2020) exceed those of the most recent multi-century warm period, around 6500 years ago." (A.2.2) |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P09).** This is the cleanest case of the assessment responding to critique and then to replication. The TAR statement leaned on a single reconstruction (MBH99) and adopted its "1998 the warmest year" claim; the NRC (2006) explicitly downgraded that claim; AR4 dropped the single-year/decade claims and hedged (*very likely* over 500 years, *likely* over 1300); AR5 hedged further (*likely… medium confidence*); AR6, after PAGES 2k (2013, 2019), made a stronger statement than the TAR's, on a different quantity (rate of warming; global rather than hemispheric) and with high confidence. The sequence is consistent with the IPCC having over-reached on one emblem in 2001 and corrected in 2007–2013, and it is inconsistent with a manufactured consensus that hardened regardless of evidence.

## 6. Observed warming and its characterization

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| **FAR 1990** [P] | "Global-mean surface air temperature has increased by 0.3°C to 0.6°C over the last 100 years, with the five global-average warmest years being in the 1980s." |
| **SAR 1995** [S: Wikipedia] | "climate has changed over the past century (air temperature has increased by between 0.3 and 0.6 °C since the late 19th century)" |
| **TAR 2001** [P] | "Globally, it is very likely that the 1990s was the warmest decade and 1998 the warmest year in the instrumental record, since 1861." |
| **AR4 2007** [P] | "Warming of the climate system is unequivocal, as is now evident from observations of increases in global average air and ocean temperatures, widespread melting of snow and ice, and rising global average sea level." "The updated 100-year linear trend (1906 to 2005) of 0.74°C [0.56°C to 0.92°C] is therefore larger than the corresponding trend for 1901 to 2000 given in the TAR of 0.6°C [0.4°C to 0.8°C]." |
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "Warming of the climate system is unequivocal, and since the 1950s, many of the observed changes are unprecedented over decades to millennia." "…a warming of 0.85 [0.65 to 1.06] °C, over the period 1880 to 2012". |
| **AR6 2021** [S: Carbon Brief; M] | 2011–2020 was 1.09 °C warmer than 1850–1900. |

The word "unequivocal" entered in 2007 for the warming and was applied in 2021 to its cause — fourteen years apart, which is itself a datum for the later strand.

## 7. Commitment, cumulative emissions, and budgets

| Cycle | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| **AR5 2013** [P] | "Most aspects of climate change will persist for many centuries even if emissions of CO₂ are stopped. This represents a substantial multi-century climate change commitment created by past, present and future emissions of CO₂." "Cumulative total emissions of CO₂ and global mean surface temperature response are approximately linearly related." "TCRE is likely in the range of 0.8°C to 2.5°C per 1000 GtC and applies for cumulative emissions up to about 2000 GtC until the time temperatures peak." "Limiting the warming caused by anthropogenic CO₂ emissions alone with a probability of >33%, >50%, and >66% to less than 2°C since the period 1861–1880, will require cumulative CO₂ emissions from all anthropogenic sources to stay between 0 and about 1570 GtC (5760 GtCO₂), 0 and about 1210 GtC (4440 GtCO₂), and 0 and about 1000 GtC (3670 GtCO₂) since that period, respectively." |
| **SR15 2018** [P] | "Using global mean surface air temperature, as in AR5, gives an estimate of the remaining carbon budget of 580 GtCO₂ for a 50% probability of limiting warming to 1.5°C, and 420 GtCO₂ for a 66% probability (medium confidence)." "In model pathways with no or limited overshoot of 1.5°C, global net anthropogenic CO₂ emissions decline by about 45% from 2010 levels by 2030…, reaching net zero around 2050." |
| **AR6 2021** [P] | "This relationship implies that reaching net zero anthropogenic CO₂ emissions is a requirement to stabilize human-induced global temperature increase at any level, but that limiting global temperature increase to a specific level would imply limiting cumulative CO₂ emissions to within a carbon budget." (Table SPM.2 gives 500 GtCO₂ from 2020 for 50 % / 1.5 °C [M].) |

**What the sequence tracks (cross-reference P10).** The cumulative-emissions framework entered the SPM in 2013, four years after Matthews et al. (2009) and Allen et al. (2009) — the fastest adoption of a new framing in the record — and the AR6 "net zero… requirement" sentence is the zero-emissions-commitment result (Matthews & Caldeira 2008; MacDougall et al. 2020) in policy form. Note the AR5 wording "will persist… even if emissions… are stopped", which is true of *most aspects* (sea level, ocean heat) but has been read as saying that warming continues, which the ZEC literature and AR6 contradict.

## Cross-cutting observations for the later strand

1. **Two directions of lag.** On attribution and sensitivity the assessments moved *after* the literature and never beyond it (the SAR being the marginal case). On sea level, ice sheets and the AMOC they moved *behind* the risk literature, sometimes by a full cycle. A manufactured-consensus account has to explain why the alleged manufacturing ran conservative on the questions with the largest damages.
2. **Explicit refusals to assert.** FAR's refusal to move the Charney range despite its own models; AR4's refusal to project ice dynamics; AR5's withdrawal of a best estimate for ECS; AR5's "no observational evidence of a trend in the AMOC." Each is a place where the assessment declined to state more than the literature supported, at some cost to its usefulness.
3. **One correction under pressure.** The millennium statement (TAR → NRC 2006 → AR4/AR5) is the only case in this table where an assessment statement was subsequently weakened in response to outside criticism, and it was later superseded by a stronger statement on better evidence.
4. **Calibrated language itself is a moving target.** The FAR and SAR had no likelihood scale; the TAR introduced one; AR5 introduced confidence qualifiers alongside likelihoods; AR6 uses both. Comparisons across cycles therefore compare different instruments, which is a point the manufactured-consensus literature rarely acknowledges and the IPCC rarely emphasizes.
