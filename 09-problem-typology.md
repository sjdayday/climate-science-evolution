# A typology of the problems, and the shape of the majority's shift

**Status:** v0.8, 2026-10-03 (v0.1–0.2 on 2026-10-01; §4.4a added; §4.1 from the five reports in the original; §4.4a checked against the citing sentences; §4.4 and §4.6 now carry the APS workshop record of the troposphere dispute; §4.4b dates the turn of each closed dispute's citation stream from the citing sentences of all years, and reads the three live-minority streams the same way). Answers two questions put on 2026-10-01: whether the thirteen problems sort into kinds of dispute that explain the types and persistence of the challenges to the science; and whether, for each problem's competing hypotheses, there is a point at which the great majority of the field accepts one while a minority continues or returns — and whether that shift can be put in numbers. Sources are the P-files (`02-problems/`), the citation traces (`05`, `data/openalex-traces.json`), the assessment language (`06`), the inquiry and claim files (`07`, `08`), and, for the surveys of the field, the original reports where they could be read this pass. Tags **[P]/[S]/[M]** as in `06`: read from the primary document this pass, from a named secondary source, from memory and not re-verified.

## 1. The categories, and why these

The four categories proposed — data (sources, cleaning, combination), proxies before the instrumental record, method (physical models against empirical decomposition), and attribution — are the right first cut, and the record needs two more. The useful principle for drawing the lines is not the subject of the dispute but *what kind of evidence can end it*, because that is what the persistence of a challenge turns on. On that principle the thirteen problems' disputes fall into seven kinds:

| Type | What the dispute is about | What settles it | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R — Radiative mechanism** | Whether the mechanism exists and its first-order size | Spectroscopy and column calculation; done once | P01 (saturation; the 1.2 °C no-feedback response); P06 (greenhouse-gas forcing values) |
| **D — Data** (proposed category 1) | Provenance, bias correction, homogenization, how series are combined | New or independent instruments; adversarial replication; a found error | P08 (surface record, urban heat island, satellite troposphere, ocean heat, the slowdown); P02 (the CO₂ record and its partition); P06 (solar-irradiance reconstructions) |
| **X — Proxy inference** (category 2) | Whether a reconstruction from proxies is valid and how certain | Replication with independent proxies and methods; the method critique itself cannot be closed by new data, since the calibration period does not lengthen | P09 (the last millennium; the Holocene; the LGM); P13 (proxy-based variability) |
| **M — Method** (category 3) | Which instrument licenses the inference: physical models, statistical decomposition of observations, energy-budget inference, or the hierarchy's reconciliation of them | Not settled by evidence alone; institutionalized (the hierarchy) and re-fought at every new discrepancy | P03; the energy-budget sensitivity line (P04); the long-memory and internal-variability critiques (P13); the Arctic–midlatitude split (P11); the tropospheric amplification residue (P08) |
| **A — Attribution** (category 4) | How much of observed change is human | The signal's growth against the noise — time itself — plus fingerprints with no natural counterpart | P07; the "it could be natural" residue (P13) |
| **Q — Magnitude within a shared framework** | A quantity everyone agrees exists and no one can pin down: sensitivity, feedbacks, aerosol forcing, carbon-cycle feedbacks, TCRE | Slowly, by combining lines of evidence; never fully | P04, P05, P06, P02, P10 |
| **N — Nonlinearity and thresholds** | Whether a component can cross a threshold, and when | Events, or models that pass stability tests; early-warning statistics are themselves disputed | P12 (AMOC, ice cliffs, tipping elements); parts of P11 |

Two other kinds of dispute run through the record without being about the science: *procedure* (the IPCC's drafting and review, the 2010 inquiries) and *framing* (what "consensus", "likely" and "unequivocal" assert). They are the subject of `07` and `08` and are kept out of the table here, with one remark at the end of §3.

Most of the problems carry more than one type, and the mixtures matter: the hockey-stick dispute is X in subject and M in substance (whether a statistical method can be trusted on proxies), the satellite-troposphere dispute is D in subject and M in substance (whether a model–observation discrepancy convicts the observations or the models), and the sensitivity dispute is Q with an M line running through it (energy-budget inference against model and paleo inference). The mapping below gives a primary and a secondary type for each.

## 2. The problems sorted

"Shift" is the year by which the P-file's markers place acceptance by the great majority (`README` markers A–E); "minority" is where the competing answer went — *vanished* (no longer in the specialist literature), *outside* (continues in non-specialist venues or blogs), *inside* (continues in the peer-reviewed literature at low volume), *returned* (came back in the literature in a new form); "settled by" names the kind of evidence that closed it, or the reason it has not closed.

Three things stand out from the sort. First, the R-type problem closed once and never reopened in the literature; nothing in the manufactured-consensus claim concerns it (P01 flashpoint). Second, the problems the P-files rate `SETTLED` or `RESOLVED` are D, R and A types with X in one case; the problems rated `CONTESTED-WITHIN` or `OPEN` are Q, N and M types. Third, where a dispute has persisted past its shift, the persisting part is almost always the M component: the troposphere's amplification rate (a model–observation discrepancy), the energy-budget sensitivity line, the long-memory and variability critiques, the Arctic–midlatitude split.

## 3. What the categories predict

The categories earn their keep if they predict something about the challenges that the problems' subjects do not. Four regularities in the record say they do.

**Lifetime and closure differ by type.** D-type disputes closed fastest and most completely — in episodes of five to fifteen years each (urban contamination 2004–2011; the satellite discrepancy 1990–2005; the slowdown 2009–2017; the 2015 SST correction 2015–17), each ended by a new instrument, a found error, or a replication the critics took part in — and they *recur* rather than persist: every new dataset re-opens a version of the same dispute, and the closed episodes leave no minority in the literature. X-type disputes closed on the *conclusion* (the millennium anomaly, replicated with independent proxies by 2013–19) while the *method* critique was partly conceded and the field's own proxy problems (the Holocene conundrum, the 100-kyr mechanism) stay open: proxy inference cannot be fully validated because the calibration period does not grow. M-type disputes do not close; they are institutionalized (the hierarchy) and re-fought at every discrepancy — the hiatus, the pattern effect, the hot models, the tropical troposphere — and they are where the persistent minority inside the literature lives. A-type disputes closed by the passage of time: the binary question (is most of it human?) went from a divided field in 1996 to a five-sixths majority by 2008 (§4.1), tracking the signal's emergence, and its residue migrated into the M-type variability question. Q-type disputes never close and are symmetric: both tails persist inside the literature (Hansen's 4.8 °C and Lewis's 2.2 °C are both in print and both cite Sherwood 2020), and the forty-one-year persistence of the 1.5–4.5 °C range is the exhibit the skeptical literature uses for non-convergence (P04 flashpoint). N-type disputes are open and asymmetric: the minority sits on the alarmed side of the assessments, which have been conservative on every nonlinear problem (`03` cross-cutting observations).

**Where the minority goes differs by type.** After a D- or R-type closure the competing answer leaves the specialist literature entirely and survives only outside it (the saturation argument; "the ocean out-gasses"; urban contamination after 2011). After an X-type closure it leaves the citation chain of the field's reference work (Neukom 2019 cites neither side of the 1998–2005 dispute) but persists outside, which is why the hockey stick remains the claim's central exhibit fifteen years after the field stopped citing either side. After an M-type dispute the minority stays inside, publishes in the main journals, and is cited — at a rate that falls to a few per cent of the resolving paper's (§4.4) but does not reach zero, and sometimes rises again. This is the pattern `08` §2.1 found for the six dissenting programmes: engagement, rejection on stated grounds, and continued presence, not exclusion.

**Where the public challenge concentrates differs from where the field's problems are.** The public disputes of 1990–2017 — the record, the millennium, the satellites, the pause — were D and X types: disputes about *verifiable* things, where an outsider can re-run a calculation or request a dataset, and where a found error is decisive. The field's own open problems (`03` §§1–5) are Q and N types: magnitudes and thresholds, where no single calculation decides anything and the disagreement is among insiders. The challenge literature largely ceased after 2017, when an adversarial replication (Berkeley Earth) and the end of the slowdown removed the two strongest D-type arguments (P08 flashpoint), and did not move to the Q and N problems, where the open questions point toward larger rather than smaller risks. The one M-type dispute the challenge literature has kept is the tropospheric amplification rate, which is the skeptical community's most durable model–observation comparison for exactly the reason M-type disputes persist: a discrepancy between models and observations can be read either way.

**The claim's own form follows the types.** The manufactured-consensus claim in its institutional form (`08` §2.2) is about procedure and framing; in its epistemic form (`08` §2.3) it attaches to the M-type disputes (tuning, validation, variability) and to the one Q-type exhibit (the sensitivity range); in its rhetorical form it attaches to the D and X episodes. It has never attached to the R-type problem or, with one exception, to the N-type ones — and the exception runs the other way (AR4's exclusion of ice-sheet dynamics is cited by scientists as the IPCC understating a risk). A classification by type thus also sorts the claim: the parts of it that survive the project's tests (`08` §3) are the M-type parts, because those disputes are the ones evidence alone does not close.

The caveat is that the categories are of *disputes*, not of problems, and most problems host disputes of more than one type over their lives. The useful statement is not "P09 is a proxy problem" but "the 1998–2010 dispute about P09 was an X-type dispute with an M-type core, which is why its conclusion closed and its method argument did not." Read that way, the categories are a good predictor of persistence; read as labels on the problems, they are not.

## 4. The shift of the majority, in numbers

The question has two halves — is there a point where the great majority accepts one hypothesis while a minority continues or returns, and can the shift be characterized numerically — and the record gives different kinds of numbers for different stages. There are no numbers at all for the shifts before 1990: the acceptance dates in §2 for P01, P02, P03, P09's orbital pacing and P13's framework rest on premise adoption and textbook incorporation (markers B and C), which the citation traces in `05` confirm but do not quantify as a share of the field. From the 1990s on there are four kinds of number, and they measure different things.

### 4.1 The surveyed field: the only series that spans the shift

The Bray and von Storch surveys of climate scientists (1996, 2003, 2008, 2013, 2015/16) are the one longitudinal record of what the field itself said, and they happen to bracket the attribution shift. The 1996 and 2003 results, read from the figure in the GKSS report [P: Bray & von Storch 2007, Figure 30, read 2026-10-01]:

| "Climate change is mostly the result of anthropogenic causes" (1 = strongly agree … 7 = strongly disagree) | 1996 (N = 539) | 2003 (N = 530) |
|---|---|---|
| Agree (1–3) | 40 % (5 + 18 + 17) | 53 % (9 + 24 + 20) |
| Neutral (4) | 14 % | 13 % |
| Disagree (5–7) | 44 % (14 + 20 + 10) | 29 % (8 + 11 + 10) |
| Mean | 4.17 | 3.62 |

(Missing 1 % and 5 %; the means and N are the report's own; the percentages are the figure's bar labels, confirmed this pass from the text layer of the HZG copy, and reproduce the means to within 0.01.) In 1996 — the year after the SAR's "discernible human influence" was approved — the surveyed field was divided almost evenly on whether the change was *mostly* anthropogenic, with slightly more disagreeing than agreeing; by 2003, two years after the TAR's "likely", a majority agreed and the disagreeing share had fallen by a third.

The 2008 survey changed the question to "How convinced are you that most of recent or near future climate change is, or will be, a result of anthropogenic causes?" on a 1 (not at all) – 7 (very much) scale, and the 2013 and 2015/16 surveys kept it. All three reports were read in the original this pass [P: GKSS 2010/9 Q20–21; HZG 2014-4 Q26–27; HZG 2016-2 Figures 1–2 and 21; `data/bray-von-storch-series.json`]. The 2013 and 2015/16 histograms carry percentage labels, which are exact fractions of N; the 2008 histograms are unlabelled, so the bar heights were measured in the rendered figure and converted to counts, which reproduce the report's mean (5.678378) and standard deviation (1.433935) exactly. On one scale — "accept" is agree (1–3) on the 1996–2003 statement and convinced (5–7) on the 2008–2016 question, "reject" the opposite end, "middle" the 4 — the five surveys give:

The response-by-response counts are in the data file; the 2008 distribution is 5 / 11 / 25 / 20 / 63 / 118 / 128 from "not at all" to "very much", the 2015/16 one 12 / 13 / 12 / 44 / 88 / 166 / 305. A second 2015/16 question asked for a number: "Since 1850, it is estimated that the world has warmed by 0.5–0.7 degrees C. Approximately what percent would you attribute to human causes?" — 0 %: 4 respondents; 1–25 %: 26; 26–50 %: 63; 51–75 %: 211; 76–100 %: 283 (N = 587), so 84.2 per cent put the human share above one-half and 15.8 per cent at or below it [P: Figure 21]. The 2013 survey also asked whether, since AR4's 2007 attribution statement, respondents had become more or less confident in it: 64.9 per cent more confident, 24.2 per cent unchanged, 10.9 per cent less (N = 285) [P: HZG 2014-4 Q7]. And the companion question "How convinced are you that climate change, whether natural or anthropogenic, is occurring now?" ran at 93.8 per cent (2008), 94.9 per cent (2013) and 96.7 per cent (2015/16) convinced, with means of 6.44–6.65 on the 7-point scale; its 1996–2003 predecessor, "We can say for certain that global warming is a process already underway", had moved from 62 per cent agreeing (24 per cent disagreeing) to 81 per cent (12 per cent) [P: GKSS 2007/11 Figure 28].

So the shift on the attribution question, in the surveyed field, happened between 1996 and 2008 — from an even split to a five-sixths majority — and has since drifted up by a few points, with the 2013 survey (the smallest, N = 277, fielded as the "hiatus" literature was appearing; the report itself notes it "was conducted prior to the reports of the hiatus") a point or two below its neighbours. The rejecting share is the "small fraction" of the question, measured: 44 → 29 → 11 → 11 → 6 per cent. It is worth noticing what the 2015/16 numbers say about that fraction's composition: of the 37 not-convinced respondents, 12 chose "not at all"; of the 93 who put the human share of warming since 1850 at or below one-half, only 4 said zero. The surveyed minority is, mostly, a minority about *magnitude* rather than about *existence* — the type-Q position of §1, not the type-R or type-A one.

Two cautions. The samples were not random (institute lists, authors of ISI-indexed papers, the Oreskes list, the IPCC contributor list; response rates of 18 per cent in 2008, 7 per cent in 2013 and 17 per cent in 2015/16 [P]), so the levels carry an unknown bias even if the trend is real; and the 1996–2003 statement and the 2008–2016 conviction question are not the same instrument, so the 53 → 84 step is partly wording. The figure that best survives both cautions is the fall in *disagreement* from 44 per cent to 29 per cent between 1996 and 2003 on an unchanged question, and the fall from 11 per cent to 6 per cent between 2008 and 2015/16 on another.

The same surveys carried, in every wave, a block of questions that bears on §3's third regularity — that the field's own open problems are the type-Q and type-N ones. Respondents rated how well atmospheric models "can deal with" particular processes on a 1 (very inadequate) – 7 (very adequate) scale [P: GKSS 2007/11 Figures 1–6; GKSS 2010/9 Q12a–f; HZG 2014-4 Q9a–f; HZG 2016-2 Figures 4–7]:

The rank order never changes in twenty years: radiation, the type-R problem settled by 1970, is rated adequately handled throughout and improves; clouds and precipitation, the type-Q and type-N problems that P05 (the sign and size of cloud feedback) and P11 (the dynamical side of the hydrological cycle) leave open, are rated below the midpoint in every survey, and the 2008 survey, whose sample was drawn most directly from the authors of the ten highest-impact climate journals, puts clouds at 2.75. The 2015/16 survey asked the same of knowledge rather than models: whether "the current state of scientific knowledge is developed well enough to allow for a reasonable estimate of the effects" of anthropogenic greenhouse gases scored 5.53 (N = 600; 82 per cent at 5–7), of turbulence 3.73 [P: Figures 11, 14]. The field that is 87 per cent convinced of attribution rates its handling of clouds at 3.4 out of 7, and sees no contradiction — which is the point of separating the types.
### 4.2 The post-shift surveys: levels, not change

The surveys that produced the "97 per cent" are all post-shift and measure the plateau at different definitions of "expert": Doran & Zimmerman 2009 (97.4 per cent of 79 climatologists active in climate research; 82 per cent of 3,146 earth scientists) [M]; Anderegg et al. 2010 (97–98 per cent of the 200 most-published of 1,372 researchers) [M]; Verheggen et al. 2014 (1,868 respondents; "90% of respondents with more than 10 climate-related peer-reviewed publications (about half of all respondents), explicitly agreed with anthropogenic greenhouse gases (GHGs) being the dominant driver of recent global warming") [P: PBL summary]; Stenhouse et al. 2014 (American Meteorological Society members: about half "mostly human", with the rest spread across "equally", "mostly natural", "insufficient evidence") [M]; Myers et al. 2021 (2,780 responses to 10,929 invitations: "agreement on anthropogenic global warming is high (91% to 100%) and generally increases with expertise. Out of a group of 153 independently confirmed climate experts, 98.7% … indicated that the Earth is getting warmer mostly because of human activity … Among those with the highest level of expertise (… 20+ peer reviewed papers on climate change between 2015 and 2019) there was 100% agreement") [P: abstract read 2026-10-01]. The gradient is the finding: agreement rises with publication count in every survey that measured it, from roughly one-half among meteorologists and AMS members through four-fifths of earth scientists to 97–100 per cent of those who publish on the question. The "small fraction" is therefore not one number but a function of who is asked.

### 4.3 The literature: the minority as a share of papers

Counted in papers rather than people, the minority is an order of magnitude smaller. Oreskes 2004 found none of 928 abstracts (1993–2003) rejecting the consensus position [M; cited in `08` §2.4]; Cook et al. 2013 found, among 11,944 abstracts (1991–2011), that "97.1% endorsed the consensus position that humans are causing global warming" of those expressing a position, with "the number of papers rejecting the consensus on AGW … a vanishingly small proportion" [P: abstract]; Lynas et al. 2021 found "four sceptical papers out of the sub-set of 3000" randomly drawn from 88,125 climate papers published since 2012, and 28 "implicitly or explicitly skeptical" papers by keyword search, so that "the scientific consensus on human-caused contemporary climate change — expressed as a proportion of the total publications — exceeds 99%" [P: abstract]. So the surveyed dissent (about 12–16 per cent of climate scientists not convinced, 2008–2016) is ten to a hundred times larger than the published dissent (0.1–1 per cent of papers). The difference is the gap between holding a doubt and defending it in print; it is also the reason the citation record (§4.4) is a record of the *published* minority only.

Read with the cautions of `05` §1 (citations are not endorsements; a dissenting paper is cited as the thing rebutted; the literature grew tenfold), the table says four things.

1. *A closed controversy decays to about a tenth to a third of its peak within fifteen years* (MM05 0.10; Lindzen 1990 0.29; Lindzen & Choi 0.23), while the resolving or superseding papers keep rising with the literature. That is the numerical form of the "great majority accepts one hypothesis": not a vote but the displacement of one citation stream by another. For the millennium dispute the displacement is complete in the sense that matters — the reference reconstruction of 2019 cites neither MBH98/99 nor M&M 2005 (`04` §1; `05` §4).

2. *A return shows as a second bump.* Lindzen et al. 2001 is the clearest: 45 citations in 2015–19, 86 in 2020–24, as the "missing iris" (Mauritsen & Stevens 2015) and the convective-aggregation literature took the mechanism up without the original paper's conclusion. Spencer & Christy 1990 doubled in the same period (22 → 38) as the tropospheric-amplification exchange revived. In both cases the return is *inside* the literature and in a different form — the mechanism rehabilitated, the original claim not.

3. *A live M-type dispute runs at roughly five to ten per cent of the mainstream anchor.* Lewis & Curry 2018 and Lewis 2023 together drew 94 citations in 2020–24 and 14 in 2025 against Sherwood 2020's 1,044 and 290 — about 1:11 and 1:20 — and McKitrick & Christy 2018 ran at 22 against Santer 2017's 30, nearly even, on the one D/M dispute where the model–observation discrepancy is conceded by both sides (AR6's medium confidence that models overestimate tropical amplification). The low-sensitivity programme is, by this measure, the most persistent published minority in the set, and it persists because its dispute is Q and M type — about the weight of one line of evidence against others — not because it was ignored: `04` §1 confirms the two sides cite each other.

4. *The solar and cosmic-ray minority is the exception to the decay rule.* Svensmark 1997 and Friis-Christensen & Lassen 1991 are still cited 20–35 times a year each, more than Lockwood & Fröhlich 2007, the paper the assessments cite for the solar forcing's smallness. Part of that is the solar-physics literature citing them as history, part is a continuing minority programme (Scafetta, Soon, Connolly and co-authors; P06 flashpoint). The traces alone cannot separate the two; the citing sentences (§4.4b) can, and the answer is that almost none of it is history: since 2020, 21 of the 38 sentences citing Svensmark 1997 adopt the claim, 13 call the question open, one argues against it and none narrates it as past.

The one dispute in this table that both sides concede is open — the tropical troposphere — was argued by its principals in the same room at the APS workshop of January 2014, and the transcript gives each side's statement of it in its own words [P: APS 2014; `08` §4.1 for the setting]. Christy, on 102 CMIP5 RCP4.5 runs against the balloon and satellite records since 1979: "In every case, all 102, they are much warmer than the observations showed… this is over a third of a century we are looking at… none of the models were able to come within the range of observations" (pp. 343, 347), and against AR5's low confidence in the observations, "I don't think that's the case. I think we do have good information on observations and we have pretty good confidence" (p. 340). Santer: the slowdown "is there in tropospheric temperature, too. This is not something that is confined to surface temperature" (p. 210); the question is "why are the tropospheric temperature trends in CMIP5 models, on average, larger than those observed over the stasis period?" (p. 211); part of the answer is forcing — the models "underestimated the cooling associated with post-Pinatubo volcanic aerosols… the unusually broad solar minimum… some of the cooling associated with stratospheric ozone" (p. 266) — and part is the ocean: a model given the observed sea-surface temperatures "does not produce that error structure in the way that John showed… on monthly, on annual, on El Niño time scales, models and observations are not in fundamental disagreement… It's on those long decadal time scales where the observation results were most sensitive to the adjustments that you make with things like orbital drift" (pp. 542–43). Held, on where the model spread comes from: "clouds… are changing in different ways in the different models. They are causing the tropical ocean to warm in different ways. It's influencing the tropical atmospheric profile" (p. 545); and on the slowdown, "I think it's mostly internal variability myself" (p. 465). That is the dispute as it stood in 2014, and as the stance table above shows it still standing: one side reading the model–observation gap as a model failure, the other as the sum of forcing errors, the Pacific's decadal state and a dataset sensitivity on exactly the time scale at which the trends are compared — a Q-type disagreement about the size of each term, not a disagreement about the mechanism, which neither side disputed.

That is what a persistent published minority looks like in citation data: a few dozen authors, cross-citing, answered by a few dozen others, inside a literature that otherwise cites the contested papers as history or not at all.

Three different turns.

1. *Lindzen 1990: fifteen years as an open question, then narrated — never a rebuttal phase.* Through 2004 nearly half of all citing sentences call the water-vapour question open — "considerable controversy over whether changes in upper tropospheric water vapor will be a positive or negative feedback" (1994); "the consensus is that the upper troposphere will become more humid … this view has been challenged [Lindzen, 1990]" (1999); "the sign of the feedback … is not well defined" (2004) — and the 0.5 °C sensitivity is carried as a scenario bound in decision analyses ("one of the lowest found in the literature and is due to Lindzen (1990)", 1997). Sentences that argue against it are a small minority in every period (4 of 26, 4 of 38, 5 of 38): "a contrarian view (Lindzen 1990) holds that the increased convection … should act instead to dry the upper troposphere" (1998); a cumulus-ensemble model whose results "do not support hypothesis that upper tropospheric drying may accompany enhanced convection" (2003); "while there have been speculations that even a negative water vapor feedback might exist, most studies today favor a positive feedback" (2006). The S class throughout is Lindzen's own group and a handful of sceptical or peripheral citers ("predictions made with GCMs need to be viewed with caution (Lindzen, 1990)", a transport review of 2007). The turn is 2005–09, the window the P-file's markers give (§2, P05); and it is a turn from *open* to *history*, not from rebuttal to history — the stream never had a phase in which arguing against the paper was the main thing citers did with it.

2. *Spencer & Christy 1990: the claim left the stream within five years, the tests ran 1998–2004, the dataset stayed.* The "no warming" finding is cited as a result only in 1990–91 (4 of 6 sentences: "recent satellite data show no warming trend over the last 10 years"; "no warming of the Earth's lower atmosphere since measurements began in 1979, despite surface measurements showing an increase"). From 1995 the paper is cited three times in four as the source of a dataset or a method (9 of 12, 10 of 18, 12 of 15, 14 of 24); the explicit tests of the record fall in one window — orbital decay (Wentz & Schabel 1998), diurnal drift and the channel-2 reanalysis (Mears et al. 2002, 2003: the UAH series "has played a high-profile and controversial role in the debate over the presence and magnitude of anthropogenic warming signals"), stratospheric contamination (Fu & Johanson 2004; Fu et al. 2004: "the inconsistency between the trends at the surface and in the troposphere, traceable to the pioneering work of …") — and none appears after 2004. The history sentences begin in 2009 ("the argument shifted … based on satellite data … it was argued, there is no temperature [trend]"; "much debate surrounding tropical tropospheric temperatures since the first attempt to create a satellite-based climate dataset") and the 2011 review titled "history of an ongoing controversy" states the close: "the most recent versions of all datasets do not support the conclusion of a significant difference in trend between the surface and troposphere". The S class after 1991 is the UAH group's own dataset papers (2000, 2003, 2011, 2017, 2018) and four sceptical citers; the one O sentence after 1998 (2020) concerns the UAH–RSS trend difference — the amplification dispute of §4.4, not the 1990 claim. The P-file's "2005–06 (satellite)" shift (§2, P08) is where the tests stop: Mears & Wentz 2005 was the last, and nothing after it argues.

3. *McIntyre & McKitrick 2005: two closures, one per community.* In 2005–09 the stream is three-sided: the published comments and tests (5 of 33: the comments of Huybers and of von Storch & Zorita, Ammann & Wahl 2007, one dismissal and a duplicate record), neutral statements that the reconstruction "has been challenged" or is "discussed extensively" (9), and sceptical citers for whom the critique had already settled the matter (14: "the complete discrediting of the 'hockey stick' curve", 2007; "work that has been found to be badly flawed by improper data handling, faulty statistical methodology", 2009; the authors' own replies and expositions). After 2009 the R class is empty — no indexed sentence argues against the paper — and the mainstream citers narrate ("Their analysis was criticized by a number of authors, but most sharply by McIntyre and McKitrick", 2010; "the earliest issue on which sceptical bloggers achieved scientific publications", 2015; "Later papers criticized in turn the statistical methods of McIntyre and McKitrick (2005) and confirm[ed] …", 2019), while the S class persists at about half the stream (11 of 20 in 2010–14, none of 7 in 2015–19, 2 of 3 since 2020: 13 of 30), nearly all of it from outside the paleoclimate literature — a glacier paper ("it has gradually been acknowledged that this curve has been deduced using poor mathematical and statistical calculations", 2011), an Australian temperature-network critique, Scafetta's solar papers, policy essays, a geology text for which "the Mann curve was invalid". Read sentence by sentence, the stream is the X-type signature of §2: the field stopped arguing in 2007 and started narrating in 2010; the minority stopped arguing at the same time because, for it, the matter was settled the other way. Both communities treat the dispute as closed and disagree about who won.

Put beside §4.4, the turns coincide with the decays: Lindzen 1990 peaked in 1990–94 and turned in 2005–09; McIntyre & McKitrick peaked in 2005–09 and turned in 2010; Spencer & Christy's claim was gone from the sentences by 1995 while the paper's citations went on, because what was being cited was the dataset. So the "exception to the decay rule" of §4.4 (point 4) is explained at the level of the sentence: the two solar papers are not cited as history because, for the community that cites them, the matter was never closed. The climate literature tested the claims between 1998 and 2016 and left; the stream that persists is the adjacent one, in which the claim is a standing premise.

Set beside the three closed disputes, then, the six streams sort into three shapes. A dispute the field settled and absorbed (Lindzen 1990; Spencer & Christy 1990) ends in H and M: the claim is narrated or the paper is cited for something else. A dispute the field settled and the minority did not accept (McIntyre & McKitrick 2005; Lindzen & Choi 2009) ends in H on one side and S on the other, with R confined to the first years. A dispute that moved to another community (the solar papers) never acquires an H class at all: O and S persist at their original shares, and the R class is the trace of a field that tested the claim and stopped citing it.

### 4.5 The assessments' own numbers

The IPCC's calibrated language is the field's formal statement of its own majority, and for attribution it is a monotone series: "discernible" (1995) → *likely*, > 66 per cent (2001) → *very likely*, > 90 per cent (2007) → *extremely likely*, > 95 per cent (2013) → "unequivocal" (2021) (`06` §attribution table [P]). These are stated probabilities of the proposition, not shares of the field; but the one place both are measured — 2001–2008 — they move together: the assessment's step from likely to very likely sits inside the surveyed field's step from a 53 per cent to an 84 per cent majority. The one calibrated statement that went the other way, AR5's withdrawal of a sensitivity best estimate "because of a lack of agreement on values across assessed lines of evidence" (`06` cross-cutting 4 [P]), is the formal record of a Q-type dispute reopening — and `08` §2.3 traces how the low estimates that caused it were taken up and then explained.

### 4.6 So: is there a shift point, and can it be put in numbers?

For the A-type question, yes on both counts: the surveyed field crossed from an even split to a five-sixths majority between 1996 and 2008 (and the three critics at the APS workshop of January 2014, questioned by Koonin, conceded the warming, the anthropogenic CO₂ rise and the existence of an anthropogenic influence, drawing the line at the fraction and the sensitivity — `08` §4.1), the assessments stepped from "discernible" to "very likely" over the same years, and the published dissent fell below one per cent of papers; the small fraction that continues is 6–11 per cent of surveyed climate scientists (11 per cent in 2008 and 2013, 6 per cent in 2015/16, most of it a disagreement about the human share rather than its existence) and 0.1–1 per cent of published papers, and it has not grown. For the D-type episodes, the shift is a date rather than a curve — the found error or the replication — and the minority's citations decay to a tenth of their peak within a decade; the citing sentences (§4.4b) put the satellite episode's last test in 2004–05 and the water-vapour question's passage from "open" to "history" in 2006–09. For the X-type millennium dispute the conclusion's shift is datable (2006 with caveats, firm by 2013–19) and the minority's citations fell to a tenth, but the dispute lives on outside the literature at a volume the citation record cannot see — and the sentences show it: after 2009 no citer argues against McIntyre & McKitrick 2005, the field narrates it, and 13 of the 30 citing sentences since 2010, nearly all from outside paleoclimatology, cite it as proof. For the M-type disputes there is no shift point: the field institutionalized a framework rather than choosing a hypothesis, and the minority persists at a few per cent of the mainstream's citation rate, with returns whenever a new discrepancy appears. For the Q- and N-type problems the question does not apply, because the field has not accepted one hypothesis; it has accepted a range, and the "minority" is the two tails of its own distribution.

The returns deserve their own list, because they are what distinguish the M-type pattern from mere persistence: the iris (2001 → 2015, as a mechanism without its sign); internal variability (Hasselmann 1976 → the hiatus 2009–15 → Kravtsov 2018 → the 2023–25 acceleration debate, each time as the null hypothesis the forced explanation must beat); low sensitivity (Lindzen 1990 → Lindzen & Choi 2009 → the energy-budget line 2013–18 → Lewis 2023, each time on a different line of evidence); the tropical troposphere (Spencer & Christy 1990 → Douglass 2008 → McKitrick & Christy 2018, each time on a longer record). In each case the return came with a new discrepancy or a new dataset, was published in the main journals, and was answered there — which is the record `08` §2.1 reads against the suppression claim, and which the typology explains: a dispute about method can always be re-opened by the next observation, because the observation does not decide it.

## 5. Limits, and what would sharpen the numbers

- The stance classification in §4.4a reads each citing paper's own position from its title, venue and abstract; the citing sentences were then checked for the 37 S- and R-class works for which Semantic Scholar holds them (a third of the S class), and confirmed the sort with five changes. The two-thirds of the S class without an indexed sentence — mostly the German books, the regional solar-correlation papers and the 2025–26 items — remain classified by abstract. The two solar papers' counts are estimates from titles.
- The dating in §4.4b rests on the citing sentences Semantic Scholar has extracted — a third of each paper's indexed citers, skewed to open-access texts, in bins of 6 to 38 sentences for the closed disputes and up to 108 for the solar papers (1,063 sentences in all) — and on one reader's sort of each sentence into six classes; the sort is recorded sentence by sentence in `data/citer-contexts-by-bin.json` so that it can be re-done. The O/H boundary (a question called open versus a debate narrated as past) is the one most open to a second reading; the S, R and M counts do not depend on it, and a second reading of the O/H sentences would move each turn by a year or two, not a decade.
- The Bray and von Storch series is now read from the five reports themselves, but the 2008 response counts are reconstructed from an unlabelled histogram (they reproduce the reported mean and standard deviation exactly, which leaves little room for error) and the five samples are convenience samples of different construction, so the series is a trend in a self-selected population, not a census of the field. The raw data released with the 2008 and 2013 reports (academia.edu) were not consulted.
- Stenhouse et al. 2014, Doran & Zimmerman 2009 and Anderegg et al. 2010 are cited from memory; their pages were not reachable this pass (publisher and PMC bot-walls).
- There are no survey numbers before 1996 and no citation-stance numbers before OpenAlex's coverage thins (the 1960s–70s shifts of P01–P03 rest on markers B and C alone).
- The typology is a sorting of disputes by what settles them; a second sorting — by *who* is on each side (insiders, adjacent fields, outsiders) — would test the claim's sociological form more directly and is not attempted here.
