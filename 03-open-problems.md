# Problems without a widely accepted answer

This file answers the third question of the brief: which problems have no widely accepted answer, how the discussion of each has evolved, and what the stakes are. It draws on the per-problem files (P01–P13) and is organized from the problems with the largest stakes to those with the smallest. Each section ends with what would change the situation, so that later passes can record whether it has.

Two preliminary distinctions. First, "no widely accepted answer" is used here in the field's own sense: the mainstream literature contains competing, published positions and the assessments express the disagreement in wide ranges or low confidence. It does not mean that positions outside the mainstream exist (they exist for every problem). Second, there is a difference between problems that are open because the physics is unresolved (clouds), because the observations are insufficient (aerosols, internal variability), because the system may be nonlinear in ways models under-represent (AMOC, ice cliffs), and because they are recent (the 2023–25 surge). The sections note which.

## 1. Cloud feedback and the upper tail of climate sensitivity (P04, P05)

**The problem.** The net cloud feedback is positive but its magnitude ranges, in the assessments, from near zero to nearly +1 W m⁻² K⁻¹; that range is most of the difference between ECS ≈ 2.5 °C and ECS ≈ 4 °C, and the hot CMIP6 models sit above it.

**Evolution.** Schneider (1972) showed the sign was indeterminate; Cess et al. (1989) showed clouds explained the model spread; Bony & Dufresne (2005) localized it to subtropical low clouds; the 2009–2021 observational programme (Clement 2009; Dessler 2010; cloud-controlling factors — Klein 2017; Myers 2021; Ceppi & Nowack 2021) established the sign as positive and constrained the low-cloud term; Tan et al. (2016) and Zelinka et al. (2020) showed that mixed-phase microphysics and aerosol–cloud interactions push some models to ECS above 5 °C; Sherwood et al. (2020) and AR6 narrowed the assessed range to 2.5–4 °C by combining lines of evidence while the model range widened to 1.8–5.6 °C. The discussion has thus moved from "which clouds" (answered) to "how do we constrain a process the models cannot resolve" (not answered), and the current tension is between the assessed range and a subset of the models. Roe & Baker (2007) showed why the upper tail is hard to cut by any improvement in the feedbacks alone.

**Current positions.** The assessments (~3 °C, 2.5–4 likely); the hot-model groups (5 °C+ "plausible," Bjordal et al. 2020); Hansen et al. (2023) at 4.8 °C on paleo and aerosol grounds; Lewis (2023) at ~2.2 °C on energy-budget grounds. All accept the framework; they differ on which line of evidence to trust.

**Stakes.** Every projection, budget and damage estimate scales with this. A 4.5 °C world under the same emissions is not a 3 °C world made worse; it crosses tipping thresholds (§5) earlier and shrinks the remaining budget for any target by roughly a third.

**What would change it.** Km-scale global models that resolve convection (DYAMOND; Stevens et al. 2019) reproducing the low-cloud response; a decade more of CERES with a clear cloud-feedback signal; a settled pattern-effect correction (§3); a coherent aerosol constraint (§2).

## 2. Aerosol forcing and its decline (P06)

**The problem.** Total aerosol forcing over the industrial era is assessed at −1.1 W m⁻² with a very-likely range of −1.7 to −0.4 — a factor of four. It is entangled with clouds (the aerosol–cloud term carries most of the uncertainty) and with sensitivity (Kiehl 2007's degeneracy). It is now *declining* (Quaas et al. 2022), so its magnitude sets how much warming is being unmasked.

**Evolution.** Charlson et al. (1992) put it on the map; Mitchell et al. (1995) used it to reconcile models with the record; Kiehl (2007) showed the reconciliation was degenerate; Stevens (2015) argued for a weak lower bound and was disputed; Bellouin et al. (2020) assembled all lines; AR6 kept the wide range. Since 2020 the question has been re-posed by the ship-fuel regulation and the Chinese emission declines: is the 2023–25 surge partly unmasking (Hansen 2025: yes, largely; the IMO2020 literature: a few hundredths of a degree; Farago 2026: small)? The discussion has not converged in thirty-four years; it has changed subject from "how much cooling did we have" to "how much warming are we releasing."

**Current positions.** Strong (Hansen and co-authors, ~−1.5 or more, implying higher ECS); moderate (assessments, ~−1); weak (Stevens, ~−0.5, implying the historical record constrains ECS well).

**Stakes.** The historical line of evidence for ECS is weak because of this term; the near-term warming rate (§4) depends on how fast it is being removed; and the "termination shock" framing bears on geoengineering debates.

**What would change it.** Direct satellite constraints on cloud droplet number and cloud adjustments (EarthCARE, launched 2024); a resolved attribution of the 2023–25 low-cloud decline (Goessling 2024) to aerosols or to feedback or to variability.

## 3. The pattern effect and the tropical Pacific (P10, P04, P11)

**The problem.** The global feedback depends on the *pattern* of surface warming. The observed pattern since ~1980 — eastern equatorial Pacific and Southern Ocean warming little or cooling — is one that almost no coupled model reproduces, and it makes the historical feedback more negative than the equilibrium one. If the pattern is a forced response the models miss, the future may warm less than models say (or the models' equilibrium sensitivity is wrong in an unknown direction); if it is internal variability or a transient ocean response that will reverse, warming will accelerate as it reverses.

**Evolution.** The efficacy of ocean heat uptake (Winton et al. 2010) was the first form; Armour et al. (2013), Andrews et al. (2015) and Gregory & Andrews (2016) established the mechanism in models; Zhou et al. (2016) found it in satellite data; Proistosescu & Huybers (2017), Armour (2017), Andrews et al. (2018) and Marvel et al. (2018) used it to reconcile energy-budget ECS with model ECS, and Sherwood (2020) and AR6 adopted the correction. Meanwhile Seager et al. (2019), Wills et al. (2022) and Watanabe et al. (2021) documented that the models' historical SST pattern is systematically wrong, so that the *size* of the correction rests on a model behaviour that disagrees with observations. The discussion since 2020 has been about the cause of the eastern-Pacific trend: Southern Ocean and Antarctic meltwater, ocean thermostat dynamics, aerosols, or variability.

**Current positions.** Large correction, ECS ~3+ (most of the pattern-effect literature; the assessments); small correction, ECS ~2–2.5 (Lewis 2023); a forced eastern-Pacific cooling that models miss (Seager and co-authors), which would imply the models' *regional* projections are wrong even if their global ones are right.

**Stakes.** The interpretation of the entire instrumental record as a constraint on the future; the size of the near-term acceleration if the pattern reverses (as it partly did in 2023–24); the tropical Pacific's regional climate (ENSO, monsoons, Americas' drought).

**What would change it.** Continued observation of whether the eastern Pacific begins to warm; models that reproduce the historical pattern for physical reasons; a paleo constraint on the equilibrium pattern.

## 4. Recent acceleration and the doubling of the energy imbalance (P08, P06, P13)

**The problem.** Human-induced warming is now assessed at 0.27 °C per decade (2016–2025), up from ~0.18; the Earth's energy imbalance has roughly doubled since 2005 (Loeb 2021; Mauritsen 2025); 2023–24 ran ~0.2 °C above what ENSO and the trend predicted; planetary albedo fell to a record low (Goessling 2024). Is this an acceleration of the forced response beyond what forcing growth explains — and if so, from aerosol decline (§2), from a cloud feedback (§1), or from the pattern effect reversing (§3) — or is it variability plus several small forcings?

**Evolution.** Schmidt (2024) framed it as unexplained; the ship-sulphur papers (2024) supplied a small forced contribution; Raghuraman et al. (2024) and Samset et al. (2024) attributed most of 2023 to ENSO and steady warming; Rantanen & Laaksonen (2024) found the September 2023 jump very unlikely from variability alone; Goessling et al. (2024) implicated low cloud; Hansen et al. (2025) claimed a large forced acceleration; Beaulieu et al. (2024) found no detectable acceleration in the raw record; Forster et al. (2025, 2026) reported the rising attributed rate; Foster & Rahmstorf (2026) found significant acceleration after removing ENSO, volcanic and solar effects; Farago et al. (2026) reconstructed 2023–24 from known factors with the underlying trend at ~0.19 °C per decade. As of mid-2026 the field agrees the rate has risen and disagrees on whether the rise is statistically established, on its cause, and on whether 0.3 °C per decade is the new baseline.

**Stakes.** Whether 1.5 °C is crossed around 2030 (Foster & Rahmstorf's projection) rather than the late 2030s; whether the AMOC rate-tipping threshold (~0.3 °C per decade; van Westen et al. 2026) is already exceeded; the credibility of near-term projections, which the public treats as the test of the science.

**What would change it.** Three to five more years of data (the acceleration signal needs roughly a decade to emerge from ENSO noise — Beaulieu 2024); a closed radiative budget for the albedo decline; the 2025–26 La Niña/El Niño sequence.

## 5. AMOC collapse, ice-sheet instability, and tipping thresholds (P12)

**The problem.** Three coupled questions: whether the Atlantic overturning can collapse within this century and at what warming or warming rate; whether marine ice-cliff instability operates and thus whether Antarctic sea-level contributions of a metre or more by 2100 are possible; and where the thresholds of the other tipping elements lie.

**Evolution.** *AMOC:* bistability established in theory (Stommel 1961) and models (Manabe & Stouffer 1988; Rahmstorf 1995); rate-dependence found (Stocker & Schmittner 1997) and then largely forgotten; the assessments settled on "weakening, not collapse" (TAR through AR6); observational inference of a slowdown (Rahmstorf 2015; Caesar 2018); the stability-bias argument that models are too stable (Liu 2017; Weijer 2019); early-warning claims of collapse this century (Boers 2021; Ditlevsen 2023; van Westen 2024); the ensemble counter-evidence of no full collapse under extreme forcing (Baker 2025); and, in 2026, rate-induced tipping near 2 °C at current warming rates (van Westen, Börner & Dijkstra). The discussion has moved from "is it possible" (yes) to "is it happening," with the two sides now separated mainly by whether the models' stability can be trusted. *Ice sheets:* MISI accepted and observed under way (Joughin 2014); MICI proposed (DeConto & Pollard 2016), found unnecessary for the past (Edwards 2019), retained as a tail (AR6 storyline; Bamber 2019); the Thwaites collaboration's fieldwork (2018–) has narrowed some parameters without settling the rate question. *Tipping elements:* from a catalogue (Lenton 2008) to threshold estimates (Armstrong McKay 2022) that place several within 1.5–2 °C; the assessments remain more conservative.

**Current positions.** *AMOC:* collapse this century plausible-to-likely at current warming rates (van Westen, Ditlevsen, Rahmstorf, Lenton; the 2024 open letter) vs weakening without collapse (the CMIP ensemble; Baker; AR6's medium confidence). *Antarctica:* ~0.1–0.3 m by 2100 (process models without MICI) vs up to ~1 m or more (MICI, expert judgment tails).

**Stakes.** An AMOC collapse would cool north-western Europe by several degrees, shift tropical rain belts, and raise North Atlantic sea level; it would be effectively irreversible on human timescales. Multi-metre sea-level rise is the largest single long-term damage. These are the outcomes that dominate risk-weighted cost estimates (Weitzman 2009) and that the assessments' "low-likelihood, high-impact" language was invented for.

**What would change it.** Sustained direct AMOC observations (RAPID/OSNAP arrays now ~20 years) showing or not showing decline; models that pass the freshwater-transport stability test; Thwaites bed and grounding-line observations; an agreed treatment of early-warning statistics.

## 6. Regional precipitation and circulation change (P11)

**The problem.** The thermodynamic part of hydrological change is robust; the dynamical part — where the storm tracks, monsoons and subtropical dry zones will move, and what happens to ENSO — is not, and for regional precipitation over the next decades internal variability alone dominates the projection spread.

**Evolution.** Held & Soden (2006) gave the robust part; Hawkins & Sutton (2009, 2011) partitioned the uncertainty; Deser et al. (2012) showed the internal component with large ensembles; Shepherd (2014) argued the dynamical uncertainty is structural; the storyline approach (Shepherd 2018) is a response, not a solution; the eastern-Pacific problem (§3) sits underneath. Little has converged since 2014, and the field's own position is that regional projection is a weakly constrained problem.

**Stakes.** Adaptation planning — water supply, agriculture, flood defence — needs regional precipitation, and this is the field's weakest deliverable.

**What would change it.** Resolving §3; km-scale models with better convection–circulation coupling; longer observed records to characterize regional variability.

## 7. Arctic–midlatitude linkage; tropical-cyclone frequency; Antarctic sea ice (P11)

Three smaller problems with a common feature: a mainstream literature split along methodological lines. *Arctic–midlatitude:* observational studies find that Arctic amplification alters mid-latitude winter weather and model studies do not (Cohen et al. 2020's "divergent consensuses"; Blackport & Screen 2020); stakes are in the public narrative about cold-air outbreaks, not in projections. *Tropical-cyclone frequency:* intensification is accepted; whether the global count falls, stays, or rises is not (Knutson 2010, 2020), because models and theory disagree on genesis; stakes are in damage projections. *Antarctic sea ice:* a slight increase to 2014 that no model reproduced, then a collapse to record lows (Parkinson 2019; Purich & Doddridge 2023) that may be a regime shift; stakes are in the Southern Ocean's heat and carbon uptake and in the pattern effect (§3).

## 8. Tropical tropospheric amplification (P08, P05)

**The problem.** Models warm the tropical upper troposphere 1.5–2 times faster than the surface; the satellite and radiosonde records show less amplification over 1979–present than most models simulate.

**Evolution.** The satellite discrepancy of the 1990s was resolved (Mears & Wentz 2005) in the sense that the troposphere warms; the amplification *rate* remained (Santer 2005, 2008, 2017; McKitrick & Christy 2018, 2020). AR6 concedes with medium confidence that models overestimate it, in part because they overestimate tropical SST warming (§3). Positions: a data problem (residual satellite/radiosonde biases), a forcing problem (early-2000s volcanic and solar), an SST-pattern problem (§3), or a real deficiency in the models' moist physics.

**Stakes.** Modest for projections — the lapse-rate feedback's *existence* is not in doubt — but large for model credibility, since this is the skeptical community's most durable model–observation comparison (Christy's congressional charts).

**What would change it.** Agreement between the satellite groups' intersatellite calibrations; resolution of §3.

## 9. Carbon-cycle feedbacks and the remaining budget (P02, P10)

**The problem.** The climate–carbon feedback is positive with a wide spread (C4MIP 2006; CMIP6); the permafrost contribution, tropical forest response, and Southern Ocean uptake are each uncertain; the remaining 1.5 °C budget (~130 GtCO₂ from 2025 for 50 per cent) is already within the noise of its own uncertainty.

**Evolution.** From the missing sink (solved) to feedback sign (solved, positive) to magnitude (open since 2006, spread not narrowing); the budget accounting (Rogelj 2019; Lamboll 2023; the Indicators) has become an annual exercise whose inputs (TCRE, ZEC, non-CO₂, aerosols) each carry the uncertainties of §§1–2. The 2023 weak land sink (Ke et al. 2024) raised the question of whether the sinks are beginning to fail.

**Stakes.** The timing of net zero; whether 1.5 °C remains a budget at all or only an overshoot target.

**What would change it.** Constraints on tropical forest and permafrost responses from the 2015–16 and 2023–24 El Niños; a narrowing of TCRE; atmospheric-inversion evidence on sink trends.

## 10. The magnitude of internal variability (P13)

**The problem.** The bound on how much of the century's warming could be internal (±0.2 °C) rests on models' variability, which is not independently verifiable on multidecadal and regional scales; Laepple & Huybers (2014) found models under-represent regional low-frequency variability; the signal-to-noise paradox (Scaife & Smith 2018) suggests models' noise and signal are mis-proportioned; the AMV's nature is disputed.

**Evolution.** Hasselmann (1976) posed it; large ensembles (2012–) characterized *model* variability thoroughly; the proxy–model comparisons since 2014 opened the question of whether it is the right variability; the AMV-as-forced argument (Mann 2014–2021) reduces the space for internal explanations of the century's warming; the field treats the global-mean bound as adequate and the regional bound as not.

**Stakes.** This is the last defensible form of the "it could be natural" argument, and the field's bound on it is model-derived. For projections the stakes are in §6; for attribution they are small in practice (fingerprints and the energy budget do not depend on the global-mean noise bound alone) but large in principle.

**What would change it.** Longer, better-calibrated proxy records of regional variability; resolution of the signal-to-noise paradox; models whose variability is validated at the relevant scales.

## 11. Paleoclimate puzzles: the 100-kyr cycle, the Holocene conundrum, state dependence (P09, P04)

Three problems with high scientific and low policy stakes. *The 100-kyr cycle:* orbitally paced but mechanism contested (ice-sheet hysteresis with CO₂ — Abe-Ouchi 2013 — vs obliquity pacing — Huybers 2011). *The Holocene conundrum:* proxies show cooling after ~6 kyr ago while models show warming (Liu 2014); seasonal proxy bias (Bova 2021) is the leading resolution; stakes are in the use of the Holocene as a baseline for "unprecedented." *State dependence:* sensitivity appears higher in warm climates (Caballero & Huber 2013; Friedrich 2016; Zhu 2019), which weakens the LGM as a constraint on a warmer future and strengthens the case for a positive cloud-feedback dependence on temperature (§1); stakes are in whether ~3 °C is the right number for a 2–4 °C warmer world.

## Cross-cutting observations

- **Four of the eleven open problems are one problem.** Clouds (§1), aerosols (§2), the pattern effect (§3) and recent acceleration (§4) are entangled: low clouds respond to aerosols, to SST pattern, and to warming, and the 2023–25 albedo decline could be any of the three. Progress on one will move the others. The field's expression of this is the WCRP Grand Challenge on clouds, circulation and sensitivity (Bony et al. 2015).
- **The open problems are not where the public controversy is.** The public disputes of 1990–2017 concerned the record, the millennium reconstruction, the satellite data and the pause — all now closed (P08, P09). The field's own open problems (§§1–5) are about magnitude and nonlinearity, and the most consequential of them (§5) point toward larger rather than smaller risks.
- **The assessments have been conservative on the nonlinear problems and central on the linear ones.** AR4's sea-level exclusion, AR6's medium confidence against AMOC collapse, and the tipping literature's complaint that the assessments lag it are the pattern; on sensitivity and attribution the assessments have tracked the literature closely. This asymmetry is worth documenting in the project's later strand.
- **Every open problem is limited by the same two things:** sub-grid physics (clouds, convection, ice-cliff failure, permafrost hydrology) and the shortness of the record relative to the noise. Neither is peculiar to climate science; both are why the field converges slowly on magnitudes and quickly on signs.
